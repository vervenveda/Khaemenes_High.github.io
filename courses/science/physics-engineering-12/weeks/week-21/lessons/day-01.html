Angular Momentum & Moment of Inertia

Why can rotational speed change when mass distribution changes?

Weekly EvidencePhysics CourseScience Hall

Explicit physics instruction

Angular momentum for a rigid body can be modeled as L=Iω. Moment of inertia I depends on how mass is distributed relative to the axis.

When net external torque is negligible, total angular momentum is conserved. If I decreases, ω must increase so the product remains constant.

The analogy with linear momentum is useful but not identical: torque changes angular momentum just as external force/impulse changes linear momentum.

Core relation: L=Iω and ΔL=τ_extΔt.

Worked example

A rotating system has I=4 kg·m² and ω=2 rad/s, so L=8 kg·m²/s. If I decreases to 2 kg·m² with negligible external torque, ω rises to 4 rad/s.

Guided question

Why does moving mass farther from the axis generally increase moment of inertia?

Independent work

Misconception check

Conserved angular momentum does not mean angular speed is constant; I can change.

Formative check

If I is halved with L conserved, what happens to ω?

Reveal response

It doubles.

Vocabulary and angular-momentum depth extension

Vocabulary: angular momentum; moment of inertia; external torque; angular impulse; system boundary.

Moment of inertia is the rotational analogue of inertia, but it depends on both total mass and how that mass is distributed relative to the axis. Moving the same mass farther outward increases I because distance from the axis matters strongly. For point masses, I=Σmr²; for extended bodies, geometry determines the appropriate expression.

Angular momentum L=Iω therefore depends on both mass distribution and angular speed. If net external torque is negligible, total L is conserved even while I and ω change. Conservation must be tied to a defined system and interval, just as linear momentum conservation depends on negligible external impulse.

Evidence revision / CER

Defend why reducing I can increase ω without creating angular momentum. Use one before/after calculation and state the external-torque assumption. Revise any explanation that treats constant L as constant ω.

Spiral retrieval and safety

Recall Week 15 momentum conservation and Week 20 torque. Use simulations or supplied data only; no rapid spinning of people or heavy apparatus is needed.

Worked transfer practice

Consider two 1 kg point masses rotating at radius 0.50 m with angular speed 4 rad/s. The moment of inertia is I=Σmr²=2(1)(0.50²)=0.50 kg·m², so L=2.0 kg·m²/s. Move both masses inward to 0.25 m with negligible external torque. The new inertia is 2(1)(0.25²)=0.125 kg·m². Conservation requires ω=2.0/0.125=16 rad/s.

This example shows why radius strongly affects rotational inertia: halving radius reduces each mr² term by a factor of four. The resulting angular-speed increase follows from the conserved product Iω, not from an added torque.

Mass-distribution transfer check

Compare two systems with equal total mass and equal angular speed but different radii. Because I depends on r², the system with mass farther from the axis has larger angular momentum at the same ω. This gives a direct test of whether students understand that rotational inertia depends on distribution, not only mass.

Then reverse the comparison: require both systems to have the same L. The larger-I system must rotate more slowly. Explain why this does not violate conservation and why no energy conclusion follows from L alone without additional information.

Phenomenon / Prediction

A spinning system changes rotation rate when its mass distribution changes.
