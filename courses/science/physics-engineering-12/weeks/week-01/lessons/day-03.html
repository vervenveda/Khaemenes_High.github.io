Slope, Graphs & Experimental Uncertainty

Interpret slope and spread as quantitative evidence while distinguishing random variation from systematic bias.

Weekly EvidencePhysics CourseScience HallArchaemenesPrevious DayNext Day

Explicit physics instruction

Graphs compress evidence into patterns. Slope represents a rate of change and must carry units derived from the axes.

Repeated measurements reveal spread and support estimates of random uncertainty. A tight cluster can be precise while still shifted from the true value by systematic bias.

Residuals and uncertainty should remain visible. Deleting inconvenient measurements without documented justification weakens reproducibility.

Vocabulary: slope; residual; random uncertainty; systematic bias; precision.

Worked example with reasoning

A position graph changes from 2 m at 1 s to 14 m at 5 s. Slope=(14−2)/(5−1)=3 m/s. The slope unit comes from metres divided by seconds.

Guided question

Four measurements are 9.8, 9.9, 9.8, and 9.9 while a trusted value is 10.4. What do the data suggest about precision and accuracy?

Independent work

  1. Calculate slopes from three two-point datasets.
  2. Identify one random and one systematic uncertainty source.
  3. Explain why a precise dataset can still be inaccurate.

Misconception check

A straight-looking graph does not remove uncertainty or prove causation.

Formative understanding check

What does the unit of slope come from?

Reveal response

The vertical-axis unit divided by the horizontal-axis unit.

Mentor evidence conference

Ask Archaemenes to audit units, assumptions, coordinate choices, and evidence limits.

Teacher guidance

Foundation: Use a slope triangle and uncertainty vocabulary.

Core: Calculate/interpret slopes and distinguish random from systematic effects.

Extended: Use residual patterns to compare two candidate models.

Graph-model validation extension

Before calculating slope, identify which variable is on each axis, the units, and whether a linear model is justified. A two-point slope can describe an interval, but multiple points are needed to test whether the relationship is actually linear. Residuals reveal where a candidate model systematically misses the evidence.

Separate random scatter from systematic bias. Random variation widens repeated measurements in both directions, while a calibration offset can shift every result similarly. Averaging many trials can reduce random scatter but does not automatically remove a systematic bias. A precise cluster can therefore remain inaccurate.

When comparing two models, do not choose the one with the smallest visual error alone. Check residual structure, uncertainty, units, and the tested range. A model that fits one narrow interval may fail outside it. State whether the evidence supports the model strongly, weakly, or only within a limited domain.

Retrieval and model-selection defense

Retrieval: from memory, state what slope means, how its units are constructed, and how random scatter differs from systematic bias. Then predict what a curved residual pattern suggests about a straight-line model.

Evidence revision: compare a linear and nonlinear candidate model using the same dataset. A stronger model should reduce meaningful residual structure without adding unjustified complexity. Write a CER naming the selected model, the graph or residual evidence supporting it, one uncertainty that weakens the conclusion, and the range over which the model is defensible.
