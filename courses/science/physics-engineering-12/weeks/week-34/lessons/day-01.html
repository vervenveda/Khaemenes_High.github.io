Orbits & Remote Observation

Explain how orbital motion enables repeated Earth observation and measurement from a distance.

Weekly EvidencePhysics CourseScience Hall

Explicit physics instruction

A satellite in orbit is continuously falling under gravity while moving tangentially fast enough to miss Earth.

Remote sensing measures electromagnetic radiation reflected or emitted by a target; it does not require direct physical contact.

Orbit choice affects revisit time, coverage, resolution, and communication geometry.

Vocabulary: orbit; remote sensing; revisit time; resolution; electromagnetic signal.

Worked example

A satellite that revisits the same region frequently may trade broad coverage for temporal detail, depending on its orbit.

Guided question

Why is a satellite in orbit still accelerating even if its speed magnitude is nearly constant?

Independent work

  1. Sketch velocity and acceleration vectors for circular orbit.
  2. Compare two orbit choices qualitatively.
  3. Name two remote-sensing measurements that rely on EM radiation.

Misconception check

Orbiting does not mean gravity is absent; gravity supplies centripetal acceleration.

Formative check

What direction is orbital acceleration in a circular model?

Reveal response

Toward the center of Earth.

Orbital-model depth

For a near-circular orbit, gravity supplies the inward acceleration. The velocity vector is tangent to the path while acceleration points toward the center. Constant speed therefore does not mean zero acceleration because velocity changes direction continuously. The circular model is useful, but real missions include eccentricity, atmospheric drag at low altitude, orbital perturbations, and changing viewing geometry.

Remote sensing adds an engineering layer. Lower orbits can support finer spatial detail for a given sensor while seeing a smaller region at one time; mission geometry also affects revisit time, ground-track coverage, communication windows, and power requirements. No single orbit maximizes every mission goal.

Quantitative transfer

Use the circular-orbit relation v²/r = GM/r² to show that orbital speed scales as √(GM/r). Check dimensions before interpreting the result. Then explain qualitatively why a higher circular orbit has a lower orbital speed and a longer orbital period.

Evidence revision / CER

Claim which orbit characteristic matters most for a stated observation goal. Support it with orbital and sensing evidence, then revise the claim after considering one competing priority such as resolution, revisit frequency, coverage, bandwidth, or mission lifetime.

Uncertainty and inference

Separate orbital position uncertainty, sensor resolution, and interpretive uncertainty. A precisely known satellite position does not guarantee a unique surface interpretation, and a high-resolution image does not eliminate calibration or atmospheric effects.

Spiral retrieval

Retrieve inverse-square gravity from Week 09 and centripetal acceleration from the mechanics sequence. Explain how those models combine in a circular-orbit approximation and identify one assumption that limits the approximation.

Mission-validation check

Compare the chosen orbit with one alternative using the same mission criterion. A defensible recommendation should show not only why one orbit works, but why the alternative performs worse under the stated priorities and assumptions.

Phenomenon / Prediction

Satellites infer weather, surface, and atmospheric properties from electromagnetic signals.

Investigation / model check

Satellite/remote-sensing dataset analysis; Solanar extension. Preserve observations or supplied data, compare them with the model, and record one limitation or source of uncertainty.
