Integrated Mechanics Problem Solving

Combine kinematics, dynamics, energy, and momentum across a multi-stage system.

Weekly EvidencePhysics CourseScience Hall

Explicit physics instruction

Real systems often require different models at different stages. Break the event into intervals and define the relevant system for each.

Carry units, signs, and intermediate quantities consistently. A result from one stage can become initial evidence for the next.

Cross-check with conservation or dimensional reasoning whenever possible.

Vocabulary: multi-stage model; state variable; boundary; cross-check; dimensional analysis.

Worked example with reasoning

A block slides down a frictionless ramp, gaining speed by energy conservation, then sticks to a cart in a collision solved with momentum conservation. Energy is appropriate before impact; momentum is appropriate during the short collision.

Guided question

Identify the natural stage boundaries in a ramp-plus-collision problem and state the best model for each.

Independent work

  1. Solve one two-stage mechanics problem.
  2. Label each stage with its system and model.
  3. Perform at least one dimensional or conservation check.

Misconception check

One conservation law need not apply unchanged across every stage or system boundary.

Formative understanding check

Why can energy be useful before a collision while momentum is more useful during it?

Reveal response

Different interactions and losses make different conserved quantities or models most efficient in each stage.

Multi-stage transfer extension

For a multi-stage event, define a separate system and model for each interval. Example: a block descends a nearly frictionless ramp, collides and sticks to a cart, then the combined cart slides under friction. Energy is efficient for the ramp stage, momentum for the brief collision, and Newton's second law or work-energy for the friction stage.

Carry outputs forward carefully. The collision model needs the pre-impact velocity produced by the ramp model. Any uncertainty or error in the first stage becomes an input uncertainty for the next. This is why traceable intermediate values matter.

Use dimensional and conservation checks at every transition. A speed handed from one stage must have velocity units; a collision result must preserve signed system momentum if external impulse is negligible; a friction stage should account for negative work or a net opposing force.

Evidence revision / CER

Write a CER defending the chosen stage boundaries and model transitions. Revise any approach that uses one conservation law unchanged across an interaction where its assumptions fail.

Spiral retrieval

Recall Weeks 10, 15, and 16 and map one quantity from each as an input to the next stage.

Worked three-stage transfer

A 2 kg block descends from 1.25 m on a frictionless track. Energy gives v≈√(2gh)=4.95 m/s before impact. It sticks to a stationary 3 kg cart, so momentum gives v_f=(2·4.95)/(5)=1.98 m/s. If a later friction force of 4.0 N opposes the 5 kg combined cart, acceleration is −0.80 m/s². Each stage uses a different model because the governing interaction changes.

Audit the handoff between stages: the pre-impact speed becomes the collision input, and the post-collision speed becomes the friction-stage initial condition. A mistake in Stage 1 propagates forward, so preserve intermediate values and uncertainty rather than rounding aggressively at each step.
