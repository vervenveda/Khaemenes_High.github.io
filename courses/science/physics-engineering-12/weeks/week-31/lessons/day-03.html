Generators, Motors & Energy Conversion

Connect induction and magnetic force to energy conversion in generators and motors.

Weekly EvidencePhysics CourseScience Hall

Safety boundary

Use simulations or supervised low-voltage coil/magnet activities only. Do not use mains electricity, high-current coils, or improvised high-voltage equipment.

Explicit physics instruction

A generator converts mechanical energy into electrical energy through changing magnetic flux.

A motor converts electrical energy into mechanical rotation through magnetic forces on current-carrying conductors.

Neither device creates energy; efficiency is limited by heating, friction, magnetic losses, and other mechanisms.

Vocabulary: generator; motor; energy conversion; efficiency; back emf.

Worked example

A hand-cranked generator can light an LED because mechanical work changes magnetic flux and produces electrical energy transfer.

Energy-conversion depth extension

Generators and motors are energy-conversion devices. In a generator, mechanical work changes magnetic flux and produces electrical energy transfer. In a motor, electrical input drives current that experiences magnetic force and produces mechanical output.

Back emf is part of the motor/generator energy story: as motion increases, induced effects can oppose the applied electrical drive. Real devices also lose energy through resistance, friction, and magnetic effects.

Evidence revision / CER

Defend why increasing electrical load can make a generator harder to turn. Use energy conservation and induction rather than saying the generator “creates resistance.” Revise any energy-flow diagram that omits the mechanical input.

Generator/motor efficiency transfer

A hand-cranked generator receives 20 J of mechanical work and delivers 14 J electrically to a load. The simple efficiency is 70%, with the remaining energy transferred through heating, friction, sound, or magnetic losses. If measurements carry uncertainty, report an efficiency range rather than a false exact percentage.

For a motor, compare electrical input power P=VI with useful mechanical output power. The difference represents losses within the stated system boundary, not destroyed energy.

Use this to explain why generator loading can increase the required input torque.

Power-conversion transfer check

If a motor draws 2.0 A from a 6.0 V source, electrical input power is 12 W. If measured mechanical output is 8 W, efficiency is about 67%. The remaining 4 W must be accounted for through heating, friction, sound, or other losses within the chosen boundary.

Back-emf transfer check

Explain why a spinning motor can draw less current than the same motor when stalled in a simple model: motion produces back emf that reduces net driving voltage. State why a stalled motor can overheat even though it is not rotating.

Final energy-conversion check

Compare two motor trials with the same electrical input but different mechanical output. Use the difference in efficiency to identify whether added friction, resistance heating, or load conditions changed the energy pathways.
