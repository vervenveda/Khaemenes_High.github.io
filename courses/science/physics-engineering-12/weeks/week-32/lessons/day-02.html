Reflection, Refraction & Imaging

Use ray and wave models to explain reflection, refraction, and image formation.

Safety boundary

Explicit physics instruction

Reflection follows equal incident and reflected angles measured from the normal in the ideal ray model.

Refraction occurs because wave speed changes between media, changing wavelength and often direction.

Ray diagrams are geometric models that approximate wave behavior when dimensions are large compared with wavelength.

Vocabulary: reflection; refraction; normal; refractive index; ray model.

Worked example

A ray striking a mirror at 30° from the normal reflects at 30° on the other side of the normal.

Optics-model depth extension

Reflection in the ideal ray model obeys equal incident and reflected angles measured from the normal. Refraction occurs because wave speed changes between media while source frequency remains fixed, so wavelength changes as well.

Snell-style reasoning can be represented by n₁sinθ₁=n₂sinθ₂ in the geometric-optics model. Ray models work best when system dimensions are large relative to wavelength and diffraction is limited.

Evidence revision / CER

Defend one reflected and one refracted ray diagram. Revise any diagram that measures angles from the surface or changes frequency at the boundary without a source change.

Uncertainty and retrieval

Angle measurement uncertainty affects inferred refractive behavior. Recall Week 01 measurement discipline and Week 24 boundary effects.

Refraction transfer calculation

For light entering a higher-index medium, Snell's law predicts bending toward the normal when n₂>n₁ for ordinary positive-index materials. If n₁=1.00, θ₁=30°, and n₂=1.50, then sinθ₂≈0.333, so θ₂≈19.5°.

Frequency remains set by the source while wave speed and wavelength change in the material. Angle uncertainty propagates into any inferred refractive index, especially near steep geometries.

Ray-wave model check

Compare a large optical element with one whose feature size approaches the wavelength of light. The first is well suited to ray tracing; the second may require diffraction/wave reasoning. State why a successful ray diagram does not prove wave effects are absent.

Refraction evidence check

Compare measured refraction angles at two incidence angles and test whether one refractive-index value explains both within uncertainty. A single successful ray diagram is weaker evidence than repeated angle pairs consistent with one model.

Final optics check

Repeat one refraction calculation at a second incidence angle and test whether the same refractive index explains both. Consistency across angles is stronger evidence than a single successful ray diagram.

Optics uncertainty transfer

Suppose the incident angle is 30.0±0.5° and the refracted angle is 19.5±0.5°. Use those ranges to estimate whether the inferred refractive index is stable enough to support one material model. If small angle changes produce a large index spread, the conclusion should be reported with that limitation.
