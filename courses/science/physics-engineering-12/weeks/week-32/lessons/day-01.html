Electromagnetic Spectrum & Wave Model

Connect wavelength, frequency, and energy across the electromagnetic spectrum.

Weekly EvidencePhysics CourseScience Hall

Safety boundary

Use simulations, printed optics, and eye-safe light only. Do not direct lasers or intense light at people.

Explicit physics instruction

Electromagnetic waves can travel through vacuum and obey v=fλ, with v≈3.00×10⁸ m/s in vacuum.

Frequency and wavelength are inversely related at fixed wave speed. Different regions of the spectrum are classified primarily by frequency/wavelength, not by different physical laws.

Photon energy increases with frequency; wavelength alone does not determine signal quality or hazard without considering intensity/exposure.

Vocabulary: EM spectrum; wavelength; frequency; photon; vacuum.

Worked example

A 100 MHz radio wave has λ=(3.00×10⁸)/(1.00×10⁸)=3.0 m.

Guided question

A wave has frequency 6.0×10¹⁴ Hz. Estimate wavelength in vacuum.

Independent work

  1. Solve three v=fλ problems.
  2. Order spectrum regions by frequency.
  3. State one limitation of using wavelength alone to judge risk.

Misconception check

All electromagnetic waves travel at the same speed only in vacuum, not necessarily in materials.

Formative check

What happens to wavelength as frequency increases in vacuum?

Reveal response

It decreases.

EM-spectrum depth extension

In vacuum, all electromagnetic waves travel at c≈3.00×10⁸ m/s and obey c=fλ. Frequency and wavelength are inversely related. A 100 MHz wave has λ=3.0 m, while visible-light frequencies correspond to much shorter wavelengths.

Photon energy increases with frequency, but practical hazard also depends on intensity, exposure, and interaction with matter. Wavelength alone is not a complete risk measure.

Evidence revision / CER

Use one radio and one visible/UV comparison to defend the inverse f–λ relationship and distinguish wave classification from exposure risk. Revise any claim that all shorter wavelengths are automatically more dangerous in every context.

Spiral retrieval

Connect Week 24 v=fλ with the vacuum-speed special case.

EM-spectrum transfer calculation

A 2.4 GHz microwave signal in vacuum has λ≈0.125 m. A visible-light frequency near 6.0×10¹⁴ Hz has wavelength about 5.0×10⁻⁷ m. Both obey the same wave relation despite belonging to different spectrum regions.

Compare photon-energy trends qualitatively: higher frequency means higher photon energy, but practical exposure risk still depends on intensity, duration, and interaction mechanisms.

Spectrum ratio check

If frequency increases by a factor of 100 in vacuum, wavelength decreases by a factor of 100. Use this ratio before numerical substitution, then verify with c=fλ. State why frequency, not color name or device label, is the more fundamental classification variable.

Photon-energy transfer check

Compare two EM frequencies differing by a factor of ten. Photon energy changes by the same factor because E=hf, while wavelength changes inversely by ten in vacuum. Use this to separate photon-energy scaling from intensity, which depends on how many photons/energy arrive per area per time.

Final spectrum check

Compare a radio wave and a visible-light wave carrying the same total power. Their frequencies and photon energies differ enormously, but equal total power does not imply equal photon count. Explain why frequency and intensity answer different questions.
