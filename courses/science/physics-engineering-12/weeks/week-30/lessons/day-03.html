Electrical Power & Energy

Calculate electrical power and connect it with energy transfer.

Weekly EvidencePhysics CourseScience Hall

Safety boundary

Use only low-voltage classroom batteries/components under supervision or an approved circuit simulation. Never use mains voltage.

Explicit physics instruction

Electrical power is the rate of energy transfer. For circuit elements, P=VI and can combine with Ohm's law into P=I²R or P=V²/R when the ohmic model applies.

Energy transferred over time is E=Pt.

Power ratings describe transfer rate, not total energy by themselves.

Core relation/model: P=VI; E=Pt

Worked example

A 6 V device drawing 2 A uses 12 W. In 30 s it transfers 360 J.

Guided question

A 9 V component draws 0.5 A for 20 s. Find power and energy.

Independent work

  1. Solve three power problems.
  2. Convert one power-time result to energy.
  3. Compare two devices at equal voltage.

Misconception check

A watt is a rate unit, not an energy unit.

Formative check

What extra quantity is needed to convert power into energy?

Vocabulary and power-depth extension

Vocabulary: electrical power; energy transfer; joule; watt; component rating.

P=VI gives energy-transfer rate. With an ohmic element, P=I²R or V²/R can be derived by substitution, but these forms inherit the assumptions of the V–I model used.

A 9 V component drawing 0.5 A uses 4.5 W. Over 20 s it transfers 90 J. If current doubles through the same fixed resistance, I²R power quadruples.

Evidence revision / CER

Defend why power and energy must remain distinct. Revise any design that checks current but ignores component power rating.

Uncertainty and retrieval

Measured V and I uncertainty propagate into P. Recall Week 12 power vs. energy.

Power-transfer practice

A 6 Ω resistor across 12 V draws 2 A and dissipates 24 W. Two identical 6 Ω resistors in series across the same source draw 1 A, so each resistor dissipates 6 W and the pair 12 W total. Circuit arrangement changes both current and power demand.

Over 60 s, the single-resistor case transfers 1440 J while the series pair transfers 720 J. Distinguish instantaneous rate from accumulated energy.

Power-rating transfer check

A 10 Ω resistor carrying 1 A dissipates 10 W. The same resistor at 2 A would dissipate 40 W. This square dependence explains why modest current increases can create large heating changes.

Compare the calculated power with the component rating before declaring a design acceptable.

Final power check

Compare two resistors receiving the same voltage but different resistances. Use P=V²/R to explain which dissipates more power and why this does not contradict Ohm's law.

Energy-runtime transfer

A 12 W device operating for 5 minutes transfers 3600 J. Compare that with a 6 W device operating for 10 minutes: the total energy is the same even though the powers differ. Use this to separate transfer rate from cumulative energy.
