Equilibrium, Period & Frequency

What causes repeated motion around equilibrium?

Weekly EvidencePhysics CourseScience Hall

Explicit physics instruction

An oscillator repeatedly moves around an equilibrium position because a restoring influence points back toward equilibrium.

Simple harmonic motion is an ideal model where restoring force is proportional to displacement and opposite in direction. Real systems can include damping and nonlinear behavior.

Period T is time per cycle; frequency f is cycles per second, with f=1/T. Amplitude sets the maximum displacement from equilibrium.

Core relation: f=1/T; for ideal SHM, restoring force ∝ −displacement.

Worked example

An oscillator completes one cycle every 0.50 s, so f=1/0.50=2.0 Hz. Ten cycles would take about 5.0 s if the period remains constant.

Guided question

An oscillator makes 15 cycles in 6 s. Find frequency and period.

Greater amplitude does not automatically mean greater frequency in the ideal small-amplitude SHM model.

Formative check

Reveal response

Vocabulary and SHM model extension

Vocabulary: equilibrium; amplitude; period; frequency; restoring force.

Simple harmonic motion is a special oscillation model in which the restoring force is proportional to displacement and opposite in direction, F=-kx for an ideal spring. The negative sign indicates direction toward equilibrium. Not every repeating motion is SHM; damping, nonlinear stiffness, or large pendulum angles can break the ideal relationship.

Period T is the time for one cycle and frequency f=1/T is cycles per second. If 15 cycles occur in 6.0 s, f=2.5 Hz and T=0.40 s. Timing many cycles reduces reaction-time error per cycle compared with timing one.

Evidence revision / CER

Defend whether a repeating motion should be treated as ideal SHM. Use restoring-force behavior, repeatability, and one model limitation as evidence. Revise any claim that assumes all periodic motion is harmonic.

Spiral retrieval and safety

Recall Week 11 spring energy and Week 03 period-like graph repetition. Use simulation or a small approved oscillator only; avoid loaded springs or unsafe swinging masses.

Worked SHM transfer practice

A spring oscillator with mass m=0.50 kg and spring constant k=8.0 N/m has ideal period T=2π√(m/k). Substitution gives T≈1.57 s and f≈0.64 Hz. This model predicts that increasing mass lengthens the period while increasing stiffness shortens it.

Compare with amplitude. In ideal linear SHM, changing amplitude does not change T. If measured period increases with amplitude, that trend is evidence that damping, nonlinear stiffness, or measurement method may be affecting the system.

For pendulum-style motion, the small-angle period model T≈2π√(L/g) has different parameters and assumptions. Do not transfer spring formulas to pendulums simply because both oscillate.

Model-sensitivity check

Compare two spring oscillators with the same k but masses 0.50 kg and 2.0 kg. Because T∝√m, quadrupling mass doubles period. Then compare two oscillators with the same mass but k values differing by a factor of four; quadrupling k halves period. Use these ratios to predict changes before calculating exact values.

If a real oscillator fails these trends, identify whether damping, nonlinear stiffness, mass estimation, or timing uncertainty is the more plausible explanation.
