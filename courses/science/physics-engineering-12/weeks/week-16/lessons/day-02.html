Momentum and Kinetic-Energy Calculations

Compare momentum and kinetic energy before and after a collision.

Weekly EvidencePhysics CourseScience Hall

Explicit physics instruction

Momentum p=mv depends linearly on speed, while kinetic energy K=½mv² depends on speed squared. A change can therefore affect the two totals differently.

Always calculate system totals with signed velocities for momentum and squared speeds for kinetic energy.

Use the same defined system and reference frame before and after the collision.

Vocabulary: momentum; kinetic energy; signed velocity; system total; reference frame.

Worked example with reasoning

A 2 kg cart at 4 m/s sticks to an identical stationary cart. Momentum gives final speed 2 m/s. Initial KE=16 J; final KE=8 J, so half the kinetic energy remains.

Guided question

Repeat the calculation for a 1 kg cart at 6 m/s sticking to an identical cart.

Independent work

  1. Solve three before/after collision energy audits.

Misconception check

Do not use signed velocity inside v² to create negative kinetic energy.

Formative understanding check

Why can kinetic energy never be negative in this classical model?

Reveal response

Mass is positive and speed is squared.

Momentum–energy comparison extension

Momentum and kinetic energy respond differently because p depends on v while K depends on v². Keep velocity signed in momentum equations but use speed squared for kinetic energy. This is why reversal changes momentum sign without making kinetic energy negative.

Calculate percent kinetic-energy change only after verifying the system momentum account. For an approximately isolated inelastic collision, the decrease in mechanical kinetic energy represents energy transferred into deformation, thermal, sound, or other internal forms rather than destroyed energy.

When measurements are uncertain, kinetic-energy uncertainty can be especially sensitive to velocity because of the square. Near an elasticity threshold, report whether uncertainty could change the classification.

Percent-energy transfer extension

For the 2 kg cart at +4 m/s sticking to an identical stationary cart, initial kinetic energy is 16 J and final is 8 J. The kinetic-energy decrease is 8 J, or 50% of the initial value. Momentum, however, remains 8 kg·m/s before and after.

Now compare a less severe inelastic case in which the carts separate with velocities that still conserve momentum but retain 14 J total kinetic energy. The percent kinetic-energy decrease is then only 12.5%. This shows that “inelastic” covers a range of energy transfers; only the perfectly inelastic case requires sticking.

Reference-frame transfer check

Repeat one momentum and kinetic-energy calculation in a different inertial reference frame. The individual values can change, but the collision classification should remain physically consistent when the before/after analysis is performed correctly within the same frame.

Phenomenon / Prediction

Objects can stick together or rebound after collision.
