Buoyancy Investigation

Why can a heavy ship float while a small dense object sinks?

Weekly EvidencePhysics CourseScience Hall

Explicit physics instruction

This investigation tests density and displacement predictions.

Use water displacement with small safe objects or a simulation. Compare mass, displaced volume, density, and floating behavior.

Preserve raw mass/volume evidence before calculating density or buoyant force.

Core relations: ρ=m/V; F_b=weight of displaced fluid.

Worked example

If measured mass is 150 g and displaced volume is 200 cm³, average density is 0.75 g/cm³, consistent with floating in water.

Investigation / Data Lab

Use water displacement with small safe objects or a simulation. Compare mass, displaced volume, density, and floating behavior.

Preserve raw mass/volume data, calculated density, displacement, uncertainty, and revision.

Misconception check

A heavier object can float if its average density is low enough and it displaces sufficient fluid.

Formative check

What sets buoyant-force magnitude in Archimedes’ model?

Reveal response

The weight of the displaced fluid.

Mentor evidence conference

Ask Archaemenes to audit density units, displacement reasoning, and force balance.

Teacher guidance

Foundation: Calculate density and identify buoyant direction.

Core: Apply Archimedes’ principle and equilibrium.

Extended: Analyze stability and uncertainty.

Vocabulary and investigation-depth extension

Vocabulary: displaced volume; density residual; measurement uncertainty; repeated trial; calibration.

Preserve raw mass and displaced-volume measurements before calculating density. For small volumes, meniscus reading, trapped air, water adhesion, or scale resolution can dominate uncertainty.

Compare predicted floating behavior from average density with observed or simulated behavior. If density is near the fluid density, uncertainty may make the sink/float prediction ambiguous and should be reported honestly.

Evidence revision / CER

Write a CER deciding whether the measurements support the density-based prediction. Revise the method if displacement readings are inconsistent across trials.

Safety boundary

Use small lightweight objects or simulation. Avoid large water containers, electrical devices near water, or unsafe submerged loads.

Repeated-trial and retrieval extension

Measure displaced volume more than once. If three trials give 198, 201, and 200 cm³, the spread is small; if they give 180, 205, and 222 cm³, the method is not repeatable enough for a confident density claim.

Spiral retrieval: connect Week 20 stability, Week 07 force balance, and Week 05 residual analysis to today's buoyancy investigation.

Preserve the raw readings, not only the averaged density. A repeated systematic bias—such as trapped air or a consistently misread meniscus—will not disappear through averaging.

Measurement-quality check

Convert a measured mass and displaced volume into SI units before computing density, then repeat using the original g/cm³ form as a cross-check. Agreement between unit systems helps catch conversion errors.

State whether meniscus reading, balance resolution, or trapped air contributes the largest uncertainty for the chosen trial.

Calibration note

Check the balance with a known mass and the volume scale with a known container before trusting the experiment. Calibration evidence should be preserved alongside the buoyancy data rather than treated as an invisible assumption.

Method transfer note

Identify one measurement revision that would most improve confidence in the displaced-volume result.

Final note

Recheck calibration.
