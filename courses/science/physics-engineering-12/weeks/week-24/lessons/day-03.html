Superposition, Reflection & Standing Waves

How do disturbances carry energy without transporting matter over long distances?

Explicit physics instruction

When waves overlap, their displacements add. Constructive interference increases resultant amplitude; destructive interference reduces it.

Reflection can invert a pulse depending on the boundary condition.

Standing waves arise from interference between oppositely traveling waves and contain nodes and antinodes.

Vocabulary and superposition extension

Vocabulary: superposition; constructive interference; destructive interference; node; antinode.

When waves overlap, displacements add algebraically. Equal upward pulses can produce twice the displacement during overlap; equal opposite pulses can cancel momentarily. After ideal superposition, the original traveling waves continue.

Standing waves form from oppositely traveling waves of the same frequency under suitable boundary conditions. Nodes have persistent zero displacement; antinodes have maximum oscillation amplitude. Reflection at fixed versus free boundaries changes phase behavior.

Interference and standing-wave practice

Two pulses of +3 cm and +2 cm overlap at one location, giving +5 cm resultant displacement at that instant. A +3 cm and −3 cm pair gives zero displacement during perfect overlap, but the individual pulses continue afterward in the ideal linear model.

For a string fixed at both ends, standing-wave nodes occur at the boundaries. The simplest mode has one antinode between them and wavelength twice the string length. Higher modes contain additional nodes and shorter wavelengths.

Destructive interference does not imply permanent energy destruction; energy remains in the wave system and redistributes through the superposed fields and subsequent motion.
