Matter & Energy Foundations

Build a particle-level foundation for the official course. Track matter without making it disappear, distinguish physical from chemical change, follow energy transfers across system boundaries, and use models without confusing the model with reality.

1 instructional weekMatter & Energy80% mastery

Learning objectives

  • Distinguish atoms, elements, molecules, compounds, and mixtures at an introductory level
  • Use particle models to explain solids, liquids, gases, and changes of state
  • Distinguish physical changes from chemical changes using evidence
  • Apply conservation of matter to closed-system reasoning
  • Distinguish temperature from thermal energy
  • Identify energy transfers by heating, mechanical work, radiation, and electrical processes at an introductory level
  • Define a system boundary and track matter and energy crossing it
  • Recognize assumptions and limitations in particle and energy models
Safety

This bridge uses supplied scenarios and models. Do not mix household chemicals, heat sealed containers, taste materials, or perform combustion experiments.

1 · Matter is modeled as particles

An atom is a basic unit of an element. Atoms can bond into molecules. A compound contains atoms of more than one element chemically combined in defined relationships. A mixture contains substances together without requiring them to be chemically bonded to one another.

Model classification

Draw simple particle diagrams for: a pure element made of single atoms, a molecular element, a compound, and a mixture. Create a legend so another scientist can interpret your symbols.

2 · State is not identity

In a simplified particle model, particles in solids remain closely associated while vibrating; liquid particles remain close but rearrange; gas particles are much farther apart and move throughout the available volume. A change of state does not by itself create a new chemical substance.

Cross-scale explanation

Explain melting ice at the observable scale and particle-model scale. Which features change? Which chemical identity remains the same?

3 · Physical change versus chemical change

A physical change changes form, state, size, or arrangement without necessarily changing chemical identity. A chemical change involves rearrangement of atoms into different substances. Evidence such as gas production, temperature change, color change, precipitate formation, or light may suggest chemical change, but context matters; no single sign is infallible.

Evidence audit

Classify and justify: ice melting, paper being cut, iron rusting, sugar dissolving in water, and a candle burning. Identify where a process may include more than one kind of change.

4 · Conservation: account for the atoms

In ordinary chemical and physical changes, matter is conserved. Atoms are rearranged, transported, or change state; they are not casually created or destroyed in the model used at this level.

Conservation reasoning

The values differ by 0.1 g. Give at least three scientifically reasonable explanations before concluding matter vanished. Include measurement uncertainty and system-boundary considerations.

5 · Open and closed systems

A system boundary defines what you are analyzing. In an open system, matter and energy may cross the boundary. In a closed-to-matter model, matter is retained while energy may still cross. Always state the boundary before making a conservation claim.

Boundary challenge

A cup of water loses mass while sitting uncovered. Draw a boundary around the cup and contents. Then draw a larger boundary around the cup plus surrounding air. Explain how the apparent “lost” matter changes when the boundary changes.

6 · Temperature is not the same as thermal energy

Temperature relates to the average kinetic energy of particles in a simplified model. Total thermal energy depends on more than temperature, including how much matter is present and the material involved. A small hot sample can have a higher temperature while a much larger cooler sample may contain more total thermal energy.

Concept check

Compare a hot cup of water with a warm bathtub. Explain why “hotter” does not automatically mean “contains more thermal energy.” State what additional information matters.

7 · Energy transfers; it does not simply disappear

Energy can be transferred between parts of a system or across its boundary. Useful introductory transfer pathways include heating because of a temperature difference, mechanical work, electrical transfer, and radiation. Scientists track where energy comes from and where it goes.

Energy-accounting chain

Trace energy for a battery-powered fan from the battery to moving blades and the surroundings. Identify at least three stages and explain why saying “the energy was used up” is incomplete.

8 · Matter flow and energy flow are different

Matter consists of particles that can be tracked through a system. Energy is a property that can be stored and transferred. Avoid treating energy as a material substance flowing like water unless an analogy is explicitly limited.

Model critique

A diagram shows food entering an organism and labels an outgoing arrow “energy particles.” Identify the problem. Revise the diagram so matter and energy are represented as different scientific categories.

Application · Sealed reaction mystery

A sealed fictional vessel contains two substances separated by a breakable divider. Total mass is 200.0 g. After mixing, bubbles form, the vessel warms, and the final total mass is 200.0 g.

  1. List direct observations separately from interpretations.
  2. Explain what the unchanged total mass supports.
  3. Explain why bubbles do not mean matter was created.
  4. Propose a particle-level model consistent with conservation.
  5. Identify the system boundary.
  6. Describe an energy transfer consistent with the temperature observation.
  7. State one conclusion the evidence does not establish.
  8. Propose one additional measurement that would discriminate between competing explanations.
