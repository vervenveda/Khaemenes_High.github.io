Measurement & Quantitative Science

Use units as part of scientific meaning. Measure, convert, estimate, calculate rates and density, interpret percentages, and communicate uncertainty without implying more precision than the evidence supports.

1 instructional weekMeasurement & Quantitative Reasoning80% mastery

Learning objectives

  • Select appropriate SI/metric units for common scientific quantities
  • Convert metric quantities using dimensional reasoning
  • Distinguish accuracy, precision, resolution, and uncertainty
  • Calculate density, rates, absolute change, and percent change
  • Estimate orders of magnitude and check whether calculated answers are reasonable
  • Carry units through calculations and use them to detect errors
  • Report numerical conclusions at a precision justified by the measurements
Safety

Use supplied measurements or ordinary classroom objects only. Do not measure hazardous substances, electrical systems, medicines, bodily fluids, or unknown materials.

Unit sense

Choose reasonable units for the thickness of a leaf, mass of a textbook, volume of a water bottle, duration of a heartbeat, and distance between two cities. Explain why each unit is useful.

2 · Metric conversion by equivalence

Conversions should preserve the physical quantity. For example, 1 m = 100 cm, so 2.4 m × (100 cm / 1 m) = 240 cm. The original unit cancels and the desired unit remains.

Conversion practice

Convert: 3.6 m to cm; 4500 mg to g; 0.75 L to mL; 2.5 km to m. Show conversion factors and unit cancellation.

3 · Accuracy, precision, resolution, uncertainty

Accuracy describes closeness to an accepted/reference value. Precision describes closeness among repeated measurements. Resolution is the smallest change an instrument can meaningfully display or distinguish. Uncertainty communicates the range or limits associated with measurement.

Instrument comparison

Instrument A reports 10.1, 10.1, 10.2 cm. Instrument B reports 9.6, 10.5, 10.0 cm for an object whose reference length is 10.0 cm. Compare precision and accuracy carefully. What additional information would you want about instrument resolution?

4 · Density connects mass and volume

Density is mass per unit volume: density = mass ÷ volume. Units matter: g/cm³ and kg/m³ describe density using different unit scales.

Density investigation

A fictional sample has mass 54 g and volume 20 cm³. Calculate density with units. A second sample of the same material has volume 35 cm³. Predict its mass using the calculated density and explain the proportional relationship.

5 · Rates describe change relative to something else

Speed, flow rate, growth rate, heart rate, reaction rate, and temperature change per minute are all rate ideas. Always identify numerator, denominator, and time or comparison interval.

Rate reasoning

Calculate the average temperature-change rate from 0–2 min, 2–4 min, and 4–6 min. Is the rate constant? Support your conclusion numerically.

6 · Absolute change and percent change answer different questions

Absolute change = new − original. Percent change = (new − original) ÷ original × 100%. Percent change expresses the change relative to the starting value.

Compare changes

A measurement rises from 40 to 50. Another rises from 200 to 220. Calculate absolute and percent change for both. Explain why the larger absolute increase does not necessarily have the larger percent increase.

7 · Estimation and reasonableness

Before trusting a calculator, estimate. Orders of magnitude and unit checks can expose misplaced decimals, incorrect conversions, or impossible interpretations.

Error detective

A student converts 2.3 m to 0.023 cm. Diagnose the error without simply redoing the arithmetic. Then estimate the correct magnitude before calculating exactly.

8 · Do not invent precision

If a ruler only supports measurements to about the nearest millimetre, a calculator result containing ten decimal places does not create ten-decimal-place knowledge. Calculated precision is constrained by measurement quality and method.

Communication challenge

A mass is recorded as 12.4 g and volume as 4.1 cm³. A calculator returns density 3.0243902439 g/cm³. Explain why reporting every digit is misleading and propose a defensible reported value.

Application · Quantitative evidence audit

A fictional investigation reports: “The sample warmed 50% faster, proving the material stores twice as much thermal energy.” The underlying measurements show Sample A changed 4 °C in 4 minutes and Sample B changed 6 °C in 4 minutes.

Calculate both average temperature-change rates and the relative difference. Then audit the claim: which part is numerically supported, which part overreaches, what variables are missing, and what additional measurements would be needed to discuss thermal-energy storage?

Week 2 mastery evidence

Complete the ten-item check. 8/10 or higher plus completed reasoning corrections records Week 2 mastery.

  1. Choose an appropriate unit for the diameter of a cell viewed microscopically.
  2. Convert 1.8 m to cm using a conversion factor.
  3. Convert 2500 mL to L.
  4. Distinguish accuracy from precision.
  5. Calculate density for 72 g occupying 24 cm³.
  6. Calculate average rate for a quantity changing from 10 to 34 over 6 minutes.
  7. Calculate absolute change from 80 to 92.
  8. Calculate percent change from 80 to 92.
  9. Explain one way units can reveal a calculation error.
  10. Explain why calculator digits do not automatically represent measurement precision.
Mastery rule: Below 80% triggers targeted correction: identify the missed quantitative skill, reconstruct the calculation with units, explain the original error, solve a parallel problem, and demonstrate the skill again.
