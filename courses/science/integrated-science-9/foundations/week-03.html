Tables, Graphs & Data

Turn measurements into evidence. Read tables and graphs carefully, calculate changes and rates, recognize variation and misleading displays, and distinguish what data show from what a scientist infers.

1 instructional weekData & Evidence80% mastery

Learning objectives

  • Identify variables, units, scales, legends, and comparison groups in tables and graphs
  • Select an appropriate display for common scientific datasets
  • Extract quantitative observations without immediately adding explanations
  • Calculate absolute change, percent change, and interval rate from data
  • Describe trends, plateaus, outliers, overlap, and variability accurately
  • Recognize misleading axes and visual encodings
  • Distinguish correlation, scientific inference, and causal claims
  • Use evidence to revise a model or propose a useful next measurement
Safety & privacy

All datasets are fictional or supplied. No personal health measurements or risky investigations are required.

1 · Read the frame before the pattern

Before interpreting a graph, inspect its title, variables, units, axis scales, legend, categories, sample information, and whether values represent individual observations or summaries.

Graph-reading protocol

For any scientific graph, answer in order: What is measured? In what units? What is compared? What does each symbol/line/bar represent? What range is shown? Only then describe the pattern.

3 · Choose a display that matches the question

Line graphs are useful for many continuous changes over an ordered variable such as time. Bar graphs often compare categories. Scatterplots help examine relationships between paired quantitative variables. Tables are useful when exact values matter.

Display choice

Choose and justify a display for: temperature every minute; mean plant height for four soil types; paired rainfall and stream-flow measurements; exact masses of ten samples.

4 · Trends are not single points

A trend is a pattern across multiple observations. A plateau is a region with little net change. An outlier is unusually distant from the broader pattern and should be investigated rather than automatically deleted.

Pattern language

Using the temperature table, describe the overall trend and identify where warming begins to slow. What additional repeated measurements would help determine whether that apparent slowing is reproducible?

5 · Rate can change within one dataset

An overall average can hide important intervals. Calculate change over the interval of interest: rate = change in dependent variable ÷ change in independent variable.

Interval slopes

For Sample B, calculate average temperature-change rate from 0–2, 2–4, 4–6, and 6–8 minutes. Describe how the rate changes without claiming a mechanism the data alone cannot establish.

6 · Variation is evidence too

Repeated measurements rarely match perfectly. Variation may reflect natural differences, measurement uncertainty, uncontrolled variables, or combinations of causes. Reporting only a mean can hide the spread.

7 · Axes can change the visual impression

A truncated axis is not automatically dishonest, but it can visually magnify small differences. Scientific readers inspect scales before deciding how large an effect appears.

Misleading-graph audit

Two bar charts show values 98 and 100. Graph A starts its vertical axis at 0; Graph B starts at 97. Explain how the same numerical difference can look dramatically different and how you would communicate the effect fairly.

8 · Data show; models explain

A dataset can establish measured patterns. Explanations require additional reasoning and evidence. If two variables rise together, describe the association first; do not automatically write “X caused Y.”

Evidence boundary

A scatterplot shows that days with higher outdoor temperature also have higher electricity use. Give one observation, two possible explanations, one confounding variable, and one additional measurement that would help discriminate among explanations.

Application · Evidence dashboard

A fictional research team provides a table, a line graph, and a short claim about the same experiment. Audit the evidence using the following sequence:

  1. Identify variables, units, scale, and comparison.
  2. Extract three direct observations.
  3. Calculate one absolute change and one interval rate.
  4. Describe variation or an outlier.
  5. Identify whether the visual scale could exaggerate or hide a difference.
  6. Classify the written claim as supported, partly supported, or unsupported.
  7. Propose one next measurement that would strengthen the explanation.

Week 3 mastery evidence

Complete the ten-item check. 8/10 or higher plus reasoning corrections records Week 3 mastery.

  1. Identify the independent and dependent variables from a described graph.
  2. State why units must be read before interpreting values.
  3. Choose an appropriate graph type for change over time.
  4. Calculate absolute change between two supplied values.
  5. Calculate an interval rate from two time points.
  6. Distinguish a trend from a single observation.
  7. Explain why two datasets with the same mean can still differ meaningfully.
  8. Explain how a truncated axis can alter visual impression.
  9. Rewrite a causal claim as an evidence-limited association statement.
  10. Propose one additional measurement that could test a competing explanation.
Mastery rule: Below 80% triggers targeted review of the missed data skill, correction of the original reasoning, a parallel table/graph task, and a new demonstration. Correct answers alone are insufficient when the missed objective requires explanation.
