Unit 03 · Day 14

Integrated Systems Case Study

Bring the unit together by explaining a fictional warm-day hill walk as interacting flows of matter, energy, and information—while keeping observations, calculations, mechanisms, and limitations distinct.

45–60 minutesTier 1 · Home-safeRequired Performance Task

Learning objectives

  • Integrate respiratory, circulatory, digestive, liver, muscular, nervous, endocrine, skin, and urinary functions into one causal model
  • Trace oxygen, carbon dioxide, glucose, water, heat, and nitrogenous waste without losing matter/energy distinctions
  • Use the unit's generalized feedback architecture in a whole-body scenario
  • Calculate changes in physiological variables and distinguish those observations from mechanistic inferences
  • Identify competing demands, delays, limitations, and privacy/evidence boundaries

Materials

  • Case-study page
  • Notebook
  • Calculator

Tier 1 · Home-safe

This is a fictional case analysis, not a physical challenge or medical evaluation. Do not recreate the exercise/heat scenario for data collection. Use the supplied dataset.

Privacy

No personal heart rate, breathing rate, hydration, diet, diagnosis, fitness, medication, or health information is required. Use only the fictional student and supplied data.

The scenario

A fictional student eats lunch, waits 45 minutes, and then walks briskly uphill for 12 minutes on a warm day. During the activity, heart rate and breathing rate increase, sweating begins, thirst changes, and several regulatory systems respond simultaneously. Eight minutes after the walk, some measured variables move back toward their earlier values.

Today's challenge: do not explain this as “the body works harder.” Replace vague language with mechanism-labeled connections among systems.

Three kinds of flow

The strongest whole-body model keeps matter, energy, and information separate even when they travel through the same organs.

Classification warm-up

Classify each as primarily matter flow, energy transfer, or information flow: oxygen entering pulmonary blood; insulin reaching a target cell; thermal energy leaving skin; glucose entering a muscle cell; a motor-neurone signal reaching muscle; carbon dioxide moving into alveoli. Defend two classifications.

Systems-link organizer

Choose at least five modules. For every connection write System A → mechanism → System B. Example structure: respiratory exchange → changes oxygen availability in pulmonary blood → circulatory transport. Do not use an unlabeled arrow.

Trace matter without making it disappear

Whole-body explanations often fail because molecules appear at one organ and disappear at another. Use conservation reasoning throughout the case.

Matter trace

Trace oxygen, carbon dioxide, glucose, water, and urea. For each substance identify an entry/source, at least one transport step, at least one relevant exchange or transformation, and an endpoint or continuing pathway. Distinguish bulk transport, membrane transport/diffusion, chemical transformation, storage, and excretion.

Matter ≠ energy: do not write “glucose turns into energy” or “oxygen becomes energy.” Cellular metabolism transfers chemical energy while atoms are rearranged among molecules; carbon-containing matter can ultimately contribute to carbon dioxide and other products.

Follow thermal energy

Muscular activity increases rates of cellular energy transfer, and some transferred energy becomes thermal energy. On a warm day, the resulting heat load interacts with environmental temperature, radiation, airflow, humidity, skin blood flow, and evaporation.

Energy pathway

Construct a causal chain from increased muscular activity to increased thermal-energy production and then to at least two heat-transfer pathways. Explain why sweating alone does not guarantee effective cooling; include evaporation and one environmental variable.

Information coordinates the response

Changes in activity and internal conditions are detected by receptors and regulatory tissues. Nervous and endocrine information can alter heart activity, breathing, blood-vessel behavior, sweating, thirst-related behavior, kidney water handling, and nutrient metabolism. Different responses occur on different time scales.

Neural/endocrine comparison

Select one rapid response and one slower or more sustained regulatory response in the case. For each identify stimulus/deviation → detection/control → information route → target/effector → response. Explain why the two signaling architectures need not operate on identical time scales.

Negative feedback in the case

Choose one regulated variable—such as internal temperature or water-balance conditions—and apply the architecture developed on Days 08–10.

Complete feedback loop

Build regulated variable changes → detection/control response → signal → effector → physiological response → variable moves toward regulated range. Then add one delay, one environmental or physiological disturbance, and one reason the variable may not immediately return to its starting value.

Calculate before explaining

The performance task provides fictional before, during, and recovery measurements. Numerical change is an observation; the biological explanation for that change is an inference supported by the model.

Quantitative preparation

For heart rate changing from 74 to 126 beats/min, calculate absolute change and percent change from baseline. For breathing rate changing from 15 to 29 breaths/min, do the same. Then calculate how far each variable has returned toward baseline at the supplied 8-minute recovery measurement. Show formulas and units.

Observation → inference

Write one sentence containing only what the heart-rate data directly show. Then write a second sentence proposing a physiological explanation. What additional measurement would strengthen that explanation?

Competing demands

Integrated physiology is interesting because multiple demands occur at once. Active muscles require increased transport; digestion is still occurring after lunch; thermoregulation can alter skin blood flow; water loss through sweating changes water-balance pressures; and regulatory signals can overlap.

Extended systems challenge

Identify two demands that could compete for circulation or regulatory resources in this case. Explain how the body can redistribute flows rather than simply increasing every process equally. Add one prediction that could be tested with additional fictional data.

Evidence limits

The supplied measurements show patterns in a fictional scenario. They do not independently reveal every mechanism and cannot establish a diagnosis, fitness ranking, hydration diagnosis, endocrine condition, or disease state.

Four limitations

State at least four limitations of the case evidence. Include one missing measurement, one uncontrolled/context variable, one timing limitation, and one reason the results should not be generalized into a medical conclusion.

Performance task

Your formal submission uses the dedicated case-study workspace. It requires at least five accurately linked systems; oxygen, carbon dioxide, glucose, water, heat, and a waste pathway; a complete negative-feedback loop; calculations; observation/inference separation; four limitations; and privacy safeguards.
