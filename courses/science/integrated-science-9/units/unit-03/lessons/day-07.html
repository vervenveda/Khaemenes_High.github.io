Unit 03 · Day 7

The Liver & Nutrient Regulation

Follow absorbed matter into a metabolic hub where storage, conversion, release, chemical processing, and waste formation connect digestion to whole-body homeostasis.

45–60 minutesTier 1 · Home-safePortfolio Evidence

Learning objectives

  • Explain selected liver functions as transformations and regulated flows rather than a list of jobs
  • Trace absorbed glucose and amino-acid matter from intestine through liver and circulation
  • Connect glycogen storage/release to blood-glucose regulation without treating the liver as the only regulator
  • Trace nitrogen from amino acids through deamination, urea formation, transport, and urinary excretion
  • Use precise language when discussing metabolism, detoxification, storage, and conversion

Tier 1 · Home-safe

Activities are educational, not medical evaluation. No supplement, medicine, dietary experiment, or personal health disclosure is required.

Privacy

Students never need to publish personal health, medicine, or dietary information. Use fictional examples.

From absorption to processing

Day 06 ended when many absorbed water-soluble nutrients entered intestinal capillary blood. Much of that blood travels first to the liver through the hepatic portal circulation. This positioning allows the liver to receive and process changing nutrient loads before those materials are distributed more broadly.

Continuity check

Trace glucose from the small-intestinal lumen to the liver. Label the intestinal exchange surface, capillary blood, hepatic portal circulation, and liver tissue. At each arrow, state whether matter is being chemically changed or mainly changing location.

The liver as a metabolic hub

The liver performs many functions simultaneously. A useful systems model asks what material enters, what transformation or storage process occurs, what leaves, and what signals influence the flow.

Storage is dynamic, not a warehouse

Glycogen storage is reversible. After a carbohydrate-containing meal, changing hormonal signals and nutrient conditions can favor glucose uptake and glycogen synthesis in liver and other tissues. At other times, liver glycogen can be broken down and glucose can be released into the blood. The liver therefore participates in a regulated flow rather than merely “holding sugar.”

Causal diagram

Build two mechanism-labeled chains: after-meal conditions → signaling changes → liver glucose handling → blood-glucose consequence and between-meal conditions → signaling changes → liver glycogen/glucose handling → blood-glucose consequence. Mark at least one feedback arrow. You will deepen the insulin/glucagon mechanisms on Day 10.

Daily word · deamination

Deamination refers to removal of an amino group during amino-acid metabolism. It is a chemical-processing step within a larger pathway; it is not synonymous with protein digestion, urea formation, kidney filtration, or excretion.

Boundary challenge

Put these events in causal order: protein digestion → amino-acid absorption → blood transport → amino-acid metabolism/deamination → nitrogen enters urea-forming pathways → urea transport in blood → kidney handling → urinary excretion. For each arrow, label whether it represents chemical transformation, membrane transport, or bulk transport.

Follow the nitrogen

Amino acids contain nitrogen as well as carbon-containing structures. During amino-acid metabolism, amino groups can be removed. In a simplified Grade 9 model, nitrogen from this metabolism contributes to urea formation in the liver. The nitrogen atoms do not disappear: urea travels in blood to the kidneys, where Day 08 will examine filtration, selective reabsorption, and excretion.

Precision: urea is not undigested protein and is not produced by the kidneys. The liver forms urea through a multi-step metabolic pathway; the kidneys remove urea from blood as part of urine formation.
Conservation-of-matter challenge

A student writes, “The liver removes the nitrogen and gets rid of it.” Rewrite the statement so every major location and transformation is accounted for. Your explanation must contain amino acid, liver, deamination, urea, blood, kidney, and urine.

Model limit: actual nitrogen metabolism and the urea cycle involve multiple reactions and intermediates. This lesson preserves the major matter-flow relationship without pretending the chemistry is a single reaction.

Bile: production is not storage or action

The liver produces bile. The gall bladder can store and concentrate bile before release into the small intestine. Bile salts help emulsify fats, increasing the surface available for lipase action. Emulsification is a physical dispersal process, not enzymatic hydrolysis of lipid molecules.

Precision repair

Correct this sentence: “The gall bladder makes an enzyme called bile that chemically digests fat.” Identify every error rather than replacing only one word.

“Detoxification” requires precise language

The liver chemically processes many endogenous substances, medicines, and environmental compounds through enzyme-mediated reactions. These transformations can make a compound easier to eliminate, change its biological activity, or sometimes create metabolites with their own effects.

Do not use “detox” to mean “the liver makes every harmful substance safe.” Toxicity depends on substance, dose, route, timing, metabolism, individual variation, and other factors. Some metabolites can remain active or be harmful.
Claim repair

Rewrite the statement “The liver removes all toxins from the body” as a scientifically defensible explanation. Include chemical transformation, dose/exposure, metabolites, and elimination pathways.

Evidence boundary

Compare “the liver participates in processing many compounds” with “this product cleans toxins from your liver.” Explain why the second statement requires specific evidence that the first biological fact does not provide. Name at least two kinds of evidence you would want before accepting the product claim.

Integrated post-meal systems model

A meal creates simultaneous flows of matter and information. Digestion produces absorbable molecules; circulation transports them; the liver processes changing nutrient loads; endocrine signals alter tissue behavior; cells assimilate matter; and kidneys later participate in regulating water, ions, and nitrogenous wastes.

Portfolio evidence

Explain how digestive, circulatory, endocrine, liver, cellular, and urinary functions cooperate after a fictional protein-and-carbohydrate meal. Your model must include: one carbon-containing nutrient pathway, one nitrogen pathway, one hormonal-information pathway, at least five mechanism-labeled arrows, and one limitation of the model. Explicitly distinguish digestion from absorption, absorption from assimilation, deamination from urea formation, and urea formation from urea excretion.

Model trade-offs

A liver-centered diagram is useful for showing processing, but it can accidentally imply that the liver acts independently. Real regulation is distributed across tissues and depends on blood flow, nervous/endocrine signals, cellular demand, kidney function, and time.

Identify one useful feature of a “metabolic hub” model and two ways it could mislead a student. Then add one missing system connection that improves the model.
