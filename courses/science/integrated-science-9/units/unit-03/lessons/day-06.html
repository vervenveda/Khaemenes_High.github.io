Unit 03 · Day 6

Digestion & Absorption

Trace matter from food molecules through digestion, absorption, transport, and assimilation while conserving atoms and distinguishing each process precisely.

45–60 minutesTier 1 · Home-safePortfolio Evidence

Learning objectives

  • Distinguish ingestion, mechanical digestion, chemical digestion, absorption, assimilation, and egestion
  • Explain how enzymes change reaction pathways without being consumed as nutrients
  • Relate villus and microvillus structure to surface area, diffusion distance, and transport
  • Distinguish nutrient entry into blood from lipid transport through lymphatic pathways
  • Trace matter from a food macromolecule to molecules used or stored by cells

Tier 1 · Home-safe

Activities are educational, not medical evaluation. No tasting, supplement use, dietary restriction, or personal health disclosure is required.

Privacy

Students never need to publish personal health or dietary information. Use fictional examples when discussing food and digestion.

The pathway is not the process

The digestive tract can be represented as mouth → oesophagus → stomach → small intestine → large intestine → rectum → anus. Salivary glands, liver, gall bladder, and pancreas contribute substances that support digestive processes. But memorizing the route is not enough: different physical and chemical changes occur along it.

ProcessWhat changes?What does not automatically follow?
IngestionFood enters the digestive tractIt has not necessarily been chemically digested or absorbed
Mechanical digestionPhysical size/arrangement changesLarge molecules are not necessarily chemically broken into absorbable units
Chemical digestionChemical bonds are broken through reactions, producing smaller moleculesThe products have not necessarily crossed the intestinal surface
AbsorptionDigested molecules cross an exchange surface into internal transport pathwaysThey have not necessarily been used by cells
AssimilationAbsorbed molecules are taken up and used, transformed, or stored by cells/tissuesThis is not the same event as intestinal absorption
EgestionUnabsorbed material leaves the digestive tractEgestion is not the same process as excretion of metabolic wastes

Digestion: physical change and enzyme-catalyzed chemistry

Mechanical processing can break food into smaller pieces and increase exposed surface area. Chemical digestion uses enzyme-catalyzed reactions to break large biological molecules into smaller molecules that can be transported across exchange surfaces.

Enzyme reasoning: an enzyme is a biological catalyst. It provides a reaction pathway with lower activation energy and can therefore increase reaction rate under suitable conditions. The enzyme is not “used up” as a nutrient in the reaction it catalyzes.

StarchSimple sugars including glucoseSmaller soluble molecules can be absorbed and transported
ProteinsAmino acids and small peptides during digestionProducts can ultimately supply amino acids for absorption and cellular use
LipidsFatty acids and monoglycerides in a simplified modelProducts can be processed by intestinal cells and transported through lipid-handling pathways
Mechanism check

Explain why chewing can increase the rate of later chemical digestion without claiming that chewing itself converts starch into glucose. Then explain why emulsification of fat can increase the surface available for enzyme action without describing emulsification as chemical digestion.

Small intestine: structure → physical consequence → absorption

The inner surface of the small intestine has folds, villi, and microscopic microvilli that greatly increase total surface area. A thin epithelial exchange barrier reduces diffusion distance. Blood capillaries and lymphatic vessels within villi provide transport routes that can carry absorbed material away from the exchange surface.

FeaturePhysical consequenceAbsorption consequence
Folds + villi + microvilliLarge total surface areaMore membrane area is available for transport at one time
Thin epithelial barrierShort transport/diffusion distanceMolecules cross less tissue between intestinal contents and internal transport
Capillary networkRapid removal/delivery of many water-soluble transported substancesSupports continued transport and movement into circulation
Lacteal/lymphatic pathwayProvides a distinct transport route for many absorbed dietary lipids after intestinal processingShows that “absorbed into blood” is not an adequate description for every nutrient pathway
Transfer from Days 03–04

Compare an intestinal villus with an alveolus. Identify two shared exchange-surface design principles and one important difference in what is transported or where transported material goes.

Blood versus lymph: do not collapse the routes

Many water-soluble digestion products, including glucose and amino acids, enter blood capillaries associated with intestinal villi and are transported toward the liver through the hepatic portal circulation. Many dietary lipid products are processed within intestinal cells and packaged into particles that enter lymphatic lacteals before later joining the bloodstream.

Precision: “all nutrients go directly from the intestine into the bloodstream” is too simple. Different absorbed materials can follow different transport routes.

Molecule trace: starch to a muscle cell

Scientific tracing requires accounting for matter at each transition. The carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen atoms in a starch molecule do not disappear when digestion occurs; atoms are rearranged into products through chemical reactions.

Portfolio evidence

Trace starch in a fictional meal through these stages: ingestion → mechanical processing → enzyme-catalyzed chemical digestion → glucose in the small intestine → absorption across intestinal cells → blood transport → liver/circulation → muscle-cell uptake → assimilation. At each arrow, write what physically or chemically happens. End by giving two possible fates for glucose carbon in the muscle cell, such as storage in glycogen or participation in cellular metabolic pathways.

Conservation-of-matter challenge

Digestion changes molecules; it does not destroy matter. Absorption changes location; it does not mean the molecule has vanished into the body. Assimilation changes how cells use, transform, or store absorbed material.

A student says, “Food disappears during digestion and becomes energy.” Rewrite the statement so that it respects conservation of matter and distinguishes matter from energy. Include at least one molecule and one cellular process in your explanation.

Model limits

A simple digestive pathway diagram is useful for sequence, but it leaves out enzyme specificity, changing chemical conditions, transport proteins, intestinal movements, microbial activity, regulatory signals, differences among nutrients, and interactions with circulation, lymphatic transport, liver processing, and cellular metabolism.

Model critique

Identify one feature a simple “tube” model represents well, two mechanisms it leaves out, and one observation that would require a more detailed model.
