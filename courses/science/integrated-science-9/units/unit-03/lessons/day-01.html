Systems Thinking & Homeostasis

Model the body as an interacting system that maintains internal conditions within functional ranges.

45–60 minutesTier 1 · Home-safePortfolio Evidence

Learning objectives

  • Define homeostasis accurately
  • Identify inputs, outputs, boundaries, and interactions
  • Distinguish ranges from perfect constancy
  • Build and critique a causal feedback diagram

Materials

  • Notebook
  • Homeostasis loop template
Tier 1 · Home-safe

Activities are educational, not medical evaluation. Use provided datasets whenever personal measurement, movement, health history, or supervision makes direct participation inappropriate.

Privacy

Students never need to publish personal health data. Use anonymous labels or fictional datasets.

Dynamic stability

Body temperature, blood glucose, water, carbon dioxide, ions, and other variables change continuously. Homeostasis keeps them within ranges that support cellular function.

Precision: Homeostasis does not mean that every value stays exactly the same. A regulated variable can fluctuate and still remain within a functional range.

Systems exchange

Organisms receive matter, energy, and information; transform and transport them; and release heat, carbon dioxide, nitrogenous wastes, water, and other outputs.

Portfolio evidence

Trace oxygen, glucose, water, or heat from entry through transport and use to storage or exit. Mark where matter is transported, transformed, stored, or released.

Feedback pathway

StimulusVariable changes
ReceptorDetects change
CoordinatorProcesses information
EffectorActs
ResponseChanges the variable

A feedback loop is a causal model. Each arrow should mean more than “comes next”: it should represent a plausible mechanism linking one step to the next.

Causal-diagram check

Choose one regulated variable and draw the pathway using arrows. Under every arrow, write the mechanism that makes the connection plausible. Then identify one observation that would weaken your model.

Negative feedback: the response tends to oppose the original deviation and move the regulated condition toward its functional range. Positive feedback: the response amplifies the change until a separate stopping condition or endpoint occurs.

Delay, overshoot & model limits

Real biological regulation is not instantaneous. Detection, signaling, transport, and effector action take time. Delays can produce temporary overshoot or oscillation, especially when a response continues after the regulated variable has already begun changing direction.

A useful model should therefore state what it leaves out. Examples include multiple interacting signals, individual variation, environmental conditions, unequal response times, and effects occurring at different biological scales.

Pathway choices
FoundationUse a prepared loop and explain each arrow.
CoreBuild two feedback loops and identify the regulated variable, detector, signal, effector, and response.
ExtendedAdd delay, overshoot, one competing influence, and at least two model limitations.
