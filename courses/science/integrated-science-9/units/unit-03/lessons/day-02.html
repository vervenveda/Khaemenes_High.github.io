Circulation & Transport

Trace pulmonary and systemic circulation as linked transport loops that move matter, thermal energy, and chemical information between tissues.

45–60 minutesTier 1 · Home-safePortfolio EvidenceScientific Reading Lab

Learning objectives

  • Trace blood through heart, lungs, and body tissues
  • Distinguish pulmonary and systemic circuits
  • Explain why vessel direction—not oxygen content—defines artery and vein
  • Connect circulation to cellular needs, exchange, and homeostasis
  • Use perfusion as a systems concept rather than a vocabulary fact
  • Extract and cite scientific evidence about vessel structure, blood flow, and perfusion

Materials

Double circulation

The pulmonary circuit links the heart and lungs. The systemic circuit links the heart with body tissues. The two circuits operate in sequence, so blood repeatedly moves through heart → lungs → heart → body → heart.

FeaturePulmonary circuitSystemic circuit
Main connectionHeart ↔ lungsHeart ↔ body tissues
Primary exchange emphasized hereOxygen and carbon dioxideGases, nutrients, wastes, heat, signals
Returns toLeft side of heartRight side of heart
Shared systems roleMaintains transport between specialized exchange, storage, signaling, metabolic, and excretory sites
Systems connection: circulation does not create oxygen, nutrients, hormones, or wastes. It transports materials and thermal energy between places where exchange, transformation, storage, signaling, or removal occur.

Evidence extraction

Write three separate statements: (1) Source observation: what the reading explicitly states about circulation or perfusion. (2) Scientific inference: what you infer from that evidence. (3) Model revision: what you would add, remove, or relabel in your own circulation model. Do not present an inference as though it were a quotation from the source.

Figure-reading challenge

Use one vessel/circulation figure in the OpenStax reading. Before reading its explanatory text, identify the variables or structures represented, the direction of flow, and one relationship the figure appears to show. Then read the caption/text and state whether your interpretation was supported, corrected, or remained uncertain.

Citation principle: a source can support a claim about vessel structure or blood flow without automatically supporting every explanation you build from it. Cite the source for what it actually establishes and label your additional reasoning as inference.

Direction matters

Arteries carry blood away from the heart; veins carry blood toward it. Pulmonary vessels demonstrate why oxygen content does not define vessel type: the pulmonary artery carries oxygen-poor blood toward the lungs, while pulmonary veins return oxygen-rich blood toward the heart.

Precision: blood does not flow because it “knows” where oxygen is needed. Pressure differences generated within the circulatory system drive bulk flow; exchange then occurs where vessel structure, gradients, and tissue conditions permit it.
Portfolio evidence · Route reconstruction

Trace a red blood cell from a leg-muscle capillary to an alveolar capillary and then back to the leg muscle. Include the major chambers and vessels in order. At each exchange surface, label what changes in the blood and what process makes that change possible.

Transported materials

Blood transports oxygen and carbon dioxide, absorbed nutrients, water, ions, hormones, immune cells and proteins, metabolic wastes, and thermal energy. Different tissues add or remove different materials depending on their activity.

Open Circulation Pathway

Perfusion: route plus delivery

Perfusion describes blood delivery through the vessels of a tissue or organ. A correct map of the circulation tells us where blood can travel; perfusion asks whether blood actually reaches an exchange region in a way that supports transport.

Prediction challenge

Imagine ventilation at the lungs stays unchanged while perfusion through part of the lung decreases in a simplified model. Predict how that could affect gas exchange and explain why “air reached the alveoli” is not, by itself, a complete explanation of oxygen transport.

From route to mechanism

A route tells us where blood travels. A scientific explanation also asks why the route works. Pressure differences drive flow, valves help limit backflow in appropriate locations, and capillary networks place blood close to tissues for exchange.

Causal reasoning

Choose one transported item and write a four-step causal chain showing how circulation contributes to homeostasis. Example structure: tissue demand changes → transported material or signal changes → circulatory delivery/removal changes → regulated condition is supported. Label each arrow with the mechanism you think connects the steps, then state one observation that would make you revise your chain.
