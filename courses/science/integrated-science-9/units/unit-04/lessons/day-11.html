Unit 04 · Day 11

Genotype, Phenotype & Dominance

Use genetic language accurately and reject strength or value misconceptions.

45–60 minutesModel and data basedPortfolio Evidence

Learning objectives

  • Distinguish homozygous and heterozygous
  • Use dominant and recessive correctly
  • Avoid genetic ranking language

Materials

  • Notebook
  • Fictional allele cards
Safe genetics learning

Use fictional organisms, supplied datasets, paper models, or digital tools. No biological samples or medical tests are required.

Genetic privacy and respect

Do not collect family medical history, ancestry, paternity evidence, reproductive information, or identifiable genetic data. Human worth is never ranked by genes.

Allele combinations

AA and aa are homozygous; Aa is heterozygous. Symbol choice does not indicate importance.

Dominance relationship

In complete dominance, the heterozygous phenotype matches one homozygote.

Dominant does not mean: common, stronger, healthier, advanced, or morally better.

Phenotype complexity

Phenotype may depend on age, environment, other genes, development, and measurement.

Define six inheritance terms and list one misuse to avoid.

Essential question and conceptual model

Essential question: How can geneticists describe allele combinations and phenotype relationships without turning “dominant” and “recessive” into rankings of strength or value?

A genotype records allele information at specified loci. An individual with two copies of the same allele at a locus is homozygous; one with two different alleles is heterozygous. A phenotype is the observable or measurable outcome under particular biological and environmental conditions.

In a simple complete-dominance model, a heterozygote may show the same categorical phenotype as one homozygote. “Dominant” therefore describes a phenotype relationship in a model—it does not mean stronger, healthier, more common, more adaptive, or more valuable.

Worked genotype-to-phenotype example

Suppose a fictional plant has alleles A and a at one locus. Under a stated complete-dominance model:

  • AA — homozygous; phenotype category 1.
  • Aa — heterozygous; phenotype category 1.
  • aa — homozygous; phenotype category 2.

The model describes this one locus under these stated conditions. It does not tell you whether A is more common, more beneficial, or associated with higher biological value.

Vocabulary and misconception checks

Homozygous: two same allele versions at a modeled locus. Heterozygous: two different allele versions. Dominant: allele/phenotype relationship visible in the heterozygous model. Recessive: phenotype relationship masked in the heterozygote under complete dominance.

  • Misconception: Dominant means common. Rare alleles can be dominant and common alleles can be recessive.
  • Misconception: Dominant means genetically superior. It does not.
  • Misconception: One genotype always guarantees one phenotype regardless of context. Many traits involve additional genes and environment.

Retrieval and explanation practice

  1. What is the difference between homozygous and heterozygous?
  2. Why is Aa not “half dominant” under a complete-dominance model?
  3. Can a recessive allele be common in a population?
  4. Why should dominance language never be used to rank people?
Practice explanations — open after attempting
  1. Homozygous means two same modeled allele versions; heterozygous means two different versions.
  2. The heterozygous phenotype falls in the dominant category under the stated model.
  3. Yes. Dominance and population frequency describe different properties.
  4. Dominance describes an allele-phenotype relationship, not intelligence, health, morality, or human worth.
