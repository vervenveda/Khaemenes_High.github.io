Two-pair model

For unlinked AaBb, independent assortment produces AB, Ab, aB, and ab gametes with equal expected probability.

Why four gamete types appear

A diploid teaching organism has two copies of each modeled chromosome. In the genotype AaBb, one homologous pair carries A or a and a second pair carries B or b. During meiosis, homologous chromosomes segregate. Under independent assortment, the orientation of one chromosome pair does not determine the orientation of the other pair. Each gamete receives one allele for each modeled gene, giving AB, Ab, aB, or ab.

The 1:1:1:1 ratio is a probability prediction, not a promise that every group of four gametes contains one of each type. It assumes two unlinked loci and a simplified model of equal gamete viability. Crossing over can create different allele combinations for linked genes; biological inheritance cannot be reduced to this model in every situation.

Worked example: from model to expected counts

  1. List the options for the first gene: A or a, each with modeled probability 1/2.
  2. List the options for the second unlinked gene: B or b, each with probability 1/2.
  3. Multiply independent probabilities: P(AB) = (1/2) × (1/2) = 1/4. The same holds for Ab, aB and ab.
  4. For 20 modeled gametes, the expected number of AB gametes is 20 × 1/4 = 5. This is an average across many comparable sets of trials, not a guaranteed observed count.
  5. Check the total: four expected counts of five add to 20; four probabilities of 1/4 add to one.

Second example: For 40 independently generated gametes, the expected count of aB is 40 × 1/4 = 10. An observed count of eight is compatible with ordinary sampling variation; it does not, by itself, disprove independent assortment.

Guided investigation and data record

  1. Prepare two equally likely A/a cards and two equally likely B/b cards, or use the linked digital meiosis laboratory.
  2. For each of 20 trials, choose one allele independently from each pair, record the two-letter gamete, and replace or reset both cards before the next trial.
  3. Create a frequency table for AB, Ab, aB and ab. Check that the counts total 20.
  4. Graph the four observed frequencies beside the expected count of five for each.
  5. Compute an observed percentage: (gamete count ÷ 20) × 100. Explain why a percentage different from 25% can occur even if the model is working correctly.
  6. Repeat or combine trials to compare variability across sample sizes. Preserve your original trial data rather than adjusting counts to match a prediction.

Important distinction: Drawing allele cards is a random model of chromosome segregation. It is not direct microscopy evidence of real gamete production.

Check your reasoning before the laboratory

  1. Can a gamete produced by the simplified AaBb model contain both A and a? Explain what a gamete receives from each homologous pair.
  2. Why does the multiplication rule give P(aB) = 1/4 in this model? State the assumption that permits multiplication.
  3. A sample of 20 modeled gametes contains six AB, four Ab, seven aB and three ab. Are the totals valid? Does this prove linkage? Justify your answer.
  4. How would you change the model if the genes were physically linked on the same chromosome?
Practice explanations — open after attempting
  1. No. The simplified gamete receives one allele from each modeled pair: one of A/a and one of B/b.
  2. P(a) × P(B) = 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4, under the independence assumption.
  3. The counts total 20; sampling variability alone is insufficient to establish linkage.
  4. Represent allele combinations on the same chromosome, include parental haplotypes, and model recombination frequency rather than using independent draws.

Scientific vocabulary and misconception checks

Allele: a version of a gene. Homologous chromosomes: chromosomes with corresponding genes at corresponding loci. Segregation: separation of allele copies into gametes. Independent assortment: independent segregation of distinct chromosome pairs in the simplified model. Expected frequency: predicted proportion or count under stated assumptions. Sampling variation: differences between observed counts and expectations arising from finite random trials.

Avoid these misconceptions: equal expected probabilities do not mean equal observed counts; a Punnett square predicts probabilities rather than guaranteed offspring; and two genes on the same chromosome need not assort independently.

Portfolio evidence and educator scoring guide

Submit the original 20-trial table, labeled comparison chart, probability calculations with units where appropriate, a claim–evidence–reasoning paragraph, and a stated model limitation. Teachers or learning partners may score four criteria at 0–2 points each: correct allele combinations, reproducible data recording, accurate quantitative comparison, and evidence-based explanation of assumptions/uncertainty. A total of 7–8 demonstrates strong proficiency; students scoring below that should receive feedback and revise. This formative rubric does not replace the separate unit assessment or its established 80% mastery requirement.

Assumptions

Genes are unlinked, gametes equally viable, mutation ignored, and sampling random.
