Meiosis I & Homologous Pairs

Model homologous pairing, crossing over, independent orientation, and chromosome-number reduction.

45–60 minutesModel and data basedPortfolio Evidence

Materials

Safe genetics learning

Use fictional organisms, supplied datasets, paper models, or digital tools. No biological samples or medical tests are required.

Genetic privacy and respect

Do not collect family medical history, ancestry, paternity evidence, reproductive information, or identifiable genetic data. Human worth is never ranked by genes.

Homologous pairing

After replication, each homologue has two sister chromatids. Homologues pair during early meiosis I.

Crossing over

Non-sister chromatids can exchange corresponding segments, producing recombinant chromatids.

Precision: Crossing over reshuffles existing DNA and is not itself a mutation.

Reduction

Homologues separate during meiosis I, reducing the chromosome set.

Open laboratory

Essential question and conceptual model

Essential question: Why does the first meiotic division reduce chromosome-set number while also creating new allele combinations?

Before meiosis begins, DNA is replicated. During meiosis I, homologous chromosomes recognize and pair with one another. In the simplified model, corresponding homologues align as pairs while sister chromatids remain joined. Exchanges can occur between non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosomes during crossing over, producing recombinant chromatids.

Homologous pairs can orient in more than one direction at the first division. That independent orientation contributes to different combinations of maternal- and paternal-origin homologues in descendant cells. When homologues separate during meiosis I, each resulting cell receives one chromosome from each homologous pair, reducing the chromosome-set state from diploid to haploid even though each chromosome is still replicated.

Worked chromosome model

For a fictional species with 2n = 4, there are two homologous chromosome pairs.

  1. After DNA replication, the cell still has 4 chromosomes, now represented by 8 chromatids.
  2. The two homologous pairs align independently.
  3. At the end of meiosis I, each daughter cell receives one chromosome from each pair.
  4. Each resulting cell is haploid with n = 2, but each chromosome still consists of sister chromatids.

Vocabulary and misconception checks

Synapsis: pairing of homologous chromosomes. Crossing over: exchange between non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosomes. Independent assortment: independent orientation and segregation of different homologous pairs in the simplified model. Reduction division: division that reduces chromosome-set number.

  • Misconception: Homologous chromosomes are the same as sister chromatids.
  • Misconception: Crossing over swaps whole chromosomes. It exchanges corresponding DNA segments between chromatids.
  • Misconception: Meiosis I separates sister chromatids. The defining separation is homologous chromosomes.

Retrieval and explanation practice

  1. What separates during meiosis I?
  2. Why can crossing over create recombinant chromatids?
  3. If 2n = 10, what is n after homologues have separated normally?
  4. Why is a cell after meiosis I haploid even though its chromosomes may still be replicated?
Practice explanations — open after attempting
  1. Homologous chromosomes.
  2. DNA segments are exchanged between non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosomes.
  3. n = 5.
  4. Haploid/diploid describes chromosome sets, not whether each chromosome has one or two sister chromatids.
