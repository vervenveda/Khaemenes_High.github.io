Unit 01 · Day 7

Tables, Graphs & Scale

Turn measurements into honest visual evidence. Organize traceable tables, choose a display that matches the variables, make scale decisions transparently, and detect graphs that exaggerate, conceal, or imply relationships the data cannot support.

60–75 minutesTier 1 · Home-safeData VisualizationGraph IntegrityPortfolio Evidence

Learning objectives

  • Construct a data table whose headings identify variables, units, raw observations, and calculated summaries.
  • Choose among bar charts, line graphs, and scatter plots based on variable type and scientific purpose.
  • Assign independent/explanatory variables and dependent/response variables appropriately to axes.
  • Select and defend readable axis ranges and intervals without visually exaggerating or hiding patterns.
  • Distinguish interpolation within observed data from extrapolation beyond the tested range.
  • Critique a graph for missing context, misleading scale, unsupported connecting lines, or claims that exceed the evidence.

Materials

  • Science notebook
  • Graph paper and ruler, or accessible spreadsheet/graphing tool
  • Paper-helicopter raw data and summaries from Day 06
  • Dataset Lab through Science Studio when useful
Tier 1 · Home-safe

This lesson analyzes existing data and requires no new helicopter drops. Use ordinary writing/computing materials safely and take normal screen/ergonomic breaks when needed.

1. A graph is an argument about data

The same numbers can look calm, dramatic, clustered, or nearly flat depending on graph type, axis range, interval, aspect ratio, and which observations are shown. A scientific graph should make the evidence easier to inspect—not manipulate the reader's impression.

First principle

Write two reasons a graph can be numerically accurate yet visually misleading.

2. Build tables that preserve the evidence trail

Table headings should name the variable and unit. Raw observations should remain distinguishable from calculated values such as mean and range.

Strong heading: Fall time (s)
Weak heading: Results
Strong structure: Rotor length (cm) | Trial 1 (s) ... Trial 5 (s) | Mean (s) | Range (s)
  • Place units in headings rather than repeatedly in every numerical cell when one unit applies to the whole column.
  • Use consistent decimal conventions that reflect the measurement method.
  • Do not silently replace raw values with rounded summaries.
  • Mark missing observations explicitly rather than entering a zero unless zero was actually measured.
Missing ≠ zero: a blank/missing trial means no usable measurement was recorded. A value of 0 s is an actual numerical claim and would mean something very different.

3. Choose the display from the variables

DisplayUseful whenCommon misuse
Bar chartComparing numerical summaries across discrete categoriesTreating a continuous numerical variable as unrelated categories when its ordered spacing matters
Line graphShowing change across time or another ordered continuum when connecting adjacent values has scientific meaningConnecting categories or untested gaps in a way that implies continuous observations
Scatter plotExamining association between two numerical variablesAssuming a visible association proves causation

For rotor length versus mean fall time, both variables are numerical. A scatter plot is a strong default because it preserves the tested x-values without requiring you to imply that every value between them was observed.

Graph plan

Name your display, x-axis variable/unit, y-axis variable/unit, title, proposed scale, and why the display fits the scientific question.

4. Axes carry scientific meaning

In a controlled experiment, the deliberately changed independent variable normally belongs on the x-axis and the measured dependent variable on the y-axis. Axis labels need both variable names and units.

x: Rotor length (cm)
y: Mean fall time (s)
Title: Mean Paper-Helicopter Fall Time at Tested Rotor Lengths

A title should describe what was plotted, not announce a conclusion such as “Long Rotors Are Best.”

5. Scale, range, and visual integrity

A truncated axis is not automatically dishonest. Sometimes a narrow range is necessary to inspect small but meaningful differences. The problem occurs when scale choices hide context or create an impression disproportionate to the actual numerical difference.

Scale audit

Suppose two means are 1.50 s and 1.55 s. Compare how they might look on a y-axis from 0–2.0 s versus 1.48–1.57 s. Which display better shows the small difference? Which better communicates its size relative to zero? What context would you provide?

  • Use evenly spaced numerical intervals unless a specialized scale is explicitly identified and justified.
  • Avoid unexplained breaks in an axis.
  • Choose enough range to show the data clearly without wasting most of the plotting area.
  • Inspect whether the graph's proportions make tiny differences appear enormous.

6. Points, connecting lines, and trend lines

Connecting plotted points can imply that values between measured conditions are known or meaningfully continuous. A trend line summarizes a pattern; it does not transform association into proof of causation and does not guarantee predictions beyond the data.

Do not draw authority: software can fit a mathematically neat line to weak, sparse, or confounded data. A fitted line cannot repair poor experimental design.
Decision

For your helicopter graph, will you leave points unconnected, connect them, or add a trend representation? Defend the choice using what was actually measured.

7. Interpolation versus extrapolation

Interpolation estimates within the range of observed x-values. Extrapolation projects beyond that range. Extrapolation is generally riskier because the relationship may change outside the tested conditions.

Example: If you tested rotor lengths from 4–12 cm, estimating behavior near 8 cm is interpolation. Predicting a 40 cm rotor from the same pattern is extrapolation and may be physically unreasonable.
Boundary statement

Write the tested x-range for your investigation and one prediction your graph does not justify.

8. Interactive Graph Integrity Lab

Use your Day 06 dataset in the Dataset Lab or another approved graphing tool. Produce two legitimate visualizations using different defensible axis ranges. Compare how the visual impression changes while the numerical data remain identical.

  1. Verify all plotted values against the original table.
  2. Check title, axis labels, units, and scale intervals.
  3. Identify the strongest visible pattern.
  4. Identify one feature the graph does not show—for example, individual-trial variation if only means are plotted.
  5. Choose the version you would publish and explain why it communicates most honestly.

The tool creates the display. You remain responsible for the scientific choices and interpretation.

9. Graph forensics

Audit any scientific graph using this sequence:

  1. Source: Where did the data come from?
  2. Variables: What exactly is being compared?
  3. Units: Are they present and consistent?
  4. Scale: What range and intervals are used?
  5. Selection: Are relevant observations, dates, groups, or uncertainties omitted?
  6. Encoding: Do bar lengths, areas, icons, or lines visually represent the numbers faithfully?
  7. Claim: Does the caption/conclusion say more than the graph demonstrates?
Forensic challenge

Create one intentionally misleading version of a small practice graph by changing only presentation—not the underlying numbers. Label it clearly Demonstration of misleading design. Then repair it and explain the ethical problem you corrected.

10. Mentor visualization defense

Bring your final graph and alternate visualization to the Science Mentor. Be ready to defend:

  • why this graph type fits the variables;
  • why the axis range and interval are fair;
  • whether connecting points or fitting a trend is justified;
  • what information is lost by graphing means instead of all raw trials;
  • what claim a careless reader might make that your evidence does not support.

Mentor review supports revision. It does not replace the student's graph, source data, or mastery assessment.

11. Choose your pathway

PathwayRequired work

12. Exit check

  1. When is a bar chart preferable to a scatter plot?
  2. Why can a truncated axis be useful yet potentially misleading?
  3. Why is a missing observation not equivalent to zero?
  4. What claim can a trend line not establish by itself?
  5. How does extrapolation differ from interpolation?
  6. What should a reader inspect before trusting the visual impression of a graph?
