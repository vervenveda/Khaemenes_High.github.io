Unit 01 · Day 9

Claim, Evidence, Reasoning & Sources

Build scientific explanations that can survive scrutiny. Make a claim no broader than the question, select evidence because it is relevant and sufficient, connect it through scientific reasoning, and trace outside claims back toward their original evidence.

60–75 minutesTier 1 · Home-safeCERSource LiteracyEvidence Citation StudioPROSEPortfolio Evidence

Learning objectives

  • Distinguish claim, evidence, and reasoning and diagnose when one component is missing or mismatched.
  • Select relevant, sufficient, traceable evidence rather than cherry-picking only supportive observations.
  • Use scientific principles to explain why evidence supports, weakens, or fails to distinguish among claims.
  • Calibrate claim strength to uncertainty, limitations, scope, and alternative explanations.
  • Trace a science-related media claim toward the original study, dataset, agency report, or other primary evidence when available.
  • Evaluate source expertise, methods, transparency, conflicts, corroboration, and headline-to-evidence fidelity.
  • Revise a scientific explanation using Evidence Citation Studio, PROSE, and Mentor feedback without outsourcing scientific judgment.

Materials

  • Science notebook
  • Paper-helicopter data, graph, and limitations from Days 06–08
  • One age-appropriate science-related media claim supplied by the learner/teacher
  • CER planning space
  • Evidence Citation Studio and PROSE Editorial Suite through Science Studio when useful
Tier 1 · information-literacy activity

No physical experiment is required today. When researching online, protect personal information, avoid downloads or account creation that are not approved, and do not treat alarming health, environmental, or safety claims as instructions for personal action. Evaluate the evidence and consult appropriate adults/professionals when a real-world safety decision is involved.

1. CER is an evidence chain

  • Claim: a direct, appropriately scoped answer to the scientific question.
  • Evidence: relevant observations, measurements, patterns, or sourced findings that bear on the claim.
  • Reasoning: the scientific ideas and logical connection explaining why the evidence supports, weakens, or limits the claim.
Claim: Within the tested paper-helicopter designs, longer rotors were associated with longer mean fall time.
Evidence: Mean fall time increased from 1.2 s at 4 cm to 1.8 s at 8 cm, with [student inserts remaining conditions/variation].
Reasoning: Changing rotor geometry can change interaction with air and descent behavior; because rotor length was deliberately varied while specified construction/drop conditions were controlled, the observed difference provides evidence about rotor length under these test conditions. Remaining timing variation and uncontrolled factors limit how broadly the result should be generalized.
Scientific integrity: a plausible mechanism is not permission to invent details. If the unit has not established a specific aerodynamic mechanism with evidence, describe it cautiously and distinguish hypothesis from demonstrated result.

2. Evidence must be relevant, sufficient, and traceable

Strong evidence answers the actual question and can be traced back to observations or sources. One convenient number may be relevant but insufficient.

Evidence problemWhy it weakens the explanationRepair
Cherry-picking one favorable trialIgnores variation and contrary observationsUse the complete planned dataset and summarize variation
Reporting only meansCan hide spread, anomalies, or missing trialsKeep summaries traceable to raw data
Unrelated factMay be true but does not answer the questionExplain explicit relevance or remove it
Untraceable statisticReader cannot inspect origin/methodFind the original source or clearly label uncertainty
Evidence ranking

Select three pieces of evidence from your helicopter investigation. Rank them from most to least useful for your claim and justify the ranking.

3. Reasoning does more than repeat the evidence

“The mean increased, so the mean increased” is not reasoning. Reasoning connects the observed pattern to relevant scientific ideas and the study design.

Reasoning repair

Rewrite this weak sentence: “Longer rotors had longer times, therefore longer rotors caused longer times.” Include the experimental design, a cautious mechanism, and one limitation.

4. Counterevidence and alternative explanations belong in science

A strong explanation does not hide evidence that complicates the story. Address an anomalous observation, overlapping variation, protocol deviation, or plausible alternative explanation when it matters.

Challenge your own claim

What is the strongest piece of evidence or limitation that could weaken your preferred explanation? Does it require rejecting the claim, narrowing it, lowering confidence, or collecting more evidence?

5. Claim strength must match evidence strength

OverclaimBetter calibrated form
“Longer rotors always fall slower.”“Within the tested designs and conditions, longer rotor lengths were associated with longer mean fall times.”
“This proves our hypothesis.”“The results support the prediction under the tested conditions, with these limitations…”
“The graph proves X causes Y.”State whether the evidence came from manipulation or observation and identify remaining alternatives.

6. Source chain: headline → report → original evidence

Science reaches the public through layers. A social post may summarize a news story; the story may summarize a press release; the release may summarize a paper; the paper may rely on a dataset. Each layer can add simplification or distortion.

  1. Record the exact scientific claim you encountered.
  2. Identify who published the version you saw and its date.
  3. Follow citations/links toward the earliest accessible original evidence.
  4. Record the study/report title, responsible authors/organization, and publication venue or agency.
  5. Identify the study design, sample/data source, key measurement, and stated limitations if available.
  6. Compare the original evidence with the headline or media wording.
Do not reward inaccessible certainty: if you cannot reach the original evidence, say so. Do not pretend you verified a method you could not inspect.

7. Source evaluation matrix

QuestionWhat to inspect
ExpertiseAre the authors/organization appropriately qualified for this specific topic?
Method transparencyAre design, sample, measures, analysis, and limitations visible enough to evaluate?
Evidence proximityOriginal research/data, official synthesis, review, news summary, repost, or unsourced claim?
Conflicts/incentivesFunding, commercial interest, advocacy role, or other relevant interests disclosed?
CorroborationDo independent credible sources or replications broadly support the finding?
Recency/contextIs the information current enough for the claim, and has later evidence materially changed it?
Claim fidelityDoes the headline/conclusion preserve the scope and uncertainty of the evidence?

Conflict of interest does not automatically make evidence false; absence of a conflict does not make it true. It is one factor in evaluating transparency and possible bias.

8. Evidence Citation Studio

Use Evidence Citation Studio to create a traceable evidence record for the external claim. At minimum record the source identity, date, original evidence location if available, relevant finding, limitation, and how the source is being used in your explanation.

Source audit portfolio

Record the claim, original source if accessible, evidence provided, one methodological strength, one limitation, one relevant conflict/incentive disclosure or “not identified,” and whether the public wording matches the evidence.

9. PROSE scientific revision studio

Draft your CER first. Then use PROSE Editorial Suite to inspect clarity, organization, repetition, grammar, and wording. Do not accept an edit that changes the scientific meaning, strengthens certainty without evidence, removes an important limitation, or invents a source.

  1. Preserve your original draft.
  2. Review suggested edits one at a time.
  3. Accept, reject, or modify each substantive change.
  4. Recheck every number, unit, source, and scientific claim against the underlying evidence.
  5. Save a short revision note explaining one change that improved scientific precision.

10. Mentor evidence-defense conference

Bring the CER and source audit to the Science Mentor. Be prepared to answer:

  • Which sentence is your claim, and why is its scope justified?
  • Which evidence is strongest and why?
  • What scientific principle does your reasoning rely on?
  • What evidence would make you revise the claim?
  • Did you locate original evidence for the outside claim? If not, what remains unverified?
  • Which PROSE suggestion did you reject because it would have changed scientific meaning or certainty?

Mentor and ProTools assist reflection, citation, and revision. The student remains responsible for evidence selection, scientific meaning, and the ≥80% mastery requirement.

11. Choose your pathway

PathwayRequired work
FoundationWrite a complete CER from the helicopter investigation, identify one limitation, and complete a basic source audit.
CoreWrite and revise a calibrated CER, address counterevidence/alternative explanation, trace one media claim toward original evidence, and complete the full source matrix.
ExtendedCompare two independent sources addressing a related scientific claim; evaluate methodological differences, convergence/conflict, and whether a synthesis supports stronger, weaker, or differently scoped conclusions.

12. Exit check

  1. What makes evidence relevant but still insufficient?
  2. How is reasoning different from restating a result?
  3. Why should counterevidence be addressed rather than hidden?
  4. What is gained by tracing a news claim to original evidence?
  5. Why does a disclosed conflict of interest neither prove nor disprove a finding?
  6. What should you do if an editorial tool makes your conclusion sound more certain than the data justify?
Portfolio evidence
