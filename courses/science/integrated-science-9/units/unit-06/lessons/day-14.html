Unit 06 · Day 14

Periodic Patterns Data Laboratory

Test predictions against the first-20 element record and document exceptions.

45–60 minutesEvidence and modelsPortfolio evidence

Learning objectives

  • Extract periodic data
  • Identify group and period patterns
  • State limits and exceptions

Materials

  • Notebook or digital record
  • Periodic table or supplied dataset
  • Calculator where required
Chemistry and radiation safety

Do not handle reactive alkali metals, concentrated halogens, unknown salts, open flames, radioactive minerals, smoke-detector sources, or unknown electrical equipment. Use published data, safe simulations, or a qualified laboratory protocol.

Evidence and source standard

Use NIST, CIAAW, IUPAC, official curriculum sources, or a documented laboratory record. Distinguish a measured spectrum or isotope abundance from an instructional model.

Pattern protocol

Define the elements included, property measured, direction of the trend, and exceptions.

Prediction protocol

Use atomic number and outer-electron pattern before predicting family or common ion.

Integrity

Do not discard a value merely because it complicates a trend.

Portfolio evidence

Complete the Periodic Patterns Laboratory and one evidence-based element prediction.

Model boundary

Atom and shell diagrams are not literal scale drawings or photographs. They simplify size, distance, probability, and electron behavior.

Essential question and conceptual model

Essential question: How can a periodic-trends laboratory turn a visual pattern into a defensible evidence-based prediction?

A data laboratory should define the comparison before looking for a preferred trend. Identify the elements included, the property being examined, the units, and the direction of change. Then distinguish a broad pattern from exceptions. A useful prediction cites the pattern and states its range: a first-20 trend should not automatically be extrapolated to every element. Unexpected values are evidence to investigate, not points to delete because they make the graph less tidy.

Worked reasoning example

Suppose an unknown first-20 element has atomic number 12 and a shell configuration 2,8,2. Atomic number identifies magnesium. The two outer electrons place it with the Group 2 pattern in the introductory model, supporting a prediction that a +2 monatomic ion is common. The prediction is justified by periodic position and repeated group behavior; it does not mean magnesium must exist only as isolated Mg²⁺ ions in every substance.

Vocabulary and misconception checks

Vocabulary: trend · outlier · prediction · evidence range · periodicity · data integrity

Misconception check: An exception does not automatically disprove a useful trend, and a trend does not justify discarding values that do not fit it.

Retrieval and explanation practice

Answer from memory first. Then reopen the lesson, check each response, and write a correction wherever your explanation is incomplete or overstates the evidence.

  1. Write a trend claim that names the property, direction, and data range.
  2. Identify an outlier without deleting it and propose a reason to investigate.
  3. Use atomic number plus shell pattern to make one constrained prediction.
  4. Explain why extrapolating beyond the observed range increases uncertainty.

Mentor evidence conference

Use the Science Mentor to test your reasoning, not to obtain scored answers. Bring a written model, calculation, graph, or evidence statement and be ready to revise it.

  • Which trend in your laboratory is strongest, and how many data points support it?
  • Which exception matters most for interpreting the pattern?
  • How should your prediction be worded so it does not claim more than the dataset supports?
