Unit 06 · Day 6

Isotopes & Nuclear Notation

Distinguish element identity from isotope mass and notation.

45–60 minutesEvidence and modelsPortfolio evidence

Learning objectives

  • Define isotopes
  • Interpret nuclide notation
  • Explain why isotopes share general chemical behavior

Materials

  • Notebook or digital record
  • Periodic table or supplied dataset
  • Calculator where required
Chemistry and radiation safety

Do not handle reactive alkali metals, concentrated halogens, unknown salts, open flames, radioactive minerals, smoke-detector sources, or unknown electrical equipment. Use published data, safe simulations, or a qualified laboratory protocol.

Evidence and source standard

Use NIST, CIAAW, IUPAC, official curriculum sources, or a documented laboratory record. Distinguish a measured spectrum or isotope abundance from an instructional model.

Same element, different isotope

Isotopes have the same proton number but different neutron numbers. Their mass numbers differ.

Notation

In nuclide notation, mass number is written upper left and atomic number lower left. Ion charge is written upper right.

Chemical similarity

Neutral isotopes have the same electron number and arrangement, so their ordinary chemical behavior is generally very similar; mass-dependent effects can still occur.

Portfolio evidence

Interpret eight real isotope symbols and write proton, neutron, and electron counts.

Model boundary

Atom and shell diagrams are not literal scale drawings or photographs. They simplify size, distance, probability, and electron behavior.

Essential question and conceptual model

Essential question: How can two atoms be the same element yet have different masses?

Isotopes are atoms of the same element with the same proton number but different neutron numbers. Because element identity depends on proton number, changing neutron number does not create a new element. Neutral isotopes of the same element have the same electron number and broadly similar ordinary chemical behavior, although mass-dependent effects can occur. An ion and an isotope describe different kinds of variation: isotope notation tracks nuclear composition, while ionic charge tracks electron imbalance.

Worked reasoning example

Chlorine-35 and chlorine-37 both have atomic number 17, so each has 17 protons. Chlorine-35 has 18 neutrons; chlorine-37 has 20. Neutral atoms of both have 17 electrons. A chloride ion made from chlorine-37, ³⁷Cl⁻, still has 17 protons and 20 neutrons but has 18 electrons. It is simultaneously a particular isotope and an ion.

Vocabulary and misconception checks

Vocabulary: isotope · nuclide · mass number · atomic number · ion · isotopic composition

Misconception check: Isotopes are not different elements, and ions are not automatically different isotopes. Neutron changes and electron changes describe different properties.

Retrieval and explanation practice

Answer from memory first. Then reopen the lesson, check each response, and write a correction wherever your explanation is incomplete or overstates the evidence.

  1. Explain why carbon-12 and carbon-14 are both carbon.
  2. Distinguish an isotope difference from an ion-charge difference.
  3. Calculate neutron numbers for two isotopes with the same atomic number.
  4. Explain why isotope masses can affect some physical behavior even when ordinary chemistry is similar.

Mentor evidence conference

Use the Science Mentor to test your reasoning, not to obtain scored answers. Bring a written model, calculation, graph, or evidence statement and be ready to revise it.

  • What single particle count proves two nuclides belong to the same element?
  • How would you explain an atom that is both an isotope and an ion without mixing the two ideas?
  • Which properties would you expect to be most similar across neutral isotopes, and why?
