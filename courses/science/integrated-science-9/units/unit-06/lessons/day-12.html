Unit 06 · Day 12

Metals, Nonmetals & Metalloids

Connect periodic position with broad property patterns without erasing exceptions.

45–60 minutesEvidence and modelsPortfolio evidence

Learning objectives

  • Locate metals, nonmetals, and metalloids
  • Describe the broad change across a period
  • Use measured properties rather than category labels alone

Materials

  • Notebook or digital record
  • Periodic table or supplied dataset
  • Calculator where required
Chemistry and radiation safety

Do not handle reactive alkali metals, concentrated halogens, unknown salts, open flames, radioactive minerals, smoke-detector sources, or unknown electrical equipment. Use published data, safe simulations, or a qualified laboratory protocol.

Evidence and source standard

Use NIST, CIAAW, IUPAC, official curriculum sources, or a documented laboratory record. Distinguish a measured spectrum or isotope abundance from an instructional model.

Broad regions

Metals occupy much of the left and center, nonmetals the upper right, with metalloids near the boundary.

Across a period

Across Period 3, metallic character generally decreases. Property changes are gradual and not captured by one label.

Evidence requirement

Conductivity, luster, brittleness, state, and chemical behavior must be measured under stated conditions.

Portfolio evidence

Compare six Period 3 elements using at least three sourced properties and note exceptions.

Model boundary

Atom and shell diagrams are not literal scale drawings or photographs. They simplify size, distance, probability, and electron behavior.

Essential question and conceptual model

Essential question: How useful are the labels metal, nonmetal, and metalloid when real properties vary continuously and by condition?

The periodic table shows broad regions associated with metallic and nonmetallic behavior, but category labels summarize several properties rather than defining one single measurement. Metals often conduct electricity and heat well and can be malleable; nonmetals show different combinations of conductivity, brittleness, molecular structure, or physical state. Metalloids are used in many instructional classifications for elements near a boundary with mixed properties, but exact membership can vary by source. Measured properties under stated conditions are stronger evidence than a label alone.

Worked reasoning example

Across Period 3, sodium, magnesium, and aluminum show metallic behavior; silicon has mixed semiconductor-like behavior; phosphorus, sulfur, chlorine, and argon are commonly classified toward the nonmetal side. The change is not a single abrupt switch in every property. If the question is electrical conductivity, use conductivity evidence. If the question is brittleness or bonding, use evidence relevant to that property.

Vocabulary and misconception checks

Vocabulary: metal · nonmetal · metalloid · conductivity · malleability · property trend

Misconception check: A category label does not mean every member has identical properties, and the metalloid boundary is not a fundamental line physically drawn through the periodic table.

Retrieval and explanation practice

Answer from memory first. Then reopen the lesson, check each response, and write a correction wherever your explanation is incomplete or overstates the evidence.

  1. Name three properties that could be measured instead of relying only on a metal/nonmetal label.
  2. Explain why a gradual property trend can cross a category boundary.
  3. Compare one metal and one nonmetal using evidence rather than stereotypes.
  4. Explain why source and measurement conditions matter when comparing properties.

Mentor evidence conference

Use the Science Mentor to test your reasoning, not to obtain scored answers. Bring a written model, calculation, graph, or evidence statement and be ready to revise it.

  • Which property actually supports the classification you used?
  • Where does a broad category hide important variation?
  • What measurement would most directly test your prediction about an unfamiliar element?
