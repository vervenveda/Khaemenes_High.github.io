Unit 06 · Day 2

How Atomic Models Changed

Trace how evidence revised Dalton, Thomson, Rutherford, Bohr, and quantum models.

45–60 minutesEvidence and modelsPortfolio evidence

Learning objectives

  • Place major model revisions in evidence-based sequence
  • Explain what cathode-ray, scattering, and spectral evidence contributed
  • Identify strengths and limits of historical models

Materials

  • Notebook or digital record
  • Periodic table or supplied dataset
  • Calculator where required
Chemistry and radiation safety

Do not handle reactive alkali metals, concentrated halogens, unknown salts, open flames, radioactive minerals, smoke-detector sources, or unknown electrical equipment. Use published data, safe simulations, or a qualified laboratory protocol.

Evidence and source standard

Use NIST, CIAAW, IUPAC, official curriculum sources, or a documented laboratory record. Distinguish a measured spectrum or isotope abundance from an instructional model.

Dalton and chemical regularity

Dalton’s model helped explain fixed composition and whole-number combining patterns, but the atom was later shown to have internal structure.

Thomson and Rutherford

Cathode-ray deflection supported negatively charged particles. Rutherford scattering supported a tiny, dense, positively charged nucleus and mostly empty space at the scale of the experiment.

Bohr and later models

Bohr’s quantized levels explained key hydrogen patterns. Modern quantum models retain quantized energy but do not assign electrons exact planetary paths.

Portfolio evidence

Create a five-column timeline: model, evidence, claim supported, limitation, and later revision.

Model boundary

Atom and shell diagrams are not literal scale drawings or photographs. They simplify size, distance, probability, and electron behavior.

Essential question and conceptual model

Essential question: Why did atomic models change, and what does each major experiment actually justify?

Scientific models change when new evidence cannot be explained adequately by an older model. Dalton's model organized chemical regularities but did not include internal atomic structure. Cathode-ray evidence supported negatively charged particles smaller than atoms. Rutherford-style scattering showed that positive charge and most atomic mass are concentrated in a very small nucleus while most of the atom's volume is comparatively empty. Line spectra supported quantized energy differences. Modern quantum models replace fixed planetary electron paths with probability-based descriptions of allowed states.

Worked reasoning example

In a scattering experiment, most positively charged projectiles pass through a thin foil with little deflection, while a very small fraction are deflected strongly. The first observation is consistent with most atomic volume not containing concentrated positive charge. The rare large deflections require a compact region capable of exerting a strong repulsive interaction. This supports a small dense positive nucleus. It does not, by itself, show electrons moving in circular planetary orbits.

Vocabulary and misconception checks

Vocabulary: atomic model · cathode ray · nucleus · scattering · line spectrum · quantized energy

Misconception check: A newer model does not make every older model useless. A simplified model can remain useful within a limited purpose even when it is not the most complete description.

Retrieval and explanation practice

Answer from memory first. Then reopen the lesson, check each response, and write a correction wherever your explanation is incomplete or overstates the evidence.

  1. Match Dalton, Thomson, Rutherford, Bohr, and modern quantum models to the evidence that forced or supported revision.
  2. Explain why Rutherford scattering supports a nucleus but not a planetary electron orbit.
  3. Distinguish a model's useful prediction from a claim that the model is literally what an atom looks like.
  4. Identify one question the Bohr model helps answer and one it cannot answer well.

Mentor evidence conference

Use the Science Mentor to test your reasoning, not to obtain scored answers. Bring a written model, calculation, graph, or evidence statement and be ready to revise it.

  • Which historical conclusion is strongest because it follows directly from the evidence?
  • Where would you be overclaiming if you treated one experiment as proof of the entire modern atomic model?
  • How can an older model be both scientifically limited and instructionally useful?
