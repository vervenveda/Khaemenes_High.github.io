Unit 06 · Day 3

Protons, Neutrons & Electrons

Use charge, relative mass, and location to describe atomic substructure.

45–60 minutesEvidence and modelsPortfolio evidence

Learning objectives

  • State relative charge and mass of each subatomic particle
  • Explain why nuclei contain nearly all atomic mass
  • Use proton count to identify elements

Materials

  • Notebook or digital record
  • Periodic table or supplied dataset
  • Calculator where required
Chemistry and radiation safety

Do not handle reactive alkali metals, concentrated halogens, unknown salts, open flames, radioactive minerals, smoke-detector sources, or unknown electrical equipment. Use published data, safe simulations, or a qualified laboratory protocol.

Evidence and source standard

Use NIST, CIAAW, IUPAC, official curriculum sources, or a documented laboratory record. Distinguish a measured spectrum or isotope abundance from an instructional model.

Particle properties

Protons have relative charge +1 and mass about 1. Neutrons have charge 0 and mass about 1. Electrons have charge −1 and much smaller mass.

Identity and mass

Atomic number is proton number. Mass number is the total number of protons and neutrons in one isotope.

Scale warning

Diagrams usually make nuclei and particles far too large and distances far too small. They are not scale drawings.

Portfolio evidence

Complete a particle comparison and explain why changing a neutron can change isotope but changing a proton changes element identity.

Model boundary

Atom and shell diagrams are not literal scale drawings or photographs. They simplify size, distance, probability, and electron behavior.

Essential question and conceptual model

Essential question: Which subatomic particles determine element identity, mass number, and net charge?

Protons, neutrons, and electrons contribute differently to atomic identity and observable behavior. Proton number defines the element. Protons and neutrons account for nearly all ordinary atomic mass, while electrons contribute very little mass but strongly influence chemical interactions. In a neutral atom, electron number equals proton number. Changing neutron number creates an isotope of the same element. Changing electron number creates an ion. Ordinary chemical reactions rearrange electrons and atoms; they do not normally change one element into another.

Worked reasoning example

For sodium-23, atomic number 11 means 11 protons. Mass number 23 means protons + neutrons = 23, so neutrons = 23 - 11 = 12. A neutral sodium-23 atom has 11 electrons. If it loses one electron, it still has 11 protons and 12 neutrons but now has 10 electrons and a +1 charge. The element remains sodium because proton number did not change.

Vocabulary and misconception checks

Vocabulary: proton · neutron · electron · nucleus · atomic number · mass number

Misconception check: Electron count does not determine which element an atom is. Proton number does. Losing an electron changes charge, not elemental identity.

Retrieval and explanation practice

Answer from memory first. Then reopen the lesson, check each response, and write a correction wherever your explanation is incomplete or overstates the evidence.

  1. Explain why atomic number identifies an element.
  2. Calculate neutron number for an isotope when atomic number and mass number are given.
  3. Explain why nearly all atomic mass is in the nucleus.
  4. Describe what changes—and what does not—when a neutral atom becomes an ion.

Mentor evidence conference

Use the Science Mentor to test your reasoning, not to obtain scored answers. Bring a written model, calculation, graph, or evidence statement and be ready to revise it.

  • Which particle count would you check first if you were asked to identify an unknown element?
  • How would you prove from particle counts that two atoms are isotopes rather than different elements?
  • What common diagram feature could exaggerate the physical size of electrons or the nucleus?
