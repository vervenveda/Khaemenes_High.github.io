Unit 06 · Day 5

Atomic Composition Laboratory

Apply identity, mass-number, charge, notation, and model-limit reasoning.

45–60 minutesEvidence and modelsPortfolio evidence

Learning objectives

  • Determine proton, neutron, and electron counts
  • Identify elements and ions
  • Evaluate atom-diagram limitations

Materials

  • Notebook or digital record
  • Periodic table or supplied dataset
  • Calculator where required
Chemistry and radiation safety

Do not handle reactive alkali metals, concentrated halogens, unknown salts, open flames, radioactive minerals, smoke-detector sources, or unknown electrical equipment. Use published data, safe simulations, or a qualified laboratory protocol.

Evidence and source standard

Use NIST, CIAAW, IUPAC, official curriculum sources, or a documented laboratory record. Distinguish a measured spectrum or isotope abundance from an instructional model.

Laboratory standard

Every answer must show which quantity came from atomic number, mass number, or charge.

Evidence record

Record equations and units rather than guessing from symbol shape.

Mastery

A score of 7/8 demonstrates laboratory mastery; corrections and retakes remain available.

Portfolio evidence

Complete the Atomic Composition Laboratory and attach corrections for any missed item.

Model boundary

Atom and shell diagrams are not literal scale drawings or photographs. They simplify size, distance, probability, and electron behavior.

Essential question and conceptual model

Essential question: How can a laboratory record show atomic-composition reasoning rather than guessed answers?

A strong atomic-composition laboratory record makes the reasoning auditable. Each answer should identify the source quantity—atomic number, mass number, or charge—and then show the calculation used. This matters because a visually plausible diagram can still contain the wrong number of particles. Error analysis is part of the laboratory: a correction should name the mistaken rule, recompute the value, and explain why the corrected composition is consistent.

Worked reasoning example

Suppose a card shows ²⁷₁₃Al³⁺. Atomic number gives 13 protons. Neutrons = 27 - 13 = 14. A +3 ion must have three fewer electrons than protons, so electrons = 10. If a diagram shows 13 electrons, the diagram actually represents neutral aluminum, not Al³⁺. The correction is not “change three dots” only; it must state the charge-accounting rule that was violated.

Vocabulary and misconception checks

Vocabulary: evidence record · consistency check · ion · isotope · particle count · correction

Misconception check: Getting the final number right without showing which relationship was used is not strong laboratory evidence because the reasoning cannot be audited.

Retrieval and explanation practice

Answer from memory first. Then reopen the lesson, check each response, and write a correction wherever your explanation is incomplete or overstates the evidence.

  1. Explain how to verify an ion diagram using proton and electron counts.
  2. Describe the difference between a wrong arithmetic step and a wrong scientific rule.
  3. Write a correction note for a fictional atom card whose electron count does not match its charge.
  4. State one limitation of a shell-style atom diagram.

Mentor evidence conference

Use the Science Mentor to test your reasoning, not to obtain scored answers. Bring a written model, calculation, graph, or evidence statement and be ready to revise it.

  • Which laboratory answer is least secure, and what check would make it auditable?
  • Did any correction change only arithmetic, or did it reveal a conceptual misunderstanding?
  • How can you distinguish a drawing error from a particle-accounting error?
