Unit 09 · Day 5

Low-Voltage Circuit Data Laboratory

Use measured or labeled practice records to test circuit relationships.

45–60 minutesEvidence & modelsLow-voltage safety

Learning objectives

  • Collect voltage and current safely
  • Calculate resistance and electrical power
  • Graph I–V data and evaluate uncertainty

Materials

  • Notebook or digital record
  • Calculator and supplied reference tables
  • Optional approved low-voltage circuit kit with adult supervision
Electrical, sound, light, and magnet safety

Use only approved battery-powered circuits of 6 V or less with current limiting. Never access wall outlets, mains wiring, chargers, power supplies, internal appliance parts, or damaged batteries. Do not short batteries, heat wires, use laser pointers, create loud headphone or speaker exposure, place strong magnets near medical implants, or build improvised radio transmitters.

Evidence standard

Separate measured values, published records, calculations, circuit and wave models, digital simulations, and engineering inferences. State units, conditions, uncertainty, system boundary, and signal assumptions.

Hands-on option

Use only an approved low-voltage battery pack, current-limiting resistors, insulated leads, and adult supervision.

Practice option

The application provides clearly labeled practice values that are not experimental evidence.

Evaluation

Consider meter resolution, loose contacts, source internal resistance, component heating, and repeated trials.

Portfolio evidence

Complete the Circuit Laboratory and its 8-question check.

Model boundary

Circuit diagrams, field lines, ray diagrams, sinusoidal waves, photons, binary channels, and ideal transformers are purposeful simplifications. They do not show literal scale, all microscopic interactions, every loss mechanism, or complete quantum behavior.

Essential question and conceptual model

Essential question: How can low-voltage circuit data be used to test electrical relationships without overstating precision?

A safe circuit investigation should use approved low-voltage equipment or supplied datasets. Record raw current and voltage readings before processing them. Repeated measurements, meter resolution, connection quality, and component heating can all affect results. A graph can reveal proportionality or changing resistance, but a strong conclusion states the observed range and uncertainty.

Worked reasoning example

Suppose a component gives (V,I) pairs of (2.0 V, 0.40 A), (4.0 V, 0.81 A), and (6.0 V, 1.18 A). Calculated resistances are about 5.0 Ω, 4.94 Ω, and 5.08 Ω. Small variation may reflect measurement uncertainty rather than a meaningful resistance change.

Vocabulary and misconception checks

Vocabulary: raw data · meter resolution · uncertainty · repeat measurement · I–V evidence · processed data

Misconception check: A slightly different calculated resistance at each point does not automatically prove the component is non-ohmic.

Retrieval and explanation practice

Answer from memory first. Then check calculations, units, diagrams, model limits, and any claim that is broader than the evidence supports.

  1. Separate raw circuit readings from calculated quantities.
  2. Calculate resistance for several I–V points.
  3. Identify two sources of measurement uncertainty.
  4. Explain when scatter is too small to support a strong trend claim.

Mentor evidence conference

Bring your circuit model, graph, wave calculation, or communication-system explanation to the Science Mentor. The mentor may question assumptions and reasoning but does not provide protected assessment answers.

  • How large is the observed variation compared with likely meter uncertainty?
  • Which point most needs a repeat measurement?
  • What evidence would justify calling the component non-ohmic?
