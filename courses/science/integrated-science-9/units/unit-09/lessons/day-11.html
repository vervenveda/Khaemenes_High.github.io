Unit 09 · Day 11

Sound: Vibrations, Frequency, Amplitude & Hearing

Explain sound as a mechanical wave and interpret safe sound evidence.

45–60 minutesEvidence & modelsLow-voltage safety

Learning objectives

  • Relate frequency to pitch and amplitude/intensity to loudness cautiously
  • Explain sound transmission through matter
  • Use decibel information without unsafe exposure

Materials

  • Notebook or digital record
  • Calculator and supplied reference tables
  • Optional approved low-voltage circuit kit with adult supervision
Electrical, sound, light, and magnet safety

Use only approved battery-powered circuits of 6 V or less with current limiting. Never access wall outlets, mains wiring, chargers, power supplies, internal appliance parts, or damaged batteries. Do not short batteries, heat wires, use laser pointers, create loud headphone or speaker exposure, place strong magnets near medical implants, or build improvised radio transmitters.

Evidence standard

Separate measured values, published records, calculations, circuit and wave models, digital simulations, and engineering inferences. State units, conditions, uncertainty, system boundary, and signal assumptions.

Mechanical wave

Sound cannot travel through a vacuum.

Sound requires a material medium and cannot travel through a vacuum.

Perception

Pitch generally tracks frequency; loudness perception depends on intensity, frequency, duration, distance, and the listener.

Safety

No loudspeaker or headphone exposure is required. Published safety guidance is analyzed instead.

Portfolio evidence

Analyze a sound record and prepare a hearing-safety explanation.

Model boundary

Circuit diagrams, field lines, ray diagrams, sinusoidal waves, photons, binary channels, and ideal transformers are purposeful simplifications. They do not show literal scale, all microscopic interactions, every loss mechanism, or complete quantum behavior.

Essential question and conceptual model

Essential question: How do vibrating sources, wave frequency, amplitude, and the hearing system connect without reducing sound to 'loud' or 'high' alone?

Sound is a mechanical wave requiring a medium. Frequency is related to perceived pitch, though perception is not a one-to-one physical measurement. Wave amplitude and intensity are related to perceived loudness, but human hearing is frequency-dependent and logarithmic measures such as decibels require care. Safe-exposure guidance depends on both sound level and duration.

Worked reasoning example

A 500 Hz tone has period T = 1/500 = 0.002 s. A 1000 Hz tone has half that period. If both travel in air under the same conditions, the higher-frequency tone has shorter wavelength because wave speed is approximately fixed by the medium.

Vocabulary and misconception checks

Vocabulary: sound wave · frequency · pitch · amplitude · intensity · decibel

Misconception check: Pitch is not simply 'how loud' a sound is, and a decibel value should not be treated as a linear measure of acoustic power.

Retrieval and explanation practice

Answer from memory first. Then check calculations, units, diagrams, model limits, and any claim that is broader than the evidence supports.

  1. Calculate period from an audible frequency.
  2. Explain how frequency relates to pitch and amplitude/intensity relates to loudness.
  3. State why sound cannot travel through a perfect vacuum in the mechanical-wave model.
  4. Explain why exposure duration matters in hearing-safety guidance.

Mentor evidence conference

Bring your circuit model, graph, wave calculation, or communication-system explanation to the Science Mentor. The mentor may question assumptions and reasoning but does not provide protected assessment answers.

  • Which claim is physical measurement and which involves human perception?
  • How does a logarithmic level scale complicate simple comparisons?
  • What safety statement can you make without diagnosing an individual's hearing?
