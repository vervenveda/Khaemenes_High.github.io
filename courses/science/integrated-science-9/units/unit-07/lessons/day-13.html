Unit 07 · Day 13

Reaction Rates & Collision Theory

Investigate how conditions change reaction rate.

45–60 minutesReal evidenceModel limits

Learning objectives

  • Analyze effects of temperature, concentration, pressure, surface area, and catalysts
  • Use collision theory to explain trends
  • Design a fair rate investigation

Materials

  • Notebook or digital record
  • Periodic table and supplied reference data
  • Calculator where required
Chemical safety boundary

Wear properly fitting splash goggles for any approved hands-on activity. Never mix household cleaners, heat sealed containers, taste substances, burn materials, handle strong acids or bases, or use unknown chemicals. The effervescent-tablet option requires adult approval, water only, open containers, and careful handling of warm—not boiling—water.

Evidence standard

Separate measured properties, observed changes, calculated atom counts, published thermochemical values, and explanatory models. A simulation result is not independent experimental evidence.

Rate evidence

Rate can be measured through gas volume, mass change, color intensity, turbidity, or concentration over time.

Collision theory

Conditions affect collision frequency and the fraction of collisions with sufficient energy and suitable orientation.

Real investigation

The optional ACS-style effervescent-tablet investigation uses student-measured time rather than invented results.

Portfolio evidence

Collect or analyze rate data, graph the results, and evaluate uncertainty.

Model boundary

Dot-and-cross, ball-and-stick, lattice, particle-collision, and energy-pathway diagrams are useful representations. They distort scale, motion, electron density, and complexity.

Essential question and conceptual model

Essential question: How do temperature, concentration, pressure, surface area, and catalysts affect reaction rate through collision reasoning?

Reaction rate depends on how often reacting particles encounter one another and how many encounters have sufficient energy and suitable configuration to react. Higher temperature increases particle kinetic-energy distribution; higher concentration or gas pressure can increase collision frequency; greater solid surface area exposes more particles; catalysts change the pathway. Collision theory is a useful model with limits.

Worked reasoning example

A reaction produces 60 cm³ of gas in 30 s. Average rate over that interval = 2.0 cm³/s. If another trial produces the same 60 cm³ in 15 s, average rate = 4.0 cm³/s. Faster rate does not mean more total product if both trials end at the same amount.

Vocabulary and misconception checks

Vocabulary: reaction rate · collision theory · concentration · surface area · catalyst · effective collision

Misconception check: A faster reaction does not necessarily produce more final product; rate and yield are different quantities.

Retrieval and explanation practice

Answer from memory first. Then check formulas, charge balance, atom counts, units, energy direction, evidence quality, and the limits of each model.

  1. Calculate average reaction rate from change/time.
  2. Predict how changing one condition affects rate and explain why.
  3. Distinguish rate from extent/yield.
  4. Identify a controlled variable in a rate experiment.

Mentor evidence conference

Bring your structure model, formula, balanced equation, rate calculation, or equilibrium explanation to the Science Mentor. The mentor may challenge your reasoning but does not provide protected assessment answers.

  • Which collision variable changes in your explanation?
  • Did the intervention affect rate, yield, or both?
  • What alternative variable must be controlled?
