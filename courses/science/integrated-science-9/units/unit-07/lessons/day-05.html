Unit 07 · Day 5

Bond Polarity & Molecular Properties

Use electronegativity difference and molecular arrangement to reason about uneven electron distribution.

45–60 minutesReal evidenceModel limits

Learning objectives

  • Define bond polarity qualitatively
  • Use electronegativity difference as evidence
  • Distinguish polar bonds from whole-molecule polarity

Materials

  • Notebook or digital record
  • Periodic table and supplied reference data
  • Calculator where required
Chemical safety boundary

Wear properly fitting splash goggles for any approved hands-on activity. Never mix household cleaners, heat sealed containers, taste substances, burn materials, handle strong acids or bases, or use unknown chemicals. The effervescent-tablet option requires adult approval, water only, open containers, and careful handling of warm—not boiling—water.

Evidence standard

Separate measured properties, observed changes, calculated atom counts, published thermochemical values, and explanatory models. A simulation result is not independent experimental evidence.

Unequal sharing

When bonded atoms attract electron density differently, partial charges and a bond dipole can result.

Electronegativity

Electronegativity difference is a useful model for bond polarity, but boundaries between categories are conventional.

Molecular shape

Bond dipoles can reinforce or cancel depending on arrangement. Carbon dioxide has polar bonds but no net dipole in its linear model.

Portfolio evidence

Compare H–H, C–H, H–Cl, O–H, and C–O bonds and explain one molecular-polarity example.

Model boundary

Dot-and-cross, ball-and-stick, lattice, particle-collision, and energy-pathway diagrams are useful representations. They distort scale, motion, electron density, and complexity.

Essential question and conceptual model

Essential question: How can bond polarity and molecular geometry combine to influence overall molecular polarity?

Differences in electronegativity can produce polar bonds with unequal electron distribution. Overall molecular polarity depends on both bond polarity and molecular arrangement. Symmetry can allow bond dipoles to cancel, while asymmetric geometry can produce a net molecular dipole.

Worked reasoning example

CO₂ contains polar C=O bonds, but its linear symmetric arrangement causes the bond dipoles to cancel, so the molecule is nonpolar overall. H₂O has polar O–H bonds and a bent geometry, so the dipoles do not cancel and the molecule is polar.

Vocabulary and misconception checks

Vocabulary: electronegativity · bond polarity · dipole · molecular geometry · symmetry · molecular polarity

Misconception check: A molecule with polar bonds is not automatically polar overall.

Retrieval and explanation practice

Answer from memory first. Then check formulas, charge balance, atom counts, units, energy direction, evidence quality, and the limits of each model.

  1. Predict whether bond dipoles cancel in a symmetric molecule.
  2. Explain why H₂O and CO₂ differ in overall polarity.
  3. Distinguish bond polarity from molecular polarity.
  4. State one limitation of using electronegativity difference alone.

Mentor evidence conference

Bring your structure model, formula, balanced equation, rate calculation, or equilibrium explanation to the Science Mentor. The mentor may challenge your reasoning but does not provide protected assessment answers.

  • Which part of your conclusion comes from bond polarity and which from shape?
  • Could a symmetric geometry change the net result?
  • What model information is missing if you only know the molecular formula?
