Unit 07 · Day 8

Word Equations, Symbol Equations & State Symbols

Translate reaction descriptions into chemical representations.

45–60 minutesReal evidenceModel limits

Learning objectives

  • Identify reactants and products
  • Use state symbols accurately
  • Construct word and symbol equations from supplied information

Materials

  • Notebook or digital record
  • Periodic table and supplied reference data
  • Calculator where required
Chemical safety boundary

Wear properly fitting splash goggles for any approved hands-on activity. Never mix household cleaners, heat sealed containers, taste substances, burn materials, handle strong acids or bases, or use unknown chemicals. The effervescent-tablet option requires adult approval, water only, open containers, and careful handling of warm—not boiling—water.

Evidence standard

Separate measured properties, observed changes, calculated atom counts, published thermochemical values, and explanatory models. A simulation result is not independent experimental evidence.

Representation levels

Words identify substances; formulas identify composition; coefficients show reacting ratios; state symbols describe physical state.

State symbols

Use (s), (l), (g), and (aq). Aqueous means dissolved in water, not merely liquid.

Evidence

A valid equation must match the substances actually involved and conserve each atom type.

Portfolio evidence

Translate five documented reactions through word, formula, balanced, and particle-model forms.

Model boundary

Dot-and-cross, ball-and-stick, lattice, particle-collision, and energy-pathway diagrams are useful representations. They distort scale, motion, electron density, and complexity.

Essential question and conceptual model

Essential question: How do word equations, symbol equations, and state symbols represent the same chemical change at different levels?

Word equations identify substances. Symbol equations use chemical formulas and can be balanced to show atom conservation. State symbols—(s), (l), (g), and (aq)—provide physical-state information relevant to interpretation. Translating among representations requires correct formulas before balancing begins.

Worked reasoning example

Word equation: magnesium + oxygen → magnesium oxide. Formula skeleton: Mg + O₂ → MgO. Balanced equation: 2Mg(s) + O₂(g) → 2MgO(s). Coefficients change amounts; subscripts in formulas remain fixed because changing them changes the substance.

Vocabulary and misconception checks

Vocabulary: word equation · symbol equation · coefficient · state symbol · aqueous · reactant

Misconception check: Balancing an equation does not permit changing subscripts inside correct formulas.

Retrieval and explanation practice

Answer from memory first. Then check formulas, charge balance, atom counts, units, energy direction, evidence quality, and the limits of each model.

  1. Translate a word equation into formulas.
  2. Add appropriate state symbols from supplied information.
  3. Explain the difference between coefficient and subscript.
  4. Identify whether an equation is chemically meaningful before balancing.

Mentor evidence conference

Bring your structure model, formula, balanced equation, rate calculation, or equilibrium explanation to the Science Mentor. The mentor may challenge your reasoning but does not provide protected assessment answers.

  • Is your formula correct before you start balancing?
  • What information does the state symbol add?
  • Did you accidentally change the identity of a substance while balancing?
