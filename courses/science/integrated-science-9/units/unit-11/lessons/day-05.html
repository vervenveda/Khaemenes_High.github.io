Unit 11 · Day 5

Solar-System Scale & Orbit Laboratory

Calculate light-time, scaled distance, orbital speed, and data comparisons using transparent assumptions.

45–60 minutesModels & cosmic evidenceObservation safety

Learning objectives

  • Use t=d/c and v=2πr/T
  • Compare rocky, giant, dwarf, moon, asteroid, and comet records
  • Evaluate circular-orbit and scale-model limitations

Materials

  • Notebook or digital record
  • Calculator, supplied tables, spectra, and light curves
  • Optional night-sky observations only from permitted stable locations with adult approval
Astronomy and observation safety

Never look directly at the Sun or through binoculars, telescopes, cameras, viewfinders, magnifiers, or improvised filters unless a certified solar system is used exactly as designed under qualified supervision. Do not observe alone from roofs, roadsides, restricted land, unstable terrain, or severe weather. Do not use lasers, launch devices, high-voltage equipment, or improvised radio transmitters.

Evidence standard

Label raw data, calibrated data, composite image, false-color image, simulation, model, catalog value, inference, projection, artist concept, and public claim accurately. State wavelength band, scale, time, instrument, processing, uncertainty, and whether the signal is direct or indirect.

Practice data

The application provides editable or labeled practice records rather than live spacecraft data.

Model choice

Circular-orbit calculations are useful averages but do not describe changing speed around an ellipse.

Scale ethics

A visual scale must state whether radius, diameter, distance, or time has been compressed.

Portfolio evidence

Complete the Solar-System Laboratory and its 8-question check.

Model boundary

Celestial spheres, orbit diagrams, scale models, color–temperature relations, stellar tracks, transit curves, galaxy classifications, and expansion graphs simplify three-dimensional geometry, selection effects, calibration, relativity, and uncertainty.

Essential question and conceptual model

Essential question: How can an orbital laboratory turn scale, period, and light-time into transparent calculations rather than decorative numbers?

Astronomical calculations should state the quantity, unit, formula, and approximation. Scale models require one consistent scale factor. Orbital-average speed can be approximated as circumference divided by period for nearly circular paths. Light-time calculations use distance divided by light speed. Uncertainty enters through rounded distances, noncircular orbits, and simplified geometry.

Worked reasoning example

Using a circular approximation for Earth: orbital circumference ≈ 2π(1 AU). With 1 AU ≈ 1.50 × 10^11 m and period ≈ 3.156 × 10^7 s, average orbital speed ≈ 2π(1.50 × 10^11)/3.156 × 10^7 ≈ 3.0 × 10^4 m/s, or about 30 km/s.

Vocabulary and misconception checks

Vocabulary: scale factor · orbital period · average orbital speed · light-time · approximation · significant figures

Misconception check: A scale model that scales distances but not object sizes is not a fully to-scale physical replica unless that limitation is stated.

Retrieval and explanation practice

Answer from memory first. Then check units, observation frames, model assumptions, uncertainty, and whether each conclusion is directly observed or inferred from remote evidence.

  1. Calculate light-time from a supplied distance.
  2. Calculate average orbital speed from circumference and period.
  3. Apply a single scale factor to two distances.
  4. State one limitation of a circular-orbit approximation.

Mentor evidence conference

Bring your calculation, spectrum, orbital model, or evidence claim to the Science Mentor. The mentor may challenge your assumptions and scope but does not provide protected assessment answers.

  • Which approximation contributes most to your result?
  • Are all units compatible before calculation?
  • How can you check that your order of magnitude is reasonable?
