Unit 11 · Day 4

Gravity, Orbits & Conservation

Model orbital motion as continual free fall under gravity and connect distance, speed, period, and energy.

45–60 minutesModels & cosmic evidenceObservation safety

Learning objectives

  • Explain why gravity provides centripetal acceleration
  • Calculate average orbital speed for a circular approximation
  • Explain why elliptical-orbit speed changes while total mechanical energy is conserved

Materials

  • Notebook or digital record
  • Calculator, supplied tables, spectra, and light curves
  • Optional night-sky observations only from permitted stable locations with adult approval
Astronomy and observation safety

Never look directly at the Sun or through binoculars, telescopes, cameras, viewfinders, magnifiers, or improvised filters unless a certified solar system is used exactly as designed under qualified supervision. Do not observe alone from roofs, roadsides, restricted land, unstable terrain, or severe weather. Do not use lasers, launch devices, high-voltage equipment, or improvised radio transmitters.

Evidence standard

Label raw data, calibrated data, composite image, false-color image, simulation, model, catalog value, inference, projection, artist concept, and public claim accurately. State wavelength band, scale, time, instrument, processing, uncertainty, and whether the signal is direct or indirect.

Orbit

An orbiting object continually falls around the central body rather than escaping tangentially or falling straight down.

Circular approximation

Average orbital speed may be estimated from circumference divided by period when the path is treated as circular.

Ellipses

The central body lies at a focus, and an object generally moves faster when closer because gravitational potential and kinetic energy exchange.

Portfolio evidence

Calculate two average orbital speeds and annotate force, velocity, and acceleration directions.

Model boundary

Celestial spheres, orbit diagrams, scale models, color–temperature relations, stellar tracks, transit curves, galaxy classifications, and expansion graphs simplify three-dimensional geometry, selection effects, calibration, relativity, and uncertainty.

Essential question and conceptual model

Essential question: How can gravity produce orbital motion without requiring a continuous forward engine?

An orbit is continual free fall in which gravity provides inward acceleration while the object also has sideways velocity. In a circular approximation, speed changes direction continuously even if its magnitude is nearly constant. Elliptical orbits involve changing speed and distance, with exchanges between kinetic and gravitational potential energy. Orbit models depend on the masses involved and the chosen approximation.

Worked reasoning example

A satellite moving sideways fast enough continually falls toward Earth while Earth's surface curves away beneath it. If it had too little tangential speed, its path would intersect Earth; with appropriate speed it remains in orbit. The gravitational force points inward rather than forward along the path.

Vocabulary and misconception checks

Vocabulary: orbit · gravity · centripetal acceleration · tangential velocity · ellipse · orbital energy

Misconception check: Astronauts in orbit are not beyond gravity; they are in continuous free fall.

Retrieval and explanation practice

Answer from memory first. Then check units, observation frames, model assumptions, uncertainty, and whether each conclusion is directly observed or inferred from remote evidence.

  1. Explain why orbital velocity can change direction without a forward thrust.
  2. Distinguish circular from elliptical orbit behavior.
  3. Explain what happens qualitatively if orbital speed is too low.
  4. Identify the force that provides inward acceleration.

Mentor evidence conference

  • Have you confused weightlessness with absence of gravity?
  • What model assumption is hidden in a perfectly circular orbit?
