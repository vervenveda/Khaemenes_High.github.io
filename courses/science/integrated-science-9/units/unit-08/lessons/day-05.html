Unit 08 · Day 5

Motion Data Laboratory

Collect or analyze time-position evidence without inventing observations.

45–60 minutesMeasurement & modelsPortfolio evidence

Learning objectives

  • Record repeated position and time measurements
  • Calculate interval and overall motion quantities
  • Evaluate precision, reaction time, and graph limitations

Materials

  • Notebook or digital record
  • Calculator and ruler
  • Optional low-speed tabletop or walking measurements with adult approval
Motion and impact safety

Use clear indoor or enclosed spaces, low speeds, light objects, stable surfaces, and adult approval. Do not enter roads, use motor vehicles, drop heavy objects from height, launch hard projectiles, test impacts on people or animals, improvise restraints, or stand beneath suspended masses.

Evidence standard

Separate direct measurements, calculated quantities, graph interpretations, idealized models, and engineering inferences. Record units, direction, reference frame, system boundary, and uncertainty.

Real data option

Use a walking person or slowly rolling object in a clear, level area with adult approval. Mark positions and record times without entering roads or using motor vehicles.

Practice option

The application may generate labeled practice scenarios for calculation, but practice values are not experimental evidence.

Evaluation

Discuss timing resolution, start/stop judgment, path alignment, surface variation, and repeatability.

Portfolio evidence

Complete the Motion Graph Laboratory and its 8-question check.

Model boundary

Point-mass, constant-acceleration, free-body, closed-system, frictionless, and ideal-machine models simplify shape, rotation, deformation, air resistance, and material behavior.

Essential question and conceptual model

Essential question: How can motion data be collected or analyzed without inventing precision the measurements do not support?

A motion-data investigation needs a defined position reference, a time method, repeated or sufficiently frequent measurements, and an honest record of uncertainty. Raw measurements should be preserved before processing. Graphs and calculated speeds can reveal patterns, but scatter and timing resolution limit precision. A strong laboratory report separates what was measured from what was calculated or inferred.

Worked reasoning example

Suppose measured positions are 0.00 m at 0.0 s, 0.48 m at 0.5 s, and 1.02 m at 1.0 s. Interval speeds are 0.96 m/s and 1.08 m/s. The difference does not automatically prove acceleration because measurement uncertainty and timing resolution may contribute. A conclusion should compare the size of the change with the quality of the measurements.

Vocabulary and misconception checks

Vocabulary: raw data · processed data · interval speed · uncertainty · resolution · repeat trial

Misconception check: More decimal places do not create more accurate data. Report precision consistent with the measuring method.

Retrieval and explanation practice

Answer from memory first. Then use the lesson to correct units, signs, diagrams, calculations, and any claim that is broader than the evidence.

  1. Distinguish raw measurements from calculated values in a motion table.
  2. Calculate an interval speed from two data points.
  3. Name two sources of uncertainty in a simple motion investigation.
  4. Explain why one unusual point should be investigated rather than silently deleted.

Mentor evidence conference

Bring your diagram, graph, calculation, or engineering claim to the Science Mentor. The mentor may question assumptions and reasoning but does not supply protected assessment answers or replace independent mastery evidence.

  • Which conclusion is directly supported by your data and which one depends on interpretation?
  • How large is the observed change compared with likely measurement uncertainty?
  • What additional trial would most strengthen your motion claim?
