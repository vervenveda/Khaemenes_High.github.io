Unit 08 · Day 8

Newton’s Second Law: Force, Mass & Acceleration

Use data and models to relate net force, mass, and acceleration.

45–60 minutesMeasurement & modelsPortfolio evidence

Learning objectives

  • Use Fnet = ma
  • Analyze proportional relationships
  • Distinguish mass from weight

Materials

  • Notebook or digital record
  • Calculator and ruler
  • Optional low-speed tabletop or walking measurements with adult approval
Motion and impact safety

Use clear indoor or enclosed spaces, low speeds, light objects, stable surfaces, and adult approval. Do not enter roads, use motor vehicles, drop heavy objects from height, launch hard projectiles, test impacts on people or animals, improvise restraints, or stand beneath suspended masses.

Evidence standard

Separate direct measurements, calculated quantities, graph interpretations, idealized models, and engineering inferences. Record units, direction, reference frame, system boundary, and uncertainty.

Second law

For a constant mass in an inertial frame, acceleration is proportional to net force and inversely proportional to mass.

Data test

Graph acceleration against net force for fixed mass or acceleration against reciprocal mass for fixed force.

Boundary

The equation applies to net external force, not to one selected force while ignoring others.

Portfolio evidence

Complete the Force and Newton Laboratory and its 8-question check.

Model boundary

Point-mass, constant-acceleration, free-body, closed-system, frictionless, and ideal-machine models simplify shape, rotation, deformation, air resistance, and material behavior.

Essential question and conceptual model

Essential question: How does Newton’s second law connect net force, mass, and acceleration quantitatively?

Newton's second law relates net external force to acceleration: F_net = ma. Acceleration points in the direction of the net force. For the same mass, larger net force produces larger acceleration. For the same net force, larger mass produces smaller acceleration. The equation uses net force, not one selected force from the diagram.

Worked reasoning example

A 3.0 kg cart has a 12 N net force to the right. a = F_net/m = 12/3.0 = 4.0 m/s² right. If the same 12 N net force acts on a 6.0 kg cart, a = 2.0 m/s² right. Doubling mass halves acceleration when net force is unchanged.

Vocabulary and misconception checks

Vocabulary: Newton’s second law · net force · mass · acceleration · newton · kilogram

Misconception check: F = ma uses the sum of external forces. Substituting the applied force while ignoring friction or other forces can produce the wrong acceleration.

Retrieval and explanation practice

Answer from memory first. Then use the lesson to correct units, signs, diagrams, calculations, and any claim that is broader than the evidence.

  1. Calculate acceleration from net force and mass.
  2. Calculate net force from mass and acceleration.
  3. Predict how acceleration changes if mass doubles while net force stays constant.
  4. Explain why direction must be included in a one-dimensional vector answer.

Mentor evidence conference

Bring your diagram, graph, calculation, or engineering claim to the Science Mentor. The mentor may question assumptions and reasoning but does not supply protected assessment answers or replace independent mastery evidence.

  • Which forces were combined to obtain your net force?
  • Does the direction of your calculated acceleration agree with your diagram?
  • What measurement uncertainty would affect an experimental F–a relationship?
