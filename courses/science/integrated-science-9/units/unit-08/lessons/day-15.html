Motion and impact safety

Use clear indoor or enclosed spaces, low speeds, light objects, stable surfaces, and adult approval. Do not enter roads, use motor vehicles, drop heavy objects from height, launch hard projectiles, test impacts on people or animals, improvise restraints, or stand beneath suspended masses.

Evidence standard

Separate direct measurements, calculated quantities, graph interpretations, idealized models, and engineering inferences. Record units, direction, reference frame, system boundary, and uncertainty.

Synthesis

A strong solution defines the system, chooses a direction, uses units, shows equations, checks signs, and evaluates whether the result is physically reasonable.

Assessment sequence

Pass four laboratory checks at 7/8, submit the case, pass the quiz at 16/20, pass the assessment at 24/30, and complete reflection.

Safety boundary

No road testing, high drops, heavy impacts, human collision tests, weapon-like projectiles, or improvised vehicle experiments are required.

Portfolio evidence

Submit the engineering case, corrections, constructed responses, and final reflection.

Model boundary

Point-mass, constant-acceleration, free-body, closed-system, frictionless, and ideal-machine models simplify shape, rotation, deformation, air resistance, and material behavior.

Essential question and conceptual model

Essential question: How can one coherent model connect motion graphs, forces, momentum, energy, and engineering evidence?

A strong mechanics explanation uses the representation that matches the question. Motion graphs describe how position or velocity changes with time. Free-body diagrams identify forces and net force. Newton's laws connect net force and acceleration. Momentum and impulse analyze interactions over time. Work and energy track transfer and transformation. Power describes rate. No single representation replaces the others; a complete argument chooses the right model, calculates carefully, and states system boundaries and limitations.

Worked reasoning example

A 2.0 kg cart starts at 1.0 m/s and reaches 5.0 m/s after a constant net force acts for 2.0 s. Acceleration = (5 - 1)/2 = 2.0 m/s². Net force = ma = 4.0 N. Momentum changes from 2.0 to 10.0 kg·m/s, so impulse = 8.0 N·s, consistent with 4.0 N × 2.0 s. Kinetic energy changes from 1.0 J to 25 J, showing that momentum and energy are related to motion but are not interchangeable quantities.

Vocabulary and misconception checks

Vocabulary: representation · system boundary · net force · impulse · energy transfer · model limitation

Misconception check: A correct equation does not guarantee a correct explanation. The chosen quantity, object/system, direction, units, and evidence must all match the question.

Retrieval and explanation practice

Answer from memory first. Then use the lesson to correct units, signs, diagrams, calculations, and any claim that is broader than the evidence.

  1. Connect a position–time graph to velocity and a velocity–time graph to acceleration and displacement.
  2. Use one free-body diagram to calculate net force and acceleration.
  3. Solve one momentum/impulse problem and one energy problem for the same motion.
  4. Identify a model limitation or uncertainty in an engineering interpretation.

Mentor evidence conference

Bring your diagram, graph, calculation, or engineering claim to the Science Mentor. The mentor may question assumptions and reasoning but does not supply protected assessment answers or replace independent mastery evidence.

  • Which representation gives the strongest evidence for your claim?
  • Where could two correct calculations describe different physical quantities and therefore not be interchangeable?
  • Which mastery objective still needs a fresh transfer example before assessment?
