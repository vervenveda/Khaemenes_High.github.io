Unit 08 · Day 2

Distance, Displacement, Speed & Velocity

Calculate and interpret motion while separating path length from change in position.

45–60 minutesMeasurement & modelsPortfolio evidence

Learning objectives

  • Calculate average speed
  • Calculate one-dimensional displacement and velocity
  • Distinguish instantaneous from average quantities

Materials

  • Notebook or digital record
  • Calculator and ruler
  • Optional low-speed tabletop or walking measurements with adult approval
Motion and impact safety

Use clear indoor or enclosed spaces, low speeds, light objects, stable surfaces, and adult approval. Do not enter roads, use motor vehicles, drop heavy objects from height, launch hard projectiles, test impacts on people or animals, improvise restraints, or stand beneath suspended masses.

Evidence standard

Separate direct measurements, calculated quantities, graph interpretations, idealized models, and engineering inferences. Record units, direction, reference frame, system boundary, and uncertainty.

Distance and displacement

Distance is total path length. Displacement is the straight-line change in position with direction.

Speed and velocity

Average speed equals total distance divided by total time. Average velocity equals displacement divided by elapsed time.

Reference frames

Motion descriptions require a stated frame of reference; a passenger may be at rest relative to a seat but moving relative to the road.

Portfolio evidence

Solve six motion records and explain one reference-frame comparison.

Model boundary

Point-mass, constant-acceleration, free-body, closed-system, frictionless, and ideal-machine models simplify shape, rotation, deformation, air resistance, and material behavior.

Essential question and conceptual model

Essential question: How can the same trip have a large distance but a small or zero displacement?

Distance measures total path length. Displacement measures the change from initial position to final position with direction. Average speed uses distance divided by elapsed time; average velocity uses displacement divided by elapsed time. A traveler can move a long distance and return to the starting point, producing nonzero distance and average speed but zero displacement and zero average velocity over the whole trip.

Worked reasoning example

A student walks 30 m east, then 20 m west, in 25 s. Distance = 30 + 20 = 50 m. Displacement = 10 m east. Average speed = 50/25 = 2.0 m/s. Average velocity = 10/25 = 0.40 m/s east. Using 50 m in the velocity calculation would confuse path length with net position change.

Vocabulary and misconception checks

Vocabulary: distance · displacement · speed · velocity · reference direction · average

Misconception check: Average velocity is not always total distance divided by time. It uses displacement.

Retrieval and explanation practice

Answer from memory first. Then use the lesson to correct units, signs, diagrams, calculations, and any claim that is broader than the evidence.

  1. Calculate distance and displacement for 8 m north followed by 3 m south.
  2. Explain how an object can have zero average velocity but nonzero average speed.
  3. Identify the reference direction in one vector answer.
  4. Write a sentence explaining why speed cannot be negative in this introductory treatment.

Mentor evidence conference

Bring your diagram, graph, calculation, or engineering claim to the Science Mentor. The mentor may question assumptions and reasoning but does not supply protected assessment answers or replace independent mastery evidence.

  • Which quantity in your motion story depends on the chosen positive direction?
  • How can a correct calculation still answer the wrong quantity?
  • What extra information is required to convert a speed statement into a velocity statement?
