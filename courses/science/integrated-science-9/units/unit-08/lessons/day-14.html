Unit 08 · Day 14

Power, Machines & Engineering Tradeoffs

Compare rates of energy transfer and evaluate safe designs.

45–60 minutesMeasurement & modelsPortfolio evidence

Learning objectives

  • Calculate power
  • Relate power to work and time
  • Evaluate efficiency, force, distance, stability, and safety tradeoffs

Materials

  • Notebook or digital record
  • Calculator and ruler
  • Optional low-speed tabletop or walking measurements with adult approval
Motion and impact safety

Use clear indoor or enclosed spaces, low speeds, light objects, stable surfaces, and adult approval. Do not enter roads, use motor vehicles, drop heavy objects from height, launch hard projectiles, test impacts on people or animals, improvise restraints, or stand beneath suspended masses.

Evidence standard

Separate direct measurements, calculated quantities, graph interpretations, idealized models, and engineering inferences. Record units, direction, reference frame, system boundary, and uncertainty.

Power

Power is work done or energy transferred per unit time.

Machines

Simple machines can trade force for distance or direction; ideal machines do not create energy.

Engineering

Design decisions balance performance, energy use, materials, cost, access, reliability, and safety.

Portfolio evidence

Complete the Energy, Work and Power Laboratory and evaluate one device.

Model boundary

Point-mass, constant-acceleration, free-body, closed-system, frictionless, and ideal-machine models simplify shape, rotation, deformation, air resistance, and material behavior.

Essential question and conceptual model

Essential question: How do power and engineering tradeoffs distinguish how much energy is transferred from how quickly it is transferred?

Power is the rate of energy transfer or work: P = E/t or W/t, measured in watts. Two devices can transfer the same total energy but at different rates. Machines can trade force for distance, speed, convenience, or control, but they do not create energy. Real designs also involve friction, efficiency, material limits, accessibility, cost, safety, and user needs.

Worked reasoning example

Machine A transfers 1200 J in 60 s, so P = 20 W. Machine B transfers the same 1200 J in 20 s, so P = 60 W. Both transfer the same energy, but B transfers it three times as quickly. If B achieves the higher power by increasing current, speed, or force elsewhere, the design must still be evaluated for heat, structural limits, safety, and efficiency.

Vocabulary and misconception checks

Vocabulary: power · watt · rate · machine · mechanical advantage · engineering tradeoff

Misconception check: Higher power does not necessarily mean higher efficiency, and a machine cannot provide more useful energy than the total energy supplied.

Retrieval and explanation practice

Answer from memory first. Then use the lesson to correct units, signs, diagrams, calculations, and any claim that is broader than the evidence.

  1. Calculate power from energy and time.
  2. Compare two devices that perform the same work in different times.
  3. Distinguish power from energy and efficiency.
  4. Name three non-mathematical constraints that can affect an engineering choice.

Mentor evidence conference

Bring your diagram, graph, calculation, or engineering claim to the Science Mentor. The mentor may question assumptions and reasoning but does not supply protected assessment answers or replace independent mastery evidence.

  • Which metric—energy, power, efficiency, or force—actually addresses your design goal?
  • What tradeoff appears when one performance metric improves?
  • Which safety or accessibility constraint could change the 'best' design?
