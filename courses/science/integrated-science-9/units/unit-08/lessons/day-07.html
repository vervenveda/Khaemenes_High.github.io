Unit 08 · Day 7

Newton’s First Law, Inertia & Reference Frames

Explain constant velocity and changes in motion using net force.

45–60 minutesMeasurement & modelsPortfolio evidence

Learning objectives

  • State Newton’s first law
  • Relate inertia to mass
  • Distinguish balanced forces from absence of forces

Materials

  • Notebook or digital record
  • Calculator and ruler
  • Optional low-speed tabletop or walking measurements with adult approval
Motion and impact safety

Use clear indoor or enclosed spaces, low speeds, light objects, stable surfaces, and adult approval. Do not enter roads, use motor vehicles, drop heavy objects from height, launch hard projectiles, test impacts on people or animals, improvise restraints, or stand beneath suspended masses.

Evidence standard

Separate direct measurements, calculated quantities, graph interpretations, idealized models, and engineering inferences. Record units, direction, reference frame, system boundary, and uncertainty.

First law

An object remains at rest or moves with constant velocity unless a net external force acts.

Inertia

Mass measures resistance to acceleration in the Newtonian model. Greater mass requires greater net force for the same acceleration.

Safety application

Seat restraints change a passenger’s motion by exerting force over time; they do not cancel inertia.

Portfolio evidence

Analyze three transport scenarios and identify the object, forces, and frame of reference.

Model boundary

Point-mass, constant-acceleration, free-body, closed-system, frictionless, and ideal-machine models simplify shape, rotation, deformation, air resistance, and material behavior.

Essential question and conceptual model

Essential question: What does Newton’s first law say about motion when the net external force is zero?

Newton's first law states that an object maintains constant velocity—including rest—when the net external force is zero in an inertial reference frame. Inertia is not a force; it is the tendency associated with mass to resist changes in velocity. Everyday motion often slows because friction or drag creates a nonzero net force, not because motion naturally 'runs out.' Reference frames matter when describing whether velocity is constant.

Worked reasoning example

A puck glides right at 3 m/s on a nearly frictionless surface. If horizontal forces sum to zero, its velocity remains approximately 3 m/s right. No continuing forward force is required to 'keep it moving.' If friction of 0.5 N acts left with no balancing rightward force, net force is left and velocity will change.

Vocabulary and misconception checks

Vocabulary: Newton’s first law · inertia · inertial reference frame · constant velocity · balanced forces

Misconception check: Inertia is not an extra force pushing an object forward. It describes resistance to a change in velocity.

Retrieval and explanation practice

Answer from memory first. Then use the lesson to correct units, signs, diagrams, calculations, and any claim that is broader than the evidence.

  1. Explain what motion is possible when net force equals zero.
  2. Distinguish inertia from force.
  3. Explain why a rolling object slows on a floor even though Newton's first law is valid.
  4. Describe one situation from two different reference frames.

Mentor evidence conference

Bring your diagram, graph, calculation, or engineering claim to the Science Mentor. The mentor may question assumptions and reasoning but does not supply protected assessment answers or replace independent mastery evidence.

  • Which force in your explanation actually changes velocity?
  • Have you accidentally treated motion itself as evidence of a forward force?
  • How would your description change in a moving reference frame?
