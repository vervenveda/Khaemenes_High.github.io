Unit 10 · Day 8

Water Cycle, Watersheds & Groundwater

Track water among reservoirs and apply water-budget reasoning.

45–60 minutesSystems & evidenceSafe field boundaries

Learning objectives

  • Trace evaporation, transpiration, condensation, precipitation, infiltration, runoff, recharge, and discharge
  • Define watershed and groundwater concepts
  • Use a simplified water-budget equation

Materials

  • Notebook or digital record
  • Calculator, maps, and supplied data
  • Optional safe observations from stable public locations with adult approval
Earth-science field safety

Do not enter floodwater, storm drains, caves, mines, construction sites, wildfire areas, disaster zones, unstable slopes, cliffs, sinkholes, restricted shorelines, or fast-moving streams. Do not taste minerals, inhale dust, use acids on unknown rocks, climb road cuts, or collect protected materials.

Evidence standard

Identify direct measurement, mapped observation, laboratory evidence, calculation, model, historical inference, projection, scenario, and decision separately. State scale, units, system boundary, uncertainty, and source conditions.

Water cycle

Water changes location and phase while the global amount is approximately conserved over human timescales.

Watersheds

A watershed drains toward a common outlet; boundaries are topographic rather than political.

Groundwater

Groundwater occupies pores and fractures. The water table is not a universal underground river.

Portfolio evidence

Complete a watershed map, water budget, and source–pathway–receptor explanation.

Model boundary

Earth cross-sections, plate maps, water budgets, reservoir boxes, flux arrows, energy balances, climate projections, and hazard maps simplify scale, heterogeneity, time variability, interactions, and uncertainty.

Essential question and conceptual model

Essential question: How can a watershed and groundwater system be described using reservoirs, pathways, and a water budget?

Water moves among atmospheric, surface, soil, ice, biological, and groundwater reservoirs. Precipitation can become runoff, infiltration, groundwater recharge, evaporation, transpiration, or storage. A watershed is the land area draining toward a common outlet. Groundwater occupies connected pore spaces or fractures; the water table is not a flat underground lake surface in every landscape.

Worked reasoning example

If a watershed receives 80 mm of precipitation during a period, 25 mm leaves as runoff and 40 mm as evapotranspiration, then storage change is 80 - 25 - 40 = +15 mm in a simplified budget with no other terms. The positive sign means water stored in soil, groundwater, snow, or surface reservoirs increased.

Vocabulary and misconception checks

Vocabulary: watershed · runoff · infiltration · recharge · groundwater · water table

Misconception check: Groundwater is not usually a vast open underground river or lake; it commonly occupies pores and fractures in geologic material.

Retrieval and explanation practice

Answer from memory first. Then check budgets, units, system boundaries, model assumptions, uncertainty, and the scope of every causal claim.

  1. Write a simple water-budget equation.
  2. Explain the difference between infiltration and runoff.
  3. Describe how paving could affect runoff and recharge.
  4. Distinguish a watershed boundary from a political boundary.

Mentor evidence conference

Bring your map, budget, data interpretation, or Earth-system model to the Science Mentor. The mentor may challenge assumptions and evidence quality but does not provide protected assessment answers.

  • Which water-budget term is directly measured and which may be estimated?
  • How would an omitted groundwater-flow term affect your storage calculation?
  • What land-use change could alter both flood risk and recharge?
