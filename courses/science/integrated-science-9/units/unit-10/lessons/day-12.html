Unit 10 · Day 12

Biogeochemical Cycle Modeling Laboratory

Model matter conservation through reservoirs, fluxes, residence time, and human modifications.

45–60 minutesSystems & evidenceSafe field boundaries

Learning objectives

  • Calculate net reservoir change
  • Calculate simple residence time
  • Compare carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, and water-cycle structures

Materials

  • Notebook or digital record
  • Calculator, maps, and supplied data
  • Optional safe observations from stable public locations with adult approval
Earth-science field safety

Do not enter floodwater, storm drains, caves, mines, construction sites, wildfire areas, disaster zones, unstable slopes, cliffs, sinkholes, restricted shorelines, or fast-moving streams. Do not taste minerals, inhale dust, use acids on unknown rocks, climb road cuts, or collect protected materials.

Evidence standard

Identify direct measurement, mapped observation, laboratory evidence, calculation, model, historical inference, projection, scenario, and decision separately. State scale, units, system boundary, uncertainty, and source conditions.

Conservation

Atoms are conserved while compounds, reservoirs, and transfer rates change.

Residence time

Reservoir divided by total outflow gives a simplified average residence time under steady-state assumptions.

Human modification

Combustion, land-use change, fertilizer, mining, dams, groundwater withdrawal, and waste alter fluxes.

Portfolio evidence

Complete the Cycle Modeling Laboratory and its 8-question check.

Model boundary

Earth cross-sections, plate maps, water budgets, reservoir boxes, flux arrows, energy balances, climate projections, and hazard maps simplify scale, heterogeneity, time variability, interactions, and uncertainty.

Essential question and conceptual model

Essential question: How can a biogeochemical-cycle model obey matter conservation while still representing uncertainty and human modification?

Box-and-arrow cycle models should conserve matter across defined system boundaries. Every arrow must represent a flux with direction and, when quantitative, units. Human activities can add, remove, or redirect fluxes, but a model should avoid implying that matter disappears. Model usefulness depends on scale: a local watershed, agricultural field, atmosphere, or global cycle can require different reservoirs and pathways.

Worked reasoning example

A reservoir begins with 500 units. During one year it receives 40 from natural input and 25 from human input, while 50 leave through one pathway and 10 through another. Net change = 40 + 25 - 50 - 10 = +5 units. Ending stock = 505 units if no other terms exist. If measurements suggest 520, the missing 15 units require investigation rather than silent adjustment.

Vocabulary and misconception checks

Vocabulary: mass balance · reservoir · flux · boundary · human modification · uncertainty

Misconception check: A cycle diagram with arrows is not automatically a conservation model unless inputs, outputs, and storage changes can be reconciled.

Retrieval and explanation practice

Answer from memory first. Then check budgets, units, system boundaries, model assumptions, uncertainty, and the scope of every causal claim.

  1. Write a mass-balance equation for a reservoir.
  2. Calculate ending stock from starting stock and fluxes.
  3. Identify one human activity that modifies a nitrogen, phosphorus, carbon, or water flux.
  4. Explain how changing spatial scale changes the model.

Mentor evidence conference

Bring your map, budget, data interpretation, or Earth-system model to the Science Mentor. The mentor may challenge assumptions and evidence quality but does not provide protected assessment answers.

  • Does your model actually conserve matter?
  • Which arrow has the weakest evidence or largest uncertainty?
  • What missing flux would explain a budget mismatch?
