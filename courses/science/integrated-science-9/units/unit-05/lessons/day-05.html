Biodiversity Survey & Evidence

Measure species richness and evenness without disturbing organisms or exposing sensitive locations.

45–60 minutesEvidence firstPortfolio evidence

Learning objectives

  • Distinguish genetic, species, and ecosystem diversity
  • Plan a repeatable non-contact survey
  • Calculate richness and Simpson diversity
  • Evaluate sampling bias and detectability

Materials

  • Biodiversity survey app
  • Public or locally collected count data
  • Notebook
Field and organism safety

Use non-contact observation unless a qualified protocol explicitly permits otherwise. Do not handle wildlife, collect protected organisms, disturb nests or dens, culture unknown microbes, trespass, or work in unsafe weather or terrain.

Location, identity, and worldview respect

Do not publish precise locations of sensitive species. Do not require students to disclose a personal religion or family belief. Evaluate evidence classification and reasoning rather than compelled agreement.

Dimensions of biodiversity

Biodiversity includes variation within populations, numbers and relative abundance of species, and diversity of ecosystems and ecological functions.

Sampling methods

Quadrats, transects, point counts, camera traps, acoustic monitoring, environmental DNA, museum collections, and community-science records answer different questions. Every method has detection limits.

Richness and evenness

Richness counts how many species are represented. Evenness describes how balanced abundances are. Two communities may have equal richness but different dominance patterns.

Simpson diversity

One form of Simpson diversity is 1 minus the sum of n(n−1) divided by N(N−1), where n is each species count and N is the total count. Larger values generally indicate greater diversity in the sampled data.

Evidence standard

Use your own non-contact observations or a public dataset. Record sampling effort, date range, and method. Never collect protected organisms or reveal a sensitive species location.

Portfolio evidence

Enter at least five species counts in the biodiversity calculator, export or print the result, and explain two sources of undercounting or bias.

Boundary and limitation

An index summarizes a sample. It does not capture every ecological function, genetic difference, seasonal visitor, or undetected species.

Deep learning: richness, evenness, and sampling

Essential question: Why can two communities contain the same number of species yet differ greatly in biodiversity?

Species richness counts how many species are represented. Evenness describes how evenly individuals are distributed among those species. Diversity indices combine aspects of both, but every index has assumptions and should be interpreted alongside sampling method and effort.

Worked diversity example

Community A has 5 individuals of species X and 5 of species Y. Community B has 9 X and 1 Y. Both have richness = 2, but A is more even.

Using one common Simpson diversity form, D = 1 − Σ[n(n−1)] / [N(N−1)]:

  • A: 1 − (20 + 20)/90 ≈ 0.56.
  • B: 1 − (72 + 0)/90 = 0.20.

The exact numerical interpretation depends on which Simpson index convention is used, so always record the formula rather than writing only “Simpson index.”

Misconception and retrieval check

  • High richness does not automatically mean high evenness.
  • A larger sample can detect more species even when the true community is unchanged.
  • Absence from a sample is not proof of absence from the habitat.
  1. Which community is more even: 10,10,10 or 28,1,1?
  2. Why should two sites be compared using similar effort and method?
  3. What privacy concern applies to rare or sensitive species locations?
