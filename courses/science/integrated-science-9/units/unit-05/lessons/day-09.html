Unit 05 · Day 9

Disturbance, Succession & Resilience

Analyze how ecosystems respond to fire, storms, floods, eruptions, land use, and restoration.

45–60 minutesEvidence firstPortfolio evidence

Learning objectives

  • Distinguish disturbance from ecosystem destruction
  • Compare primary and secondary succession
  • Explain resistance, recovery, and resilience
  • Evaluate when intervention or non-intervention is justified

Materials

  • Before-and-after image or dataset
  • Notebook
  • Source dossier
Field and organism safety

Use non-contact observation unless a qualified protocol explicitly permits otherwise. Do not handle wildlife, collect protected organisms, disturb nests or dens, culture unknown microbes, trespass, or work in unsafe weather or terrain.

Location, identity, and worldview respect

Do not publish precise locations of sensitive species. Do not require students to disclose a personal religion or family belief. Evaluate evidence classification and reasoning rather than compelled agreement.

Disturbance

A disturbance changes resources, organisms, or physical conditions. Its ecological significance depends on intensity, duration, spatial extent, frequency, and the history of the system.

Succession

Primary succession begins where little developed soil or biological legacy remains. Secondary succession follows disturbance where soil, seeds, roots, microbes, or surviving organisms persist.

Resilience and thresholds

Resistance is limited change during disturbance; recovery is return or reorganization afterward; resilience is the capacity to absorb change while retaining key functions. Some systems cross thresholds and reorganize into a different state.

Management decisions

Prescribed fire, invasive-species removal, hydrologic restoration, wildlife corridors, and passive recovery involve benefits, costs, uncertainty, and community values.

Evidence standard

Use documented before-and-after observations or a time series. Avoid calling every change recovery; define the indicator and reference condition.

Portfolio evidence

Analyze a documented disturbance: identify the direct evidence, inferred mechanisms, indicators of recovery, uncertainties, and one management trade-off.

Boundary and limitation

There is not always one historically correct endpoint. Climate, species movement, and human land use can change the feasible future state.

Deep learning: disturbance does not mean ecological reset to zero

Essential question: How can ecosystems change after disturbance while retaining memory, legacies, and alternative recovery pathways?

A disturbance changes ecosystem structure, resources, or physical conditions. Primary succession begins where biological legacies such as developed soil are minimal; secondary succession follows disturbance where soil or other biological legacies remain. Real recovery pathways depend on disturbance severity, seed banks, surviving organisms, connectivity, climate, invasive species, and repeated events.

Resistance describes how little a system changes during disturbance; resilience describes aspects of recovery after change. Neither means returning to an identical prior state.

Worked time-series reasoning

A grassland has 80% native plant cover before a fire, drops to 25% immediately after, reaches 55% after one year, and 72% after three years. The data show partial recovery toward the original measured condition. They do not prove every species composition, soil process, or food-web interaction returned to its original state.

Misconception and retrieval check

  • Fire is not universally harmful; many ecosystems have fire-adapted processes, while altered fire regimes can still cause severe damage.
  • Succession is not always a fixed march toward one inevitable “climax” community.
  • Resilience is multidimensional; one variable can recover while another remains changed.
  1. What distinguishes primary from secondary succession?
  2. Why can a single recovery metric be misleading?
  3. Name one ecological legacy that can influence recovery.
