Unit 05 · Day 14

Human Origins, Creation & Worldviews

Study human-origins evidence, creation traditions, intelligent-design arguments, and naturalistic interpretations without compelling personal belief.

45–60 minutesEvidence firstPortfolio evidence

Learning objectives

  • Distinguish specimen, measurement, dating result, classification, ancestry inference, and worldview conclusion
  • Represent evolutionary, creationist, intelligent-design, and evolutionary-creation views accurately
  • Identify methodological naturalism and its limits
  • Evaluate sources without grading a student’s personal faith

Materials

  • Smithsonian Human Origins sources
  • Worldview source dossier
  • Hearing template
Field and organism safety

Use non-contact observation unless a qualified protocol explicitly permits otherwise. Do not handle wildlife, collect protected organisms, disturb nests or dens, culture unknown microbes, trespass, or work in unsafe weather or terrain.

Location, identity, and worldview respect

Do not publish precise locations of sensitive species. Do not require students to disclose a personal religion or family belief. Evaluate evidence classification and reasoning rather than compelled agreement.

Evidence categories

Human-origins research uses fossils, archaeological artifacts, comparative anatomy, geology, dating methods, ancient DNA, and living-genome comparisons. These data differ in directness, preservation, and uncertainty.

Scientific reconstruction

Researchers classify specimens, estimate ages, and test branching hypotheses. A fossil may be interpreted as an ancestor, close relative, or side branch; direct ancestry is rarely demonstrable for one individual.

Worldview literacy without false equivalence

Students may encounter young-earth creation, old-earth or progressive creation, intelligent-design arguments, evolutionary creation, and secular philosophical naturalism. These positions differ in theology and philosophy and should be represented accurately when they are discussed.

They do not all function as scientific theories. In science class, claims are scientific to the extent that they are framed in ways that generate testable empirical predictions and can be evaluated against observations. Religious belief, divine purpose, and metaphysical claims may be personally important while remaining outside what empirical methods can directly test.

Important distinction: methodological naturalism is a working rule of empirical science—investigating testable natural mechanisms. It is not the same claim as metaphysical naturalism, which is a philosophical position about what ultimately exists.

Methodological boundary

Empirical science normally tests natural mechanisms using observable consequences. Supernatural agency is not ordinarily isolated as a laboratory variable. This methodological limit neither proves nor disproves God; it defines what a scientific method can directly test.

Evidence standard

For scientific human-origins claims, use primary or authoritative scientific evidence: fossils, archaeological context, comparative anatomy, geochronology, ancient DNA, and population-genomic data. For worldview literacy, use primary sources from the worldview or religious communities being described. Never treat an advocacy statement as if it were a fossil measurement, genetic dataset, or dating result, and never treat a scientific dataset as though it settles a theological question by itself.

Portfolio evidence

Prepare a two-part brief. Part A is scientific: five direct observations, three scientific historical inferences, two uncertainties, and an accurate explanation of the current scientific common-ancestry model. Part B is worldview literacy: accurately summarize at least two philosophical or religious interpretations and explain which claims fall outside direct empirical testing. Personal belief is not scored.

Boundary and limitation

Students are assessed on accuracy, evidence classification, reasoning, source representation, and respect—not on adopting or rejecting a religious belief.

Worked source-classification example

“This femur has a measured length of 410 mm” is a specimen observation. “The volcanic layer above the site is dated to a particular interval using a stated radiometric method” is a measurement-based age constraint. “These specimens share a recent common ancestor” is a scientific historical inference tested against anatomy, dating, and genetic patterns. “Humans possess a divinely given spiritual purpose” is a theological claim. The statements can coexist in one person's worldview, but they are supported and evaluated by different methods.

Retrieval and discussion practice

  1. Why is direct ancestry rarely demonstrated for one fossil individual?
  2. What is the difference between methodological and metaphysical naturalism?
  3. Why should a science course accurately teach evolutionary common ancestry even when students hold diverse religious beliefs?
  4. Why should the course also avoid claiming that empirical science has disproved every possible belief about God or purpose?
Practice explanations — open after attempting
  1. Fossils preserve samples of past populations; relationships are inferred from multiple lines of evidence rather than witnessed pedigrees.
  2. Methodological naturalism is a scientific working method; metaphysical naturalism is a broader philosophical claim.
  3. Scientific literacy requires understanding the evidence-based model used in modern biology regardless of personal belief.
  4. Empirical methods test observable consequences and natural mechanisms; many theological claims are not framed as laboratory-testable hypotheses.
