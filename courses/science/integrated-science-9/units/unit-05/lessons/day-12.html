Unit 05 · Day 12

Microevolution, Speciation & Macroevolution

Clarify definitions and evidentiary levels without implying an instantaneous jump between fundamentally unrelated organisms.

45–60 minutesEvidence firstPortfolio evidence

Learning objectives

  • Define microevolution, speciation, and macroevolution as used in biology
  • Explain reproductive isolation and species-concept limits
  • Distinguish observed processes from deep-time reconstruction
  • Compare scientific and creation-based boundary interpretations fairly

Materials

  • Evidence classification lab
  • Source dossier
  • Notebook
Field and organism safety

Use non-contact observation unless a qualified protocol explicitly permits otherwise. Do not handle wildlife, collect protected organisms, disturb nests or dens, culture unknown microbes, trespass, or work in unsafe weather or terrain.

Location, identity, and worldview respect

Do not publish precise locations of sensitive species. Do not require students to disclose a personal religion or family belief. Evaluate evidence classification and reasoning rather than compelled agreement.

Microevolution

Microevolution commonly refers to measurable changes in inherited traits or allele frequencies within populations.

Speciation

Speciation is the development of reproductive isolation and independent evolutionary trajectories. It can be studied in living populations, but species concepts differ for asexual organisms, fossils, hybridizing populations, and geographically separated groups.

Macroevolution

In standard biology, macroevolution includes patterns at or above the species level, such as speciation, extinction, and diversification. It does not require an individual to give birth to a fundamentally unrelated organism.

Competing boundary interpretations

Evolutionary common-descent models treat accumulated population divergence as producing branching lineages. Creation-based models may accept extensive adaptation and speciation while proposing created boundaries or kinds. “Kind” is a theological or creation-model category and does not have one universally accepted empirical definition.

Evidence standard

For every example, classify whether the evidence is a directly observed reproductive outcome, measured genetic difference, fossil anatomy, historical inference, or worldview conclusion.

Portfolio evidence

Build a comparison table with definitions, one observed example, one historical inference, one limitation, and one creation/evolution interpretation for microevolution, speciation, and macroevolution.

Boundary and limitation

A definition does not prove a history. Students must evaluate the evidence offered for each claim rather than arguing from terminology alone.

Pathway choices
FoundationUse prepared definitions, labeled evidence categories, and guided calculations.
CoreComplete the full evidence analysis and defend conclusions within stated limits.
ExtendedCompare primary sources, quantify uncertainty, and evaluate alternative models.
