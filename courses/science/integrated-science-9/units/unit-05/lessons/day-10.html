Unit 05 · Day 10

Biodiversity, Human Impacts & Conservation

Use evidence and community priorities to compare conservation and restoration strategies.

45–60 minutesEvidence firstPortfolio evidence

Learning objectives

  • Identify major pressures on biodiversity
  • Explain ecosystem services without reducing nature to price alone
  • Compare in situ and ex situ conservation
  • Evaluate conservation choices for effectiveness, equity, and uncertainty

Materials

  • Conservation case template
  • Biodiversity record
  • Notebook
Field and organism safety

Use non-contact observation unless a qualified protocol explicitly permits otherwise. Do not handle wildlife, collect protected organisms, disturb nests or dens, culture unknown microbes, trespass, or work in unsafe weather or terrain.

Location, identity, and worldview respect

Do not publish precise locations of sensitive species. Do not require students to disclose a personal religion or family belief. Evaluate evidence classification and reasoning rather than compelled agreement.

Pressures

Habitat loss and fragmentation, overexploitation, pollution, invasive species, altered climate, disease, and interacting pressures can reduce populations and ecological functions.

Values and services

Biodiversity supports food, clean water, pollination, soil formation, cultural identity, recreation, knowledge, and intrinsic value. Different communities may weigh benefits and responsibilities differently.

Strategies

Protected areas, habitat corridors, harvest limits, pollution control, captive breeding, seed banks, assisted recovery, Indigenous stewardship, and community-based conservation address different causes.

Evidence-based comparison

A strong plan identifies the target, causal pressure, measurable indicator, baseline, timeline, responsible parties, unintended effects, community rights, and revision rule.

Evidence standard

Use a real conservation program or public dataset. Separate documented outcome from projected benefit.

Portfolio evidence

Create a one-page conservation decision matrix comparing three strategies by ecological benefit, evidence strength, cost, accessibility, community impact, and uncertainty.

Boundary and limitation

Conservation is both scientific and civic. Science can estimate consequences, but values and rights also shape decisions.

Deep learning: conservation is a decision problem as well as a science problem

Essential question: How can communities compare conservation strategies when ecological benefits, costs, uncertainty, rights, and tradeoffs differ?

Major biodiversity pressures include habitat loss and fragmentation, overexploitation, invasive species, pollution, disease, and climate change. Conservation strategies can include protected areas, habitat restoration, wildlife corridors, harvest rules, invasive-species management, pollution reduction, captive breeding, and community-led stewardship.

Science can estimate likely ecological outcomes and uncertainty. Communities and governments must also consider law, livelihoods, cultural values, land rights, feasibility, and who bears costs or receives benefits.

Worked decision comparison

A fictional wetland project compares two strategies. Strategy A restores 40 hectares at a cost of 2 units per hectare and is expected to improve habitat score by 20%. Strategy B restores 25 hectares at 1 unit per hectare and is expected to improve habitat score by 15% while preserving local access.

A responsible recommendation should not select a winner from one number alone. It should compare ecological effect, cost, area, community impact, uncertainty, time horizon, and monitoring indicators.

Misconception and retrieval check

  • “Natural” does not automatically mean unmanaged or undisturbed.
  • Conservation success should be monitored with measurable indicators rather than intention alone.
  • Protecting biodiversity should not erase legitimate community rights or knowledge.
  1. Name three biodiversity pressures.
  2. What makes an indicator useful for evaluating restoration?
  3. Why can two scientifically plausible strategies lead to different policy choices?

Mentor and portfolio handoff

Give Archaemenes your proposed strategy and ask for one ecological tradeoff, one community question, and one uncertainty you may have missed.
