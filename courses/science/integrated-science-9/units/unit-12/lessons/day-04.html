Unit 12 · Day 4

Research, Baselines, Metrics & Evidence Quality

Establish a defensible baseline and choose indicators that can detect meaningful improvement.

45–75 minutesEngineering evidenceSafe capstone boundaries

Learning objectives

  • Use authoritative sources and community knowledge appropriately
  • Distinguish baseline, target, benchmark, indicator, and proxy
  • Evaluate bias, missing data, uncertainty, and conflicts of interest

Materials

  • Notebook or digital portfolio
  • Calculator, supplied records, and low-risk modeling materials
  • Only teacher- or parent-approved prototype materials and procedures
Capstone safety and authorization

No mains electricity, combustion, pressure vessels, structural loading of people, medical testing, human-subject experiments, private-data collection without consent, road testing, water-entry, hazardous chemicals, biological culturing, weapons, drones, lasers, radio transmitters, construction-site access, or deployment into public infrastructure is required. Use models, simulations, supplied data, and low-risk prototypes.

Evidence and accountability standard

Record source, stakeholder, baseline, unit, method, calibration, trial count, uncertainty, assumptions, conflicts of interest, system boundary, impacts, and who is authorized to accept residual risk. Distinguish measured, calculated, modeled, estimated, assumed, projected, and preferred values.

Baseline

A baseline records current conditions before the proposed intervention.

Metrics

A useful metric is relevant, measurable, interpretable, repeatable, and connected to the stated need.

Evidence quality

Source authority alone is insufficient; method, date, sample, assumptions, uncertainty, and applicability matter.

Portfolio evidence

Create a baseline table with source, variable, unit, method, uncertainty, and target.

Model boundary

Decision matrices, prototypes, simulations, life-cycle estimates, risk scores, and optimization results depend on selected boundaries, data quality, assumptions, stakeholder priorities, and explored alternatives. They support decisions but do not certify a product or eliminate professional review.

Essential question and conceptual model

Essential question: How can a baseline and evidence-quality plan show whether a redesign actually improves a system?

A baseline describes current performance before intervention. Metrics should be valid for the need, measurable with available tools, and sensitive enough to detect meaningful change. Evidence quality depends on repeatability, sample size, measurement resolution, controls, and whether the metric is a proxy or direct measure.

Worked reasoning example

If a device currently uses 120 Wh per task and the redesign uses 96 Wh per task, percent reduction = (120−96)/120 ×100% = 20%. That result is only meaningful if both measurements represent comparable tasks and operating conditions.

Vocabulary and misconception checks

Vocabulary: baseline · metric · indicator · measurement validity · repeatability · proxy

Misconception check: Any number is not automatically a useful metric; it must connect to the design need.

Retrieval and explanation practice

Answer from memory first. Then check calculations, requirements, evidence quality, risk assumptions, stakeholder effects, and whether each recommendation is reproducible.

  1. Identify a baseline metric for your problem.
  2. Calculate percent improvement from a before/after pair.
  3. Explain why operating conditions must be matched.
  4. Distinguish a direct performance measure from a proxy.

Mentor design review

Bring your need statement, decision matrix, prototype data, risk analysis, or final recommendation to the Science Mentor. The mentor may challenge assumptions and design reasoning but does not replace independent work or protected assessment evidence.

  • Which metric most directly tests whether the need has been improved?
  • What measurement could look precise but fail to represent the real problem?
  • How many repeated trials would make your comparison more credible?
