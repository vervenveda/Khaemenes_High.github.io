Risk, Reliability & Resilience Laboratory

Capstone safety and authorization

No mains electricity, combustion, pressure vessels, structural loading of people, medical testing, human-subject experiments, private-data collection without consent, road testing, water-entry, hazardous chemicals, biological culturing, weapons, drones, lasers, radio transmitters, construction-site access, or deployment into public infrastructure is required. Use models, simulations, supplied data, and low-risk prototypes.

Evidence and accountability standard

Record source, stakeholder, baseline, unit, method, calibration, trial count, uncertainty, assumptions, conflicts of interest, system boundary, impacts, and who is authorized to accept residual risk. Distinguish measured, calculated, modeled, estimated, assumed, projected, and preferred values.

Screening scores

Ordinal risk scores help prioritize review but do not represent precise probabilities.

Series systems

If every independent component must work, system reliability is the product of component reliabilities.

Residual risk

A control can reduce risk without eliminating it; remaining risk must be documented and accepted by authorized stakeholders.

Portfolio evidence

Complete the Risk & Reliability Laboratory and its 8-question check.

Model boundary

Decision matrices, prototypes, simulations, life-cycle estimates, risk scores, and optimization results depend on selected boundaries, data quality, assumptions, stakeholder priorities, and explored alternatives. They support decisions but do not certify a product or eliminate professional review.

Essential question and conceptual model

Essential question: How can reliability and recovery calculations support—rather than replace—engineering judgment?

For independent components arranged in series where every component must work, system reliability is the product of component reliabilities. Redundancy can improve availability but introduces complexity and common-cause failures. Recovery performance can be measured by time to restore critical function or fraction of service maintained during disruption. Screening calculations should state assumptions clearly.

Worked reasoning example

Two independent series components have reliabilities 0.95 and 0.90. System reliability =0.95×0.90=0.855, or 85.5%. If a mitigation raises the second component to 0.98, new system reliability =0.95×0.98=0.931, or 93.1%. This assumes independence and that both components are required.

Vocabulary and misconception checks

Vocabulary: series reliability · redundancy · common-cause failure · availability · recovery time · critical function

Misconception check: Adding redundant components does not guarantee perfect reliability; shared hazards can defeat multiple components at once.

Retrieval and explanation practice

Answer from memory first. Then check calculations, requirements, evidence quality, risk assumptions, stakeholder effects, and whether each recommendation is reproducible.

  1. Calculate series-system reliability.
  2. Compare reliability before and after a component improvement.
  3. Identify one assumption behind multiplying reliabilities.
  4. Define one measurable resilience/recovery metric.

Mentor design review

Bring your need statement, decision matrix, prototype data, risk analysis, or final recommendation to the Science Mentor. The mentor may challenge assumptions and design reasoning but does not replace independent work or protected assessment evidence.

  • Are component failures truly independent?
  • Which assumption most threatens your reliability calculation?
  • How would a common-cause failure change the design strategy?
