Unit 12 · Day 1

Engineering for Sustainable Systems

Frame engineering as an evidence-based, iterative process for improving systems within ecological and social limits.

45–75 minutesEngineering evidenceSafe capstone boundaries

Learning objectives

  • Distinguish science questions from engineering problems
  • Identify stakeholders, system boundaries, needs, and unintended consequences
  • Explain why sustainable solutions balance environmental, social, and economic performance

Materials

  • Notebook or digital portfolio
  • Calculator, supplied records, and low-risk modeling materials
  • Only teacher- or parent-approved prototype materials and procedures
Capstone safety and authorization

No mains electricity, combustion, pressure vessels, structural loading of people, medical testing, human-subject experiments, private-data collection without consent, road testing, water-entry, hazardous chemicals, biological culturing, weapons, drones, lasers, radio transmitters, construction-site access, or deployment into public infrastructure is required. Use models, simulations, supplied data, and low-risk prototypes.

Evidence and accountability standard

Record source, stakeholder, baseline, unit, method, calibration, trial count, uncertainty, assumptions, conflicts of interest, system boundary, impacts, and who is authorized to accept residual risk. Distinguish measured, calculated, modeled, estimated, assumed, projected, and preferred values.

Science and engineering

Science develops explanations about the world; engineering designs and evaluates solutions to needs and problems.

Sustainability

A sustainable design considers present and future resource use, environmental integrity, human well-being, and economic feasibility.

Iteration

Engineering rarely follows a perfect straight line; evidence from testing changes the problem definition and the design.

Portfolio evidence

Choose a locally or globally relevant challenge and create an initial system map.

Model boundary

Decision matrices, prototypes, simulations, life-cycle estimates, risk scores, and optimization results depend on selected boundaries, data quality, assumptions, stakeholder priorities, and explored alternatives. They support decisions but do not certify a product or eliminate professional review.

Essential question and conceptual model

Essential question: What makes an engineering solution sustainable rather than merely functional?

Engineering improves systems under real constraints. A sustainable solution must meet a defined need while accounting for materials, energy, maintenance, lifetime, safety, accessibility, cost, environmental effects, and end-of-life pathways. Sustainability is not a label added after design; it changes the criteria and system boundary used from the beginning.

Worked reasoning example

Suppose two water-purification concepts both remove the same contaminant. Concept A has lower initial cost but needs a disposable cartridge every month; Concept B costs more initially but has a washable component lasting five years. A sustainable comparison must evaluate performance, replacement materials, energy, maintenance, waste, and lifetime—not purchase price alone.

Vocabulary and misconception checks

Vocabulary: engineering system · sustainability · system boundary · functional unit · life cycle · stakeholder

Misconception check: A solution is not automatically sustainable because it uses a recycled material or renewable-energy component.

Retrieval and explanation practice

Answer from memory first. Then check calculations, requirements, evidence quality, risk assumptions, stakeholder effects, and whether each recommendation is reproducible.

  1. Define the need served by one engineered system.
  2. Name at least five life-cycle stages that should be considered.
  3. Explain why system boundary changes the apparent impact of a design.
  4. Distinguish technical performance from sustainability performance.

Mentor design review

Bring your need statement, decision matrix, prototype data, risk analysis, or final recommendation to the Science Mentor. The mentor may challenge assumptions and design reasoning but does not replace independent work or protected assessment evidence.

  • Which criterion would cause your design to fail even if its main function works?
  • What impact lies outside your current system boundary?
  • How would changing the functional unit alter your comparison?
