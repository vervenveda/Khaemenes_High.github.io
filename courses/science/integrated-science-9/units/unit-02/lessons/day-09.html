Unit 02 · Day 9 · Nonequilibrium Cell Systems

Active & Bulk Transport

Explain how cells spend and couple energy to maintain gradients, move selected solutes, reshape membrane, and traffic large cargo. Compare pumps, cotransport, endocytosis, and exocytosis as mechanisms that keep living cells far from passive equilibrium.

60–75 minutesTier 1 · Model-BasedMechanism StudioMastery ≥80%Portfolio Evidence

Learning objectives

  • Distinguish primary active transport, secondary active transport, facilitated diffusion, and bulk vesicular transport.
  • Explain how ATP hydrolysis or stored electrochemical gradients can be coupled to otherwise unfavorable transport.
  • Model how pumps help establish gradients that can drive additional cellular work.
  • Differentiate phagocytosis, pinocytosis, receptor-mediated endocytosis, and exocytosis at an introductory mechanistic level.
  • Use evidence and appropriately bounded claims when researching a real transport system.

Materials & tools

  • Notebook or printed lesson
  • Transport cards/tokens or digital drawing tool
  • Optional ecosystem membrane-transport simulation/game
  • Evidence & Citation Studio for the Extended real-system evidence trace

Tier 1 · model-based

No biological specimens, medications, salt-loading demonstrations, electrical stimulation, or wet-lab transport experiments are required. Use supplied models and reputable evidence. Do not experiment on your body to demonstrate membrane transport.

1. Living cells maintain nonequilibrium conditions

If membranes permitted every substance to move passively until all useful gradients disappeared, many cellular processes would fail. Cells continually use energy and regulated transport to maintain ion, pH, nutrient, and other differences across membranes. These gradients are not violations of diffusion or thermodynamics; they are maintained by energy-consuming processes in an open system.

Systems reasoning

Why does maintaining a concentration difference over time require a mechanism if passive movement tends to reduce that difference?

2. Primary active transport

In primary active transport, a transport protein couples movement against an electrochemical gradient directly to an energy-releasing chemical process such as ATP hydrolysis. The sodium-potassium pump in animal plasma membranes is a classic example: its conformational cycle uses ATP and contributes to Na⁺ and K⁺ gradients.

Precision: ATP does not “push ions” like a hand. Chemical energy is coupled to protein conformational changes and transport cycles. Also, the sodium-potassium pump is not the only active transporter.
Mechanism chain

Write a four-step causal chain connecting ATP hydrolysis → transporter conformational cycling → ion redistribution → a maintained electrochemical gradient.

3. Secondary active transport: gradients can store usable potential

A gradient established by one energy-consuming process can drive another transport process. In secondary active transport, movement of one substance down its electrochemical gradient is coupled to movement of another substance in a direction that would otherwise be unfavorable. The cotransporter itself may not hydrolyze ATP directly, but the system depends indirectly on energy previously used to establish the driving gradient.

MechanismImmediate driverCan move cargo against its gradient?
Facilitated diffusionCargo's own favorable electrochemical/concentration gradientNo
Primary active transportDirect coupling to an energy source such as ATP hydrolysisYes
Secondary active transportFavorable movement of a coupled ion/solute down a stored gradientYes, for the coupled cargo
Important correction: “does not directly use ATP” does not automatically make a transporter passive. Ask where the driving free-energy difference comes from.

4. Symport and antiport describe direction relationships

Coupled transporters can move substances in the same general direction across a membrane (symport) or in opposite directions (antiport). These terms describe relative transport directions; they do not by themselves tell you whether a particular system is passive or active.

Classification challenge

A transporter allows Na⁺ to move inward down its electrochemical gradient while glucose is carried inward against its concentration gradient. Is glucose movement passive or secondary active? What stored gradient supplies the immediate driving force?

5. Bulk transport reshapes membrane

Large particles, macromolecular assemblies, and substantial volumes of extracellular material cannot simply pass through the lipid bilayer. Eukaryotic cells can internalize material by forming membrane-bound vesicles and can export cargo when intracellular vesicles fuse with the plasma membrane.

ProcessIntroductory descriptionDo not oversimplify as
PhagocytosisEngulfment of relatively large particles by specialized cells/processes“cell eating” as the entire mechanism
PinocytosisUptake involving extracellular fluid and dissolved materialA perfectly nonspecific drinking straw
Receptor-mediated endocytosisReceptors help concentrate selected ligands/cargo into internalizing membrane regionsProof that only one molecule enters
ExocytosisVesicle fusion adds membrane and releases appropriate cargo externallyA vesicle simply “popping” through the membrane

6. Membrane area is part of the accounting

Endocytosis removes portions of plasma membrane into the cell; exocytosis can add vesicle membrane to the plasma membrane. Cells regulate membrane trafficking so surface area and membrane composition can be maintained or deliberately changed.

Conservation-style reasoning

If a cell performed sustained exocytosis with no compensating membrane retrieval, what would you predict about plasma-membrane surface area? Why is that prediction only a simplified model of a real cell?

7. Transport mechanism decision tree

  1. Can the substance cross the lipid bilayer directly under the conditions?
  2. If a protein is involved, is movement favorable relative to the relevant gradient?
  3. If movement is unfavorable, what energy source or coupled gradient drives it?
  4. Is the cargo being moved molecule-by-molecule through a transporter or inside membrane-bound vesicles?
  5. What evidence would distinguish the proposed mechanism from an alternative?
Apply the tree

Classify and justify: O₂ entering down its gradient; K⁺ moving through an open channel down its electrochemical gradient; Ca²⁺ pumped against its gradient using ATP-linked machinery; glucose accumulated through ion-coupled cotransport; a cell internalizing a large particle.

8. Real transport evidence trace — Evidence & Citation Studio

For the Extended pathway, investigate one real transport system such as the sodium-potassium pump, a sodium-glucose cotransporter, a proton pump, receptor-mediated LDL uptake, or regulated exocytosis.

In Evidence & Citation Studio, create the scientific claim first. Then record the source metadata, classify the source, connect evidence as supporting/opposing/contextual/insufficient where appropriate, record the exact evidence location, note reliability and limitations, and preserve the current assessment rather than asking the tool to decide whether the claim is true.

Citation integrity: generated citation records are drafting aids. Check unusual source types or incomplete metadata against the relevant current official style guidance before final submission.
Claim-scope audit

Write one claim about the selected transporter that is no broader than the evidence you recorded. Then name one piece of evidence that would strengthen or challenge it.

9. Mentor mechanism defense

After your independent classification work, present one active or bulk-transport mechanism to the Science Mentor. Ask the Mentor to remove ATP, collapse a driving gradient, block a receptor, or prevent vesicle fusion. Predict the direct consequence first and distinguish it from downstream effects.

Mentor and simulations support explanation and revision. Independent mastery evidence remains the student's own reasoning.

Exit mastery check

  1. Why can a living cell maintain a gradient instead of passively reaching equilibrium?
  2. Distinguish primary from secondary active transport.
  3. Why is facilitated diffusion protein-mediated but still passive?
  4. What do symport and antiport describe?
  5. Distinguish receptor-mediated endocytosis from exocytosis.
  6. Predict what happens to transport driven by an ion gradient if that driving gradient collapses, and explain why.
