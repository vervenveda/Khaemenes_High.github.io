Unit 02 · Day 6 · Boundary Systems

The Plasma Membrane: Selectivity, Transport & Communication

Model the membrane as a dynamic molecular system—not a sealed wall or simple doorway. Use molecular properties, gradients, proteins, energy coupling, and signaling to predict how cells regulate exchange while maintaining internal conditions.

60–75 minutesTier 1 · Model-BasedTransport Reasoning StudioMastery ≥80%Portfolio Evidence

Learning objectives

  • Explain how phospholipid structure contributes to bilayer formation and selective permeability.
  • Predict relative membrane crossing using size, polarity, charge, concentration/electrochemical gradients, and transport proteins.
  • Distinguish simple diffusion, facilitated diffusion, and active transport without equating every protein-mediated movement with energy use.
  • Explain why membranes also function in signaling, recognition, adhesion, and cellular organization.
  • Evaluate membrane models for useful simplifications and misleading omissions.

  • Paper/colored pencils or digital drawing tool
  • Tokens/cards optional for transport simulation
  • Optional ecosystem membrane/transport simulation or cell game
  • Optional scientific calculator for Extended rate comparisons

Tier 1 · model-based

No chemicals, dialysis tubing, food dyes, biological specimens, membranes, or wet-lab substitutes are required. Use paper/digital models unless a separate supervised investigation provides its own approved safety procedure.

1. Why phospholipids form a boundary

Phospholipids are amphipathic: their head regions interact favorably with water while their hydrocarbon tails are comparatively hydrophobic. In an aqueous environment, these properties contribute to spontaneous bilayer organization with hydrophobic regions shielded from surrounding water.

The resulting membrane is dynamic. Lipids and many proteins can move laterally; composition is heterogeneous; membrane behavior depends on temperature, lipid composition, proteins, and interactions with the cytoskeleton and extracellular structures.

Mechanism prompt

Explain why “phospholipids line up because the cell tells them to” is weaker than an explanation based on molecular interactions with water.

2. Selectively permeable does not mean selectively choosing

Membrane permeability emerges from molecular properties and regulated transport systems. Small nonpolar molecules can generally cross the lipid portion more readily than ions and many large or strongly polar substances. Water movement is important and can occur through the bilayer and, in many cells, through aquaporin channels.

Avoid personification: a membrane does not “know what the cell needs.” Molecular interactions, gradients, channel/carrier properties, regulation, and energy coupling produce selective outcomes.
Prediction ranking

Without using a transport protein, rank these qualitatively from more likely to cross the lipid bilayer readily to less likely: O₂, a small ion such as Na⁺, and a large polar molecule such as glucose. Defend the ranking using molecular properties.

3. Gradients provide direction—but movement remains molecular

Diffusion describes net movement resulting from random molecular motion when a concentration difference exists. At equilibrium, particles do not stop moving; instead, opposing movements balance so there is no net concentration change.

Equilibrium repair

Correct the statement: “At diffusion equilibrium, molecules stop moving because both sides are equal.”

4. Three transport categories

RouteProtein required?Relationship to gradientDirect cellular energy input?
Simple diffusionNo transport protein requiredNet movement down an appropriate gradientNo
Facilitated diffusionChannel or carrierDown an electrochemical/concentration gradientNo direct energy input for the transport step
Active transportTransport machineryCan move a substance against its electrochemical gradientRequires coupling to an energy source, directly or indirectly
Critical distinction: “uses a membrane protein” does not automatically mean “active transport.” Channels and facilitated-diffusion carriers are protein-mediated but can move substances down gradients without direct ATP consumption.

5. Electrochemical gradients matter for ions

For charged particles, concentration is only part of the story. Electrical differences across the membrane also influence ion movement. Together these form an electrochemical gradient. A concentration-only model can therefore be incomplete for ions.

Model limitation

A diagram shows equal Na⁺ concentrations on both sides of a membrane but does not show membrane voltage. Why is the diagram insufficient to predict net Na⁺ movement through an open selective channel?

6. Membranes communicate as well as transport

Membrane proteins can receive signals, recognize molecular partners, attach cells to one another or to extracellular structures, anchor cytoskeletal components, catalyze reactions, and help establish gradients. Carbohydrate-containing molecules at cell surfaces can also contribute to recognition and interaction.

Beyond the doorway

Name one membrane function that is not simply moving a substance across the bilayer. Explain how that function helps a cell behave as part of a larger biological system.

7. Transport prediction studio

For each scenario, predict the dominant route and defend it:

  1. A small nonpolar molecule moves from higher to lower concentration across the lipid bilayer.
  2. An ion moves down its electrochemical gradient through a selective channel.
  3. A polar solute moves down its gradient through a carrier.
  4. An ion is maintained at a concentration far from passive equilibrium by an energy-coupled pump.

Then change one condition—remove the protein, reverse the gradient, or remove the energy source—and predict how the outcome changes.

Cause-and-effect discipline: state exactly which condition changed and why that changes the predicted transport. Avoid “the cell compensates” unless you can name the compensatory mechanism.

8. Membrane model audit

Create a membrane model showing phospholipid bilayer, at least one channel, one carrier/pump, two different solutes, a concentration difference, and one receptor or adhesion component.

Annotate at least three limitations. Good possibilities include: not to molecular scale, static instead of dynamic, two-dimensional instead of three-dimensional, oversimplified protein diversity, omitted membrane asymmetry, or a concentration-only representation that ignores electrical gradients.

Portfolio model

Label one example each of simple diffusion, facilitated diffusion, active transport, and signaling. For every arrow, explain what determines its direction or outcome.

9. Mentor transport defense

Present one transport prediction to the Science Mentor. Ask the Mentor to change one variable—gradient, charge, protein availability, or energy coupling—and require you to update the prediction without changing unrelated assumptions.

Mentor and simulations support learning and revision. Complete independent mastery checks without answer generation unless a later assessment explicitly authorizes assistance.

Pathway choices

Exit mastery check

  1. Explain how amphipathic phospholipids contribute to bilayer formation.
  2. Why does Na⁺ generally require a transport pathway rather than freely crossing the lipid interior?
  3. Distinguish facilitated diffusion from active transport.
  4. What continues happening to molecules at diffusion equilibrium?
  5. Why can concentration alone be insufficient for predicting ion movement?
  6. Name one membrane function besides transport and explain its biological significance.

Mastery target: demonstrate at least 80% understanding and correct any transport-mechanism misconceptions before treating Day 6 as secure.
