Unit 02 · Day 13 · Matter & Energy Transformations

Photosynthesis & Cellular Respiration

Track carbon, oxygen, hydrogen, electrons, and energy through two linked cellular process families. Use equations as accounting models while avoiding the false ideas that plants do not respire, mitochondria “make energy,” or photosynthesis is simply respiration run backward.

60–75 minutesTier 1 · Model-BasedMatter–Energy MappingMastery ≥80%Portfolio Evidence

Learning objectives

  • Use balanced summary equations to account for matter in oxygenic photosynthesis and aerobic cellular respiration.
  • Distinguish matter conservation/cycling from energy transfer and transformation.
  • Explain the roles of chloroplasts and mitochondria without treating either organelle as an energy source.
  • Compare photosynthesis and respiration as linked but mechanistically distinct networks of reactions.
  • Predict how changes in light, substrate availability, gas exchange, or organelle function could alter system outputs.

Materials & tools

  • Notebook or printed lesson
  • Matter-and-energy mapping template
  • Colored arrows/tokens optional
  • Optional ecosystem photosynthesis/respiration simulation or cell-energy game
  • Evidence & Citation Studio for Extended evidence claims
Interactive connection: if using a simulation, predict the direction of change before adjusting light, CO₂, O₂, substrate availability, or organelle function. Record what the model assumes. Simulation behavior is evidence about the simulation model—not automatically evidence that every living system behaves identically.
Tier 1 · model-based

No flames, sealed living-organism chambers, chemical indicators, concentrated reagents, exercise challenges, breath-holding, or ingestion experiments are required. Use equations, datasets, diagrams, and approved simulations.

1. Photosynthesis is a reaction network summarized by an equation

In oxygenic photosynthesis, light energy drives reaction networks that ultimately support synthesis of carbohydrate from carbon dioxide and water while oxygen is released. A common overall accounting equation is:

6 CO₂ + 6 H₂O + light energy → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6 O₂

This summary is useful for matter accounting, but it compresses many reactions. Light-dependent processes generate chemical intermediates used by carbon-fixation reactions; glucose is not produced in one direct collision of CO₂ and water molecules.

Model precision: the familiar glucose equation is an overall bookkeeping model. Photosynthesis first produces smaller carbon compounds that can be used to build sugars and many other biomolecules.
Atom audit

Count carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen atoms on both sides of the summary equation. What does the balance demonstrate, and what does it not tell you about mechanism?

2. Chloroplasts transform incoming energy

Chloroplasts contain membrane systems and molecular machinery that absorb light and convert part of that incoming energy into chemical forms that can drive carbon fixation and biosynthesis. The chloroplast does not create energy. Energy transformations are constrained by conservation and involve losses to the surroundings as thermal energy.

Language repair

Replace “chloroplasts make energy for plants” with a scientifically stronger statement describing the energy source, transformation, and resulting chemical products.

3. Aerobic cellular respiration transfers chemical energy

Aerobic cellular respiration is another multi-stage reaction network. A common overall accounting equation is:

C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6 O₂ → 6 CO₂ + 6 H₂O + energy transferred to ATP and other outputs, with heat released

Glycolysis occurs in the cytosol. In eukaryotic cells under aerobic conditions, later stages associated with pyruvate oxidation, the citric acid cycle, electron transport, and oxidative phosphorylation involve mitochondria. ATP is generated through more than one mechanism, and not every ATP molecule in a eukaryotic cell is produced inside a mitochondrion.

Precision: mitochondria do not “make energy.” They participate in transformations that transfer energy from chemical substrates into ATP and other forms while energy is also dissipated as heat.

4. Plants photosynthesize and respire

Photosynthetic plant cells can contain both chloroplasts and mitochondria. Plants perform cellular respiration continuously as cellular conditions require; photosynthesis depends on suitable light and other conditions. Nonphotosynthetic plant cells, such as many root cells, still require cellular respiration.

Misconception repair

Correct: “Plants photosynthesize during the day instead of respiring, and animals respire because they cannot photosynthesize.”

5. Linked does not mean exact reverse mechanism

The overall equations can appear reversed: products of one summary equation overlap with reactants of the other. But the processes are not the same pathway running backward. They use different enzymes, membranes, electron carriers, intermediate reactions, and regulatory systems.

6. Matter can cycle; energy flows and degrades in usefulness

Carbon, oxygen, hydrogen, and other atoms can move repeatedly among organisms and environments. Energy enters biological systems in forms such as sunlight or chemical energy, is transformed through biochemical pathways, supports work, and ultimately much is dispersed as thermal energy. Energy is therefore not recycled through ecosystems in the same sense as atoms.

Matter vs energy

Trace one carbon atom from atmospheric CO₂ into a plant carbohydrate, into an animal or decomposer, and eventually back to CO₂. Then trace the energy associated with the same story. Why do the two traces have different shapes?

7. Build the chloroplast–mitochondrion systems map

Create a model containing light, CO₂, H₂O, O₂, organic carbon/glucose, chloroplast, mitochondrion, ATP/cellular work, and heat. Use separate arrow conventions for matter and energy.

For every arrow, label whether it represents:

  1. matter entering/leaving a process;
  2. matter being rearranged;
  3. energy entering;
  4. energy transferred into chemical form;
  5. energy used for cellular work; or
  6. energy dispersed as heat.
Do not draw a perfect closed loop for energy. Matter connections can form cycles across biological/environmental systems; usable energy requires continued input.

8. Perturbation studio

Use your systems map to predict first-order effects of one change:

  1. light becomes unavailable to a photosynthetic cell;
  2. CO₂ availability decreases;
  3. oxygen becomes strongly limiting for an aerobic eukaryotic cell;
  4. mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation is impaired;
  5. gas exchange between a leaf and atmosphere is restricted.

For each, state the directly affected process first, then one downstream consequence. Avoid claiming that every molecule or pathway immediately stops.

Causal boundary

Choose one perturbation. Write a direct effect → downstream effect chain and identify one variable that could change the outcome.

9. Evidence & Citation Studio — Extended claim audit

Investigate one claim that goes beyond the summary equations—for example the source of photosynthetic O₂, the role of chlorophyll/light-harvesting systems, ATP synthase, mitochondrial electron transport, photorespiration, or how a particular environmental factor changes photosynthetic rate.

In Evidence & Citation Studio, create the claim first, classify sources, preserve exact evidence locations, connect supporting/opposing/contextual/insufficient evidence, record limitations, and revise the claim so it remains no broader than the evidence.

Evidence discipline: do not use a summary diagram as proof of a detailed molecular mechanism that the diagram does not actually represent.

10. Mentor matter–energy defense

Present your systems map to the Science Mentor. Ask the Mentor to identify one arrow that could accidentally imply energy creation, energy recycling, matter disappearance, or photosynthesis as reverse respiration. Defend or revise that arrow.

Mentor and simulations support post-construction critique. The student's atom accounting, causal predictions, systems map, and exit check remain independent mastery evidence.

Exit mastery check

  1. Balance the overall photosynthesis equation and explain what balancing demonstrates.
  2. Why is “mitochondria make energy” scientifically inaccurate?
  3. Why can a photosynthetic plant cell perform both photosynthesis and respiration?
  4. Explain why photosynthesis is not simply cellular respiration run backward.
  5. Distinguish matter cycling from energy flow using one carbon atom and one energy input.
  6. Predict one direct consequence of strongly limiting oxygen for aerobic respiration without claiming that all cellular ATP production instantly becomes zero.

Mastery target: demonstrate at least 80% understanding and correct matter/energy or mechanism misconceptions before treating Day 13 as secure.

Portfolio evidence

Submit both balanced summary equations with atom accounting, the chloroplast–mitochondrion matter/energy systems map, one perturbation causal chain, one misconception correction, and—if completing Extended—the Evidence & Citation Studio claim/evidence record.
