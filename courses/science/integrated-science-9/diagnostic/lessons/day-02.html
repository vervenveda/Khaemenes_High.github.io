Risk, Safety & Ethics

Learn to classify activities by safety tier, scan for hazards, define stop conditions, and protect people, evidence, organisms, and environments.

45–60 minutes Tier 1 · Safety planning Portfolio Evidence

Tier 1 · Safety planning

Follow the written procedure. Stop whenever materials, supervision, health needs, or environmental conditions differ from the lesson.

1. Safety is part of scientific quality

An investigation is not scientifically successful if it produces data by placing people, organisms, communities, property, or environments at unreasonable risk.

TierMeaningExamples
Tier 1Independent home-safeObservation, models, metric measurement, public datasets
Tier 2Responsible-adult supervisionHeat, glassware, tools, low-voltage circuits, household chemicals
Tier 3Qualified laboratory onlyConcentrated chemicals, mains electricity, unknown microbial cultures

2. Risk scan

Before an activity, identify the hazard, who or what could be harmed, how exposure could occur, the control used, and the condition that requires stopping.

Scenario

A procedure asks students to heat water in a glass container. List at least four questions that must be answered before the activity can begin.

3. Ethical habits

  • Do not fabricate, hide, or silently change data.
  • Protect privacy and remove unnecessary personal identifiers.
  • Minimize disturbance during fieldwork.
  • Use organisms responsibly and follow applicable rules.
  • Report incidents and near misses.

4. Essential question & risk model

Essential question: How can a scientist reduce risk before collecting data, and why is safe practice part of evidence quality rather than an optional extra?

A hazard is a source of possible harm. Risk depends on both how serious the harm could be and how likely exposure is under the planned conditions. A control changes the procedure, equipment, environment, or supervision to reduce risk. A stop condition states when the activity must not begin or must immediately end.

Safety planning is scientific reasoning because changing a procedure can change both risk and data quality. A control that protects a person but also changes the measured system should be documented so the evidence can be interpreted honestly.

5. Worked risk-scan example

Consider a hypothetical classroom activity that proposes warming water in a glass container. Do not perform the activity from this example.

QuestionReasoning
What is the hazard?Hot water, heated surfaces, glass breakage, and spills could cause harm.
Who or what could be harmed?The learner, nearby people, electronics, surfaces, or property.
What controls are needed?Appropriate supervision, suitable heat-safe equipment, a stable work area, safe handling procedures, and separation from electronics.
What is a stop condition?Damaged glass, missing supervision, an unstable surface, unsuitable equipment, or any condition that differs from the approved procedure.

A scientifically responsible student does not “push through” a failed safety condition to finish an assignment. The correct action is to stop, document the reason, and use an approved alternative.

6. Vocabulary, ethics & misconception checks

  • Hazard: something with the potential to cause harm.
  • Risk: the combination of possible severity and likelihood under specific conditions.
  • Control: a measure used to reduce exposure or harm.
  • Stop condition: a predefined condition that ends or prevents an activity.
  • Near miss: an event that could have caused harm but did not.
  • Research ethics: principles that protect people, organisms, communities, environments, privacy, and the integrity of evidence.
Misconception check

Why is “I have done this before and nothing bad happened” not a valid safety argument? Explain how repeated success can coexist with unacceptable risk.

7. Retrieval, mentor safety review & portfolio evidence

  1. Distinguish hazard from risk.
  2. Name one control and one stop condition for a safe household measurement activity.
  3. Explain why fabricated or silently deleted data is both an ethical and scientific-quality problem.
  4. Explain why privacy protection can matter even when a dataset seems scientifically useful.
