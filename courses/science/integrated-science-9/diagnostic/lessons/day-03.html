4. Essential question & measurement model

Essential question: What makes a measurement scientifically useful rather than merely precise-looking?

A useful measurement connects a quantity, an appropriate tool, a stated unit, and honest reporting of variation. No measurement is perfectly exact. The markings on a ruler, the response time of a stopwatch, the calibration of an instrument, the way an object is positioned, and ordinary human judgment can all affect the recorded value.

Resolution is the smallest change an instrument can meaningfully display. Repeatability asks whether repeated measurements under similar conditions are close to one another. Accuracy concerns how close a measurement is to an accepted or well-supported reference value when such a reference is available. These ideas are related but not interchangeable.

5. Worked measurement example

A learner measures the same card three times and records 124 mm, 125 mm, and 124 mm.

  1. Raw data: keep all three values exactly as observed.
  2. Range: 125 mm − 124 mm = 1 mm.
  3. Mean: (124 + 125 + 124) ÷ 3 = 124.3 mm, rounded to a level appropriate for the measuring method.
  4. Interpretation: the small range suggests good repeatability, but repeatability alone does not prove accuracy.
  5. Possible source of variation: the ruler may have been aligned slightly differently on each trial.
Reasoning check

If a digital display shows 124.3000 mm, does the extra number of decimal places automatically make the measurement more accurate? Explain what additional information you would need before trusting that precision.

6. Vocabulary & misconception checks

  • Quantity: the property being measured, such as length, mass, time, or temperature.
  • Unit: the agreed scale used to report the quantity.
  • Resolution: the smallest change an instrument can meaningfully distinguish.
  • Repeatability: closeness of repeated measurements made under similar conditions.
  • Accuracy: closeness to an accepted or well-supported reference value.
  • Uncertainty: the recognized limitation in how precisely a value is known.
Misconception check

Correct the statement: “If I measure something three times and get the same answer every time, the answer must be accurate.” What could make repeated measurements consistently wrong?
