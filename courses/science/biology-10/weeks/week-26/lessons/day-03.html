Sequencing, Reads & Genomic Evidence

Explain how sequence data are generated, assembled and quality-checked before biological interpretation.

Safety, privacy and evidence boundary

Use paper models, public educational sequence examples, and fictional DNA. Do not perform unsupervised wet-lab amplification, collect personal DNA, upload personal genomes, or provide procedural gene-editing instructions. Privacy, consent, and dual-use risks are part of the science.

Explicit science instruction

DNA sequencing determines base order in DNA fragments. Modern workflows often produce many reads that are aligned to a reference or assembled by overlap. Coverage depth, base-quality scores, repetitive DNA and reference bias affect confidence.

A sequence difference is a measurement result first; its biological meaning requires additional evidence about location, frequency, function and phenotype.

Worked example with reasoning

Three fictional reads overlap: ACTGCTA, GCTAACG, and TAACGTT. Their overlaps support an assembled sequence ACTGCTAACGTT. If one low-quality read disagrees at a base while ten high-quality reads agree, confidence should reflect quality and coverage rather than simple vote counting alone.

Guided question

Why must sequence quality be evaluated before interpreting a variant?

Independent work

  1. Assemble four short overlapping reads.
  2. Mark one ambiguous base.
  3. Compare low versus high coverage.
  4. Separate “variant detected” from “variant has functional effect.”

Misconception check

Sequencing does not automatically produce a complete, error-free genome or a diagnosis.

Formative understanding check

What does coverage describe?

Worked response

How many sequence reads support or span a region/base, depending on the stated metric.

Week 26 biotechnology depth checkpoint

Phenomenon: Genome sequencing produces many short or long reads that must be aligned or assembled before biological interpretation is possible.

Vocabulary in context: sequencing read, coverage, reference genome, alignment, variant, consensus, assembly, quality score.

Worked example: Ten reads covering the same position do not automatically provide ten independent confirmations if they share the same systematic error source. Coverage depth improves confidence only when read quality and bias are considered.

Evidence-quality task: Compare a variant supported by many high-quality reads on both strands with one supported by two low-quality reads at the edge of read alignments. Explain which is stronger evidence and why.

Quantitative transfer: If 3,000,000 total bases are sequenced across a 1,000,000-base target, average depth is 3×, while local coverage can still vary greatly.

Retrieval: Connect sequence fidelity and error correction from Week 23 to sequencing confidence.

Sequencing uncertainty safeguard

Common error: High average coverage does not guarantee high confidence at every genomic position. Repetitive regions, GC bias, mapping ambiguity, and systematic instrument errors can create local blind spots.

Data-quality check: Compare depth, base quality, strand balance, and mapping quality before accepting a variant call. State which single weakness would make you most cautious and why.
