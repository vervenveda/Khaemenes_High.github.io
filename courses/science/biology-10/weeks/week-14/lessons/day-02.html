Positive Feedback, Termination & Feedforward

Distinguish negative feedback from amplifying feedback and anticipatory control without treating every biological response as the same kind of loop.

Weekly Classroom & EvidenceBiology CourseScience HallArchaemenesPrevious DayNext Day

Safety and evidence boundary

This week uses fictional organisms, classroom-safe models, and non-diagnostic datasets. Do not collect personal medical information, change medication, perform extreme-temperature exposure, deliberately alter blood glucose, restrict food or water, or use physiological measurements to diagnose health conditions.

Explicit science instruction

Positive feedback amplifies an initial change rather than opposing it. It is useful when a process must move rapidly toward completion, but it requires a stopping condition, depletion of a component, an external event, or another regulatory process to prevent indefinite amplification.

Examples often taught include blood-clotting cascades and contraction signaling during labor, but this course treats them only as conceptual examples—not as personal medical guidance. Feedforward differs from feedback because it acts in anticipation of a predicted disturbance before the regulated variable has fully changed.

Vocabulary: positive feedback; amplification; termination condition; threshold; feedforward; anticipatory response; gain; runaway process.

Worked example with reasoning

In a fictional wound-sealing model, activated cell fragments release a signal that recruits and activates more fragments. Activation therefore increases further activation. The loop is positive feedback. It ends when the damaged surface is sealed and the activating conditions decline. Without a termination condition, the model is incomplete.

Guided question

Why is “positive feedback increases the response” not enough to explain a stable biological system?

Independent work

  1. Create one negative-feedback and one positive-feedback diagram.
  2. For the positive loop, state the termination condition.
  3. Write one example of feedforward control in a fictional organism.
  4. Explain how feedforward differs from negative feedback.

Misconception check

Positive feedback is not automatically harmful, and negative feedback is not automatically beneficial. The labels describe whether a response amplifies or opposes a change.

Formative understanding check

A response begins before the regulated variable measurably changes because a predictive cue is detected. Is that feedback?

Show the worked response

Not necessarily. If the response is triggered by an anticipated disturbance before the regulated variable changes, it is better described as feedforward or anticipatory control.
