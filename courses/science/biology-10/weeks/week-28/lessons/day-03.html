Genetic Drift, Bottlenecks & Founder Effects

Explain random allele-frequency change and why small populations are especially vulnerable to sampling effects.

Safety and evidence boundary

Use fictional populations, cards, spreadsheets, or published aggregate data. Do not use population-genetic concepts to rank human groups or infer personal ancestry, intelligence, worth, or disease. Evolutionary mechanisms are population-level processes.

Explicit science instruction

Genetic drift is random change in allele frequencies due to finite sampling of individuals and gametes. It is strongest in small populations. A bottleneck sharply reduces population size; a founder effect occurs when a new population begins from a small subset.

Drift can increase or decrease an allele regardless of whether it improves fitness.

Worked example with reasoning

A population has A frequency .5. Ten individuals found an island population and, by chance, carry A at .8. The founder population begins different without selection. Later random sampling can push frequency further.

Guided question

How can an allele become common without being advantageous?

Independent work

  1. Run a coin/card drift simulation for N=10 and N=100.
  2. Compare frequency variability.
  3. Model a bottleneck.
  4. Explain why lost alleles may not return without mutation or gene flow.

Misconception check

Drift is not the same as migration; “genetic drift” does not mean organisms drift geographically.

Formative understanding check

In which population is drift generally stronger: N=20 or N=20,000?

Worked response

N=20.

Week 28 population-genetics depth checkpoint

Phenomenon: Allele frequencies can change by chance, especially in small populations, even when alleles do not differ in fitness.

Vocabulary in context: genetic drift, bottleneck, founder effect, stochastic change, effective population size, fixation, loss.

Worked reasoning: If only a small random subset of a population reproduces, the next generation can differ sharply in allele frequency by chance. This is strongest in small populations and does not require directional advantage.

Evidence-quality task: Compare a small-population simulation with a large-population simulation and explain why one run cannot establish a universal drift trajectory.

Quantitative transfer: Track allele frequency over several simulated generations and distinguish random fluctuation from a consistent directional trend.

Retrieval: Connect sampling variation to earlier probability and uncertainty work.

Drift interpretation safeguard

Common error: Genetic drift is not a force that intentionally favors rare or common alleles. It is random sampling change in allele frequencies, with stronger effects in smaller effective populations.

Quantitative extension: Compare two replicate simulations that begin at p=0.5 but end at p=0.42 and p=0.61. Explain why divergent outcomes are expected under stochastic drift and why many replicates are needed to characterize the process.
