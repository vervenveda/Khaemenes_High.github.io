Molecular Formulae, Ratios & Quantitative Biological Chemistry

Use simple molecular formulae and atom counts to compare composition while distinguishing quantitative description from conclusions about biological function.

Safety and evidence boundary

This lesson uses only symbolic formulae and invented or standard molecular examples. No chemical handling is required. Calculations describe representations; they do not by themselves prove biological function.

Explicit science instruction

A molecular formula records how many atoms of each element occur in one molecule. Quantitative comparison can reveal patterns, but the interpretation must match the data. For example, the formula C6H12O6 contains 6 carbon atoms, 12 hydrogen atoms, and 6 oxygen atoms. The atom-count ratio C:H:O simplifies to 1:2:1. That ratio describes composition only; it does not tell us how the atoms are connected or how the molecule behaves in a cell.

Percent composition can be defined in different ways. In this lesson we will use atom-count percent, not percent by mass. Atom-count percent = number of atoms of a chosen element ÷ total atoms in the formula × 100. Always state which definition you are using. For C6H12O6, total atoms = 24, so carbon represents 6/24 × 100 = 25% of the atoms by count.

Biologists often compare ratios, concentrations, rates, and proportions. A numerical difference can be scientifically useful without proving a mechanism. If two molecules have different carbon-to-oxygen ratios, that is a compositional observation. Explaining why their biological roles differ requires structural and experimental evidence beyond the ratio.

Vocabulary: molecular formula; atom-count ratio; proportion; percent composition; denominator; quantitative description.

Worked example with reasoning

Compare model molecule X = C3H6O3 and model molecule Y = C4H8O2. For X, total atoms = 3 + 6 + 3 = 12. Carbon atom-count percent = 3/12 × 100 = 25%. Oxygen atom-count percent = 3/12 × 100 = 25%. For Y, total atoms = 4 + 8 + 2 = 14. Carbon atom-count percent = 4/14 × 100 ≈ 28.6%; oxygen atom-count percent = 2/14 × 100 ≈ 14.3%. These calculations show compositional differences. They do not establish which molecule stores more energy or what either molecule does in a cell.

Guided question

For C2H4O2, calculate total atom count and the atom-count percent represented by oxygen. Then state one conclusion the number supports and one conclusion it does not support.

Independent work

  1. Calculate C:H:O atom-count ratios for C6H12O6 and C3H6O3 and simplify each ratio.
  2. Calculate carbon atom-count percent for C5H10O5.
  3. Explain why two compounds with the same simplified atom ratio need not be identical molecules.
  4. Write a two-sentence results statement that distinguishes numerical description from a mechanistic claim.

Misconception check

A formula ratio is not a biological explanation. It cannot by itself tell you molecular shape, bond arrangement, reaction rate, energy yield, or cellular function.

Formative understanding check

If two molecules have the same C:H:O ratio, does that prove they are the same substance?

Show the worked response

No. The ratio reports relative atom counts. Molecules can differ in total size, connectivity, bond arrangement, or three-dimensional structure even when a simplified ratio is the same.
