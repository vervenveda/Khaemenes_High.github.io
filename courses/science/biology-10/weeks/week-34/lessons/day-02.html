Fragmentation, Connectivity & Edge Effects

Explain how habitat geometry can alter movement, gene flow and ecological interactions.

Safety, ethics and evidence boundary

Use public/de-identified conservation datasets and safe non-contact observation. Protect sensitive species locations and community privacy. Conservation choices involve ecological evidence plus rights, costs, access, and community values; science informs consequences but does not erase civic or ethical tradeoffs.

Explicit science instruction

Fragmentation divides continuous habitat into smaller patches, often increasing edge relative to interior habitat. Effects depend on species mobility, matrix quality, patch size, isolation and edge sensitivity. Corridors can improve connectivity but can also carry disease, predators or disturbance.

Connectivity can be structural on a map or functional for a particular organism.

Worked example with reasoning

Two reserves each contain 100 hectares. One is compact; one is split into four narrow patches. The fragmented landscape may have more edge and lower interior habitat even though total area matches. A corridor can improve movement for one species but not necessarily another.

Guided question

Why is total habitat area alone insufficient to predict fragmentation effects?

Independent work

  1. Compare two landscape maps.
  2. Calculate simple edge-to-area ratios from geometric examples.
  3. Identify species-specific connectivity needs.
  4. List one corridor benefit and one risk.

Misconception check

A corridor is not automatically beneficial; function must be evaluated for target species and landscape context.

Formative understanding check

What is an edge effect?

Worked response

A change in environmental conditions or species interactions near habitat boundaries relative to interior areas.

Open-book formative practice only; not an official mastery attempt or grade.
