Mitotic Index & Quantitative Cell-Cycle Evidence

Calculate mitotic index from prepared-image counts and interpret what it can—and cannot—reveal about a cell population.

Safety and evidence boundary

Use prepared images, paper models, supplied datasets, or teacher-approved classroom microscopy. Do not culture unknown cells or microbes, collect human tissue, handle stains or preservatives without qualified supervision, or use personal medical records. This course does not diagnose growth disorders or disease.

Explicit science instruction

The mitotic index is the fraction or percentage of observed cells that are in mitosis at a given sampling time. A common calculation is: cells in mitosis ÷ total cells counted × 100%. It summarizes a population snapshot.

A higher mitotic index can be consistent with more cells occupying mitosis, but the interpretation depends on both entry rate and how long mitosis lasts. Sampling location, field selection, tissue type, image quality, and stage-classification rules also affect the estimate.

Vocabulary: mitotic index; sample; field of view; numerator; denominator; sampling bias; classification error; confidence; snapshot.

Worked example with reasoning

In a prepared image, 36 of 240 counted cells are classified as mitotic. Mitotic index = 36 ÷ 240 × 100 = 15%. A second region has 18 mitotic cells among 90 total: 18 ÷ 90 × 100 = 20%. The second sample has a higher observed index, but the result alone does not identify the molecular cause.

Guided question

If two tissues have different mitotic indices, what additional information would you need before concluding that one tissue's cells divide faster?

Independent work

  1. Calculate mitotic index for 27 mitotic cells among 180 total.
  2. Calculate it for 42 among 350 total.
  3. Compare the percentages.
  4. Name two sampling biases.
  5. Write one mechanism claim these counts cannot establish by themselves.

Misconception check

A high mitotic index is not automatically evidence of disease. Normal growth regions, developmental stages, tissue renewal, sampling choices, and differences in mitosis duration can all affect the percentage.

Formative understanding check

A sample has a mitotic index of 12%. Does that mean each cell spends exactly 12% of its life in mitosis?

Show the worked response

Not necessarily. That inference requires assumptions about a stable population, representative random sampling, similar cell-cycle behavior, and the relationship between stage duration and snapshot frequency.
