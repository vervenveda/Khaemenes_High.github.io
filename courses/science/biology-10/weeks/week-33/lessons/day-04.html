Disturbance, Succession & Community Change

Analyze recovery after disturbance as contingent change rather than a guaranteed march toward one climax state.

Weekly Classroom & EvidenceBiology CourseScience HallArchaemenesPrevious DayNext Day

Safety and evidence boundary

Use supplied long-term ecological datasets and low-risk simulations. Do not manipulate wild populations, introduce species, enter unsafe field sites, or infer a single cause from correlation alone. Population models are approximations with explicit scale and boundary assumptions.

Explicit science instruction

Disturbances alter resources, organisms and physical conditions. Primary succession begins where developed soil/biological legacies are minimal; secondary succession follows disturbance where legacies remain. Recovery depends on severity, frequency, dispersal, climate and surviving organisms.

Communities can follow multiple trajectories and may cross thresholds into alternative states.

Worked example with reasoning

After a fictional fire, Site A retains soil and seed bank and regains vegetation quickly; Site B loses soil and recovers slowly. Calling both simply “succession” hides different starting conditions and mechanisms.

Guided question

Why does disturbance history matter for predicting recovery?

Independent work

  1. Compare two before/after datasets.
  2. Classify primary/secondary contexts.
  3. Identify biological legacies.
  4. State one alternative stable-state possibility.

Misconception check

Succession is not always a linear, predetermined sequence ending in one permanent climax community.

Formative understanding check

What is a biological legacy?

Worked response

Surviving organisms, seeds, roots, soil structure, microbes or other remnants that influence recovery.
