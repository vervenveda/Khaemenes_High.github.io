Competition, Predation & Population Oscillation

Link species interactions to population dynamics without claiming every cycle has one cause.

Weekly Classroom & EvidenceBiology CourseScience HallArchaemenesPrevious DayNext Day

Safety and evidence boundary

Use supplied long-term ecological datasets and low-risk simulations. Do not manipulate wild populations, introduce species, enter unsafe field sites, or infer a single cause from correlation alone. Population models are approximations with explicit scale and boundary assumptions.

Explicit science instruction

Competition occurs when organisms reduce one another's access to limiting resources. Predation can create coupled population responses, but weather, disease, migration, vegetation and age structure also matter. Predator–prey models simplify these interactions into rates.

Worked example with reasoning

In a fictional 20-year series, prey rises first, predator rises two years later, then prey declines. The lag is consistent with coupled dynamics, but correlation alone cannot exclude climate-driven changes affecting both.

Guided question

What additional evidence would strengthen a predator-causes-prey-decline claim?

Independent work

  1. Graph paired predator/prey series.
  2. Identify lags.
  3. List direct and indirect mechanisms.
  4. Propose two alternative explanations.

Misconception check

Predator and prey cycles are not synchronized clocks; real populations often show irregular, multi-causal dynamics.

Formative understanding check

Does a lagged correlation prove causation?

Worked response

No. It is evidence of association that must be combined with mechanism and alternative tests.
