Competition, Predation & Population Oscillation

Link species interactions to population dynamics without claiming every cycle has one cause.

Safety and evidence boundary

Use supplied long-term ecological datasets and low-risk simulations. Do not manipulate wild populations, introduce species, enter unsafe field sites, or infer a single cause from correlation alone. Population models are approximations with explicit scale and boundary assumptions.

Explicit science instruction

Competition occurs when organisms reduce one another's access to limiting resources. Predation can create coupled population responses, but weather, disease, migration, vegetation and age structure also matter. Predator–prey models simplify these interactions into rates.

Worked example with reasoning

In a fictional 20-year series, prey rises first, predator rises two years later, then prey declines. The lag is consistent with coupled dynamics, but correlation alone cannot exclude climate-driven changes affecting both.

Guided question

What additional evidence would strengthen a predator-causes-prey-decline claim?

Independent work

  1. Graph paired predator/prey series.
  2. Identify lags.
  3. List direct and indirect mechanisms.
  4. Propose two alternative explanations.

Misconception check

Predator and prey cycles are not synchronized clocks; real populations often show irregular, multi-causal dynamics.

Formative understanding check

Does a lagged correlation prove causation?

Worked response

No. It is evidence of association that must be combined with mechanism and alternative tests.

Week 33 population-dynamics depth checkpoint

Phenomenon: Predator and prey populations can oscillate, but peaks need not occur at the same time because population responses often lag behind changes in food availability or predation pressure.

Vocabulary in context: competition, predation, oscillation, time lag, interaction strength, consumer-resource dynamics.

Worked reasoning: A prey increase can support later predator growth; increased predation can then reduce prey, followed by predator decline. Real systems also include refuges, alternative prey, climate, disease, and migration.

Evidence-quality task: Explain why two oscillating time series are not enough to prove a predator-prey mechanism.

Quantitative transfer: Estimate lag between prey and predator peaks from a graph and compare cycle amplitudes.

Retrieval: Connect food-web indirect effects from Week 31.

Interaction-cycle safeguard

Common error: Similar cycles do not prove predator-prey coupling. Two populations can track the same climate driver or resource pulse without one directly controlling the other.

Evidence extension: Compare lagged correlation, diet evidence, exclusion experiments, and shared-environment controls. Explain which combination would most strongly support a causal consumer-resource mechanism.

Consumer-resource evidence extension

Given paired predator and prey time series, identify the lag between peaks, then propose one alternative shared environmental driver. State what additional field or experimental evidence would help distinguish direct consumer-resource coupling from common-cause synchrony.
