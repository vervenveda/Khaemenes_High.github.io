Safety and evidence boundary

Use only teacher- or parent-approved low-risk investigations, supplied datasets, paper models, or simulations. No human-subject experimentation, personal health data, biological culturing, unknown environmental samples, hazardous chemicals, animal manipulation, ingestion studies, or medical claims are permitted. If a proposed investigation cannot meet these limits, convert it to a data-analysis or model-based study.

Explicit science instruction

A strong claim-evidence-reasoning argument is more than a conclusion followed by numbers. The claim answers the research question. Evidence consists of specific observations or analyses. Reasoning connects those observations to biological principles. A mature argument also states uncertainty, alternative explanations, and what further evidence would change the conclusion.

Multiple lines of evidence are most valuable when they contribute different kinds of support—for example, a quantitative pattern, a structural model, and an independent comparison. Repeating the same measurement many times improves precision but does not automatically create independent evidence for mechanism.

Vocabulary: claim; evidence; reasoning; convergence; counterevidence; alternative explanation; limitation; revision; confidence; portfolio.

Worked example with reasoning

Claim: In the supplied system, increasing light exposure is associated with a higher photosynthetic rate within the tested range. Evidence: the rate rose across four light levels, replicated measurements showed the same ordering, and the chloroplast-energy model predicts light dependence. Limitation: the dataset does not test whether light remains limiting at higher levels or isolate every other factor.

Guided question

What is the difference between adding more evidence and merely repeating the same claim in different words?

Independent work

  1. Write a one-sentence claim answering your Week 17 question.
  2. Cite at least two specific lines of evidence.
  3. Explain the biological reasoning linking each to the claim.
  4. Include one counterclaim or alternative explanation.
  5. State at least two limitations and one next test.
  6. Choose one artifact from Weeks 1–17 that shows the clearest growth in your scientific reasoning and explain why.

Spiral retrieval bridge

Try these from memory before reopening earlier lessons. Then check your reasoning and record one correction if needed.

  1. From Week 1: list the elements of a reproducible investigation record.
  2. From Week 12: distinguish raw data from processed/normalized data.
  3. From Weeks 13–16: choose one model and state a limitation it has.
  4. Write one sentence explaining why a model can be useful even when it is incomplete.

Retrieval is formative. The goal is durable connection across units, not a separate grade.

Misconception check

A polished report is not automatically a strong scientific argument. Reasoning quality depends on evidence alignment, transparent methods, uncertainty, and willingness to revise.

Formative understanding check

What would justify increasing confidence in a biological explanation?

Show the worked response

Converging results from appropriate methods, replication, independent evidence types, successful predictions, and survival of credible alternative explanations can increase confidence while never eliminating all uncertainty.

Open-book formative practice only; not an official mastery attempt or grade.

Teacher guidance

Foundation: use a CER organizer with sentence stems and one required limitation. Core: produce a complete evidence-based synthesis with counterclaim and revision note. Extended: compare competing explanations, rank evidence by diagnostic value, and propose the single most informative next study.

Preserve learning evidence

Keep the research question, model, method, data display, CER, correction record, and limitations in the matching Week 17 evidence field and semester portfolio.
