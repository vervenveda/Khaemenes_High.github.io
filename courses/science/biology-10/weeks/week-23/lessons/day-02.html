Base Composition & Chargaff Reasoning

Use complementary pairing to infer base composition while recognizing what percentages cannot reveal.

Weekly Classroom & EvidenceBiology CourseScience HallArchaemenesPrevious DayNext Day

Safety, privacy and evidence boundary

Use paper/digital molecular models and fictional DNA sequences. No biological samples, genetic testing, ancestry inference, or personal genomic data are required. Molecular models are representations and should not be treated as photographs of atoms.

Explicit science instruction

In double-stranded DNA, base pairing implies approximately equal molar amounts of A and T and of G and C. Therefore A + T + G + C = 100%, A≈T, G≈C for a double-stranded sample.

These relationships reveal composition, not sequence. Two DNA molecules can have identical base percentages and entirely different information.

Worked example with reasoning

If A = 30%, then T ≈ 30%. The remaining 40% is G + C, so G ≈20% and C≈20%. If G=18%, C≈18%, leaving 64% for A+T, so A≈32%, T≈32%.

Guided question

Why can't 30% A tell you where any adenine occurs in the sequence?

Independent work

  1. Solve four composition problems.
  2. Explain assumptions.
  3. Create two different 10-base sequences with identical composition.
  4. State what composition cannot reveal.

Misconception check

Chargaff-style equality is a compositional consequence of complementary double-stranded pairing, not a complete description of DNA structure or sequence.

Formative understanding check

If C=24%, what is G and what remains for A+T?

Worked response

G≈24%; A+T≈52%, so each is ≈26% under the model.
