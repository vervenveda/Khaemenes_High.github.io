Wavelength, Frequency, and Photon Energy

Calculate frequency from wavelength and compare photon energies.

Weekly EvidenceChemistry CourseScience HallArchaemenesPreviousNext

Explicit science instruction

Electromagnetic waves satisfy c = λν. For light in vacuum, c = 3.00×10⁸ m/s. Convert nanometers to meters before substitution: 1 nm = 1×10⁻⁹ m.

Photon energy is E = hν. Higher frequency therefore means higher photon energy. Since wavelength and frequency are inversely related, shorter wavelength corresponds to higher photon energy.

Vocabulary: wavelength; frequency; speed of light; photon energy; inverse relationship.

Worked example with reasoning

For λ=500 nm, λ=5.00×10⁻⁷ m. Then ν=(3.00×10⁸)/(5.00×10⁻⁷)=6.00×10¹⁴ Hz. A 400 nm photon therefore has higher energy than a 700 nm photon.

Guided question

Calculate frequency for 600 nm light and show the wavelength conversion.

Independent work

  1. Calculate frequencies for 400, 500, and 750 nm.
  2. Rank their photon energies.
  3. Describe one unit-conversion error that would change the answer by orders of magnitude.

Misconception check

Longer wavelength does not mean higher photon energy; wavelength and frequency are inversely related.

Formative understanding check

Which has greater photon energy, 450 nm or 650 nm light?

Reveal response

450 nm light because it has higher frequency.

Photon-energy transfer extension

Compare 400 nm and 800 nm photons quantitatively. Determine the frequency and energy ratios before calculating exact values, then explain why doubling wavelength does not double photon energy.

Dimensional-analysis and uncertainty extension

Quantitative spectroscopy depends on disciplined unit reasoning. Write the wavelength conversion explicitly before using c=λν, carry units through the division, and check that visible-light frequencies are on the order of 10^14 Hz. Then use E=hν and verify joules per photon. A result far outside the expected order of magnitude should trigger a calculation audit rather than immediate acceptance.

Measurement precision also limits the energy result. A wavelength reported only to the nearest nanometer does not justify an arbitrarily long photon-energy answer. Explain how rounding wavelength, using a rounded speed of light, and instrument resolution place sensible limits on significant figures. Distinguish computational precision from experimental accuracy.

Finally, compare ratios before exact arithmetic. Because ν is inversely proportional to λ, halving wavelength doubles frequency and therefore doubles photon energy. Ratio reasoning provides an independent prediction that can expose calculator or exponent errors.
