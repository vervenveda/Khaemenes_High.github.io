Intermolecular-Forces Synthesis

Defend a boiling or evaporation prediction using molecular structure, multiple IMF types, and data.

Explicit science instruction

A complete IMF explanation starts with molecular structure, identifies all relevant interactions, compares size and shape, and then predicts a measurable property.

Use matched data when available and state confidence. Strong reasoning avoids single-label shortcuts such as 'hydrogen bond means highest boiling point.'

Structure–property claims are comparative: the same interaction can matter differently depending on molecular size, number of sites, and geometry.

Vocabulary: structure–property; interaction hierarchy; matched evidence; prediction; limitation.

Worked example with reasoning

A small alcohol and an ether of similar molar mass differ in donor ability. The alcohol’s additional hydrogen-bond network can raise its boiling point relative to the ether, while both still experience dispersion.

Misconception check

IMF names are tools for explaining patterns, not a guaranteed property ranking by themselves.

Week 10 chemistry depth checkpoint

Phenomenon: Intermolecular-force models connect molecular structure to observable physical properties, but reliable predictions require competing factors and conditions to remain visible.

Retrieval: Integrate Week 8 polarity with this week's dispersion, dipole–dipole, hydrogen-bonding, boiling, and evaporation evidence.

Evidence-quality task: Separate IMF classification from measured property evidence and identify where molar mass, shape, or environment also matters.

Evidence revision: Correct one explanation that treats hydrogen bonding as the only attraction present or treats nonpolar molecules as force-free.

Transfer: Predict which of two unfamiliar liquids should evaporate faster and state the assumptions that make the prediction testable.

Model limit: IMF categories are explanatory tools, not exact quantitative equations for all bulk properties.

Week 10 synthesis challenge

Defend a boiling-point or evaporation prediction using molecular structure, all relevant IMFs, one quantitative data comparison, one confounder, and one explicit model limitation.

Integrated IMF reasoning extension

Use a layered comparison rather than a single-label rule. First identify molecular size and polarizability, then permanent dipoles, then hydrogen-bond donor/acceptor capacity, and finally shape or symmetry effects. Compare the complete interaction profile with the measured property instead of declaring one force “strongest” without context.

Competing-factor synthesis

For an unfamiliar pair of liquids, build the explanation in layers: molecular size and shape, permanent polarity, hydrogen-bond donor/acceptor structure, then the measured property. If the observed ranking disagrees with the first prediction, revise the model rather than selectively ignoring the data. State which factor is directly observed, which is inferred from molecular structure, and which remains uncertain. This makes the final CER a traceable scientific argument instead of an IMF-labeling exercise.
