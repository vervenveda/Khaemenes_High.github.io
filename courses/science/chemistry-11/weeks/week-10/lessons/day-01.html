Dispersion Forces and Polarizability

Explain why all atoms and molecules experience dispersion forces and how polarizability affects their strength.

Weekly EvidenceChemistry CourseScience HallNext

Explicit science instruction

Electron density fluctuates continuously. Temporary uneven distributions can induce neighboring dipoles, producing London dispersion attraction even in nonpolar substances.

Dispersion generally grows as electron clouds become larger and more polarizable. Shape also matters because extended contact can increase interaction opportunities.

Dispersion is present in polar molecules too; stronger named interactions are added to it rather than replacing it.

Vocabulary: dispersion force; instantaneous dipole; induced dipole; polarizability; contact area.

Worked example with reasoning

Within a homologous series, larger molecules often boil at higher temperatures because their larger electron clouds are more polarizable and support stronger dispersion attraction.

Guided question

Why can two nonpolar molecules have very different boiling points? Give two structural factors that can matter.

Independent work

  1. Rank three nonpolar molecules by expected dispersion strength using size data.
  2. Explain how shape can affect intermolecular contact.
  3. State one reason molecular mass alone is an incomplete predictor.

Misconception check

Nonpolar does not mean 'no intermolecular attraction.'

Formative understanding check

Do noble-gas atoms experience intermolecular attraction?

Reveal response

Yes. Temporary dipoles produce dispersion forces even in monatomic species.

Week 10 chemistry depth checkpoint

Phenomenon: Nonpolar particles can still attract one another strongly enough to condense because electron density fluctuates and induces temporary dipoles.

Retrieval: Connect Week 8 molecular geometry/polarity to the fact that all particles with electrons can exhibit dispersion forces.

Evidence-quality task: Distinguish permanent polarity from temporary fluctuations and induced dipoles.

Quantitative/model transfer: Compare boiling points within a homologous series and relate increasing size/polarizability to stronger dispersion attractions without assuming molar mass alone is causal.

Evidence revision: Replace the misconception that nonpolar molecules have no intermolecular attractions.

Model limit: Polarizability depends on electron distribution, shape, and environment; simple size trends are approximations.

Dispersion-force transfer extension

Compare two nonpolar molecules with different electron counts and shapes. Predict which may have stronger dispersion forces, identify the evidence supporting that claim, and state one factor that could complicate the comparison.

Polarizability evidence extension

Dispersion strength is influenced by how easily electron density can be distorted. Larger, more diffuse electron clouds are often more polarizable, but molecular shape also matters because surface contact changes how closely particles can approach. Long, extended molecules may experience stronger dispersion interactions than compact isomers of similar molar mass.

Use boiling-point or phase data as supporting evidence, not direct measurements of force magnitude. A property difference can reflect several interactions and structural factors at once.

Evidence-boundary note

When two molecules differ in several structural features at once, report the dispersion-force explanation as a supported model rather than a uniquely proven cause.

Dispersion reasoning studio

When comparing dispersion forces, hold as many structural variables constant as possible. Within a related molecular series, increasing electron count and a more easily distorted electron cloud often increase polarizability, but molecular shape changes the surface area that neighboring particles can contact. Compare a compact isomer with a more extended isomer and explain why similar molar mass does not guarantee identical boiling behavior. Treat boiling point as supporting evidence for an intermolecular-force model, not as a direct measurement of polarizability itself.
