Limiting-Reactant Data Investigation

Use supplied reaction data to predict product, compare with actual yield, and document uncertainty.

Weekly EvidenceChemistry CourseScience Hall

Safety boundary

Use paper-token models, supplied calculations, and simulations only. No unsupervised chemical reactions are required.

Explicit chemistry instruction

This investigation uses supplied or simulated data only. Preserve initial reactant quantities before any stoichiometric calculation.

Determine the limiting reactant, theoretical yield, actual yield, and percent yield in that order.

Compare the result with uncertainty and identify one model or procedural explanation for the discrepancy.

Vocabulary: reaction dataset; limiting reactant; theoretical yield; actual yield; percent yield.

Worked example

Reactant data predict 5.00 g theoretical product; simulated actual yield is 4.35 g, giving 87.0%.

Week 16 chemistry depth checkpoint

Phenomenon: Limiting-reactant and yield data can test whether experimental outcomes are consistent with stoichiometric predictions.

Retrieval: Combine Week 15 mass–mole stoichiometry with Days 1–3 limiting/yield reasoning.

Quantitative transfer: From raw reactant masses, identify the limiting reactant, calculate theoretical yield, compare with actual yield, and compute percent yield in one traceable chain.

Evidence-quality task: Preserve raw measurements and note which quantities are observed versus calculated.

Evidence revision: If percent yield is implausible, audit the limiting-reactant choice before blaming experimental technique.

Uncertainty: Measurement precision can affect both the limiting comparison and the final yield percentage, especially when reactants are close to stoichiometric equivalence.

Stoichiometric-data audit extension

Test how a small measurement change near the limiting boundary could switch which reactant is identified as limiting. Explain why this makes uncertainty reporting important.

Boundary-sensitivity extension

When two reactants are close to the stoichiometric ratio, small measurement changes can change which one is classified as limiting. Compare the calculated product predictions with their measurement uncertainty rather than treating a tiny numerical difference as absolute.

Repeat the calculation at the high and low plausible reactant values. If the limiting identity changes across that range, report the result as sensitive to measurement uncertainty and identify the measurement that most needs improvement.

Near-boundary check

If two reactants predict nearly identical product amounts, report that the limiting identity may be sensitive to measurement uncertainty. This is stronger scientific reasoning than claiming certainty from an insignificant numerical difference.
