Pressure, Particle Motion, and Kinetic Molecular Theory

Explain gas pressure and temperature using a particle-motion model.

Weekly EvidenceChemistry CourseScience Hall

Explicit science instruction

Gas pressure results from particle collisions with container walls. In the kinetic molecular model, particles are in constant random motion and intermolecular attractions are neglected for an idealized gas.

Temperature in kelvin is related to average translational kinetic energy. Raising temperature increases average particle speed, changing collision frequency and/or force depending on the constraint.

Ideal-gas models are approximations. Real gases can deviate at high pressure or low temperature when particle volume and attractions matter more.

Vocabulary: pressure; temperature; kinetic molecular theory; collision; ideal gas.

Worked example with reasoning

A sealed rigid container is warmed. Volume is fixed, so faster average particle motion leads to more frequent and more forceful wall collisions; pressure rises.

Guided question

Compare what changes microscopically when a gas is heated at constant volume versus heated at constant pressure.

Independent work

  1. Draw particle models for low- and high-temperature gas samples.
  2. Explain pressure without saying particles 'contain pressure.'
  3. Identify one condition where ideal-gas behavior becomes less accurate.

Misconception check

Temperature is not the amount of heat stored in a sample; it is related to average particle kinetic energy.

Formative understanding check

What creates gas pressure in the particle model?

Reveal response

Collisions of moving gas particles with container walls create the macroscopic pressure signal.

Week 11 chemistry depth checkpoint

Phenomenon: A gas exerts pressure because particles collide with container walls while moving continuously and randomly.

Retrieval: Connect Week 10 intermolecular-force reasoning to the idealized gas model, which assumes particle attractions are negligible under appropriate conditions.

Quantitative transfer: Compare pressure readings at two temperatures while holding volume and particle number constant, and predict the direction before calculating any ratio.

Evidence-quality task: Distinguish measured pressure and temperature from the kinetic-molecular explanation used to connect them.

Evidence revision: Replace any claim that temperature is the speed of one particle; temperature relates to the distribution of particle kinetic energies.

Model limit: Real gases deviate from the ideal model when particle volume and intermolecular attractions become significant.

Kinetic-model transfer extension

Explain why increasing temperature at fixed volume tends to increase pressure using collision frequency and momentum transfer, then identify one condition under which the idealized explanation becomes less accurate.

Pressure-mechanism extension

Pressure is a collective result of many collisions, not a force stored inside one molecule. Increasing particle number at fixed volume raises collision frequency, while increasing temperature changes the kinetic-energy distribution and average momentum transfer. Distinguish these mechanisms when explaining why two experiments can show similar pressure changes for different reasons.

Kinetic-model evidence extension

The kinetic molecular model links a microscopic distribution of particle speeds to macroscopic pressure and temperature. At one temperature, individual particles do not all move at the same speed; the distribution shifts when temperature changes. When explaining a pressure increase, distinguish more frequent wall collisions from greater momentum transfer per collision. Then identify the assumptions that make the ideal model useful and predict whether high pressure or low temperature should make real-gas deviations more noticeable.
