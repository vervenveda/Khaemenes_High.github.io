What Makes a Buffer?

Explain how a conjugate acid–base pair can resist pH change.

Weekly EvidenceChemistry CourseScience Hall

Safety boundary

Use buffer simulations and supplied datasets only. Do not use biological or medical dosing contexts or unsupervised chemical mixtures.

Explicit chemistry instruction

A buffer contains appreciable amounts of a weak acid/base conjugate pair.

Added H+ can be consumed by the base component, while added OH− can be consumed by the acid component.

Buffering reduces pH change within a limited capacity; it does not keep pH perfectly constant.

Vocabulary: buffer; conjugate pair; capacity; weak acid; weak base.

Worked example

A solution containing HA and A− can respond to added H+ as A−+H+→HA, reducing the free-H+ increase.

Guided question

Why must both members of a conjugate pair be present in useful amounts?

Independent work

  1. Identify three buffer/non-buffer mixtures.
  2. Write particle-level responses to added H+ and OH−.
  3. State one capacity limit.

Misconception check

A buffer does not neutralize unlimited acid or base.

Formative check

What two chemical components define a simple buffer?

Reveal response

A weak acid/base and its conjugate partner.

Week 31 chemistry depth checkpoint

Phenomenon: Buffers resist large pH changes because a weak acid/base conjugate pair can consume added strong base or acid.

Retrieval: Connect Week 29 conjugate pairs and Week 30 weak-acid/base equilibrium to a coupled system.

Quantitative/model transfer: Identify the weak acid and conjugate base (or weak base and conjugate acid) and write the proton-transfer reactions that neutralize added H₃O⁺ or OH⁻.

Evidence-quality task: Distinguish buffering from “holding pH constant”; buffers reduce change within a finite capacity.

Evidence revision: Correct the claim that any mixture of acid and base is automatically a buffer.

Model limit: Effective buffering requires appreciable amounts of both conjugate partners and depends on their ratio and total concentration.

Buffer-identity transfer extension

Classify several mixtures as buffer or nonbuffer and justify each decision from conjugate-pair composition rather than from pH alone.

Buffer-reaction extension

For a weak-acid buffer containing HA and A−, added OH− is consumed primarily by HA: HA + OH− → A− + H2O. Added H3O+ is consumed primarily by A−: A− + H3O+ → HA + H2O. The buffer works because both conjugate partners are present to respond to opposite disturbances.

A solution containing only HA may be weakly acidic, but it is not yet a strong buffer against both acid and base additions because the conjugate base amount is too small.
