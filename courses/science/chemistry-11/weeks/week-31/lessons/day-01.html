What Makes a Buffer?

Explain how a conjugate acid–base pair can resist pH change.

Weekly EvidenceChemistry CourseScience Hall

Safety boundary

Use buffer simulations and supplied datasets only. Do not use biological or medical dosing contexts or unsupervised chemical mixtures.

Explicit chemistry instruction

A buffer contains appreciable amounts of a weak acid/base conjugate pair.

Added H+ can be consumed by the base component, while added OH− can be consumed by the acid component.

Buffering reduces pH change within a limited capacity; it does not keep pH perfectly constant.

Vocabulary: buffer; conjugate pair; capacity; weak acid; weak base.

Worked example

A solution containing HA and A− can respond to added H+ as A−+H+→HA, reducing the free-H+ increase.

Guided question

Why must both members of a conjugate pair be present in useful amounts?

Independent work

  1. Identify three buffer/non-buffer mixtures.
  2. Write particle-level responses to added H+ and OH−.
  3. State one capacity limit.

Misconception check

A buffer does not neutralize unlimited acid or base.

Formative check

What two chemical components define a simple buffer?

Reveal response

A weak acid/base and its conjugate partner.

Mentor evidence conference

Ask Archaemenes to audit conjugate-pair logic, equilibrium response, and whether capacity claims match the data.

Teacher guidance
