Buffer Capacity & Composition

Interpret how amount and ratio of conjugate partners influence buffer behavior.

Weekly EvidenceChemistry CourseScience Hall

Safety boundary

Use buffer simulations and supplied datasets only. Do not use biological or medical dosing contexts or unsupervised chemical mixtures.

Explicit chemistry instruction

Buffer capacity increases with the total amount of buffering species available.

The acid/base ratio influences the pH region where the buffer is most effective.

Two buffers can have similar initial pH but very different capacities if their total concentrations differ.

Vocabulary: capacity; composition; ratio; concentration; buffering range.

Worked example

A dilute and a concentrated buffer can begin at the same pH, but the concentrated one can absorb more added acid/base before pH shifts strongly.

Guided question

Why doesn't initial pH alone determine buffer capacity?

Instructional depth extension · buffer capacity and component ratio

A buffer resists pH change because a weak acid/base pair can consume limited added H⁺ or OH⁻. It does not hold pH perfectly constant, and it can be overwhelmed when one component is depleted.

Buffer performance depends on both the ratio of conjugate pair components and their total amount. Two buffers can begin at the same pH but have different capacities if one contains much larger concentrations of both components.

Reasoning example: adding a small amount of strong acid converts some conjugate base into weak acid. The pH changes modestly while both buffer components remain abundant, but rises/falls sharply once capacity is exceeded.

Misconception check

Buffer capacity and buffer pH are related but not the same property.

Formative check

What mainly increases capacity at fixed ratio?

Reveal response

Increasing the total amount/concentration of buffering species.

Week 31 chemistry depth checkpoint

Phenomenon: Buffer pH depends mainly on the conjugate-pair ratio, while buffer capacity depends strongly on the total amount of buffering species.

Retrieval: Use Day 1 buffer composition and Week 30 logarithmic pH reasoning.

Quantitative reasoning: Use Henderson–Hasselbalch conceptually or numerically, pH=pKa+log([A⁻]/[HA]), only when its assumptions are appropriate.

Evidence-quality task: Distinguish ratio from total concentration: two buffers can have the same pH but very different capacities.

Evidence revision: Correct the idea that Henderson–Hasselbalch is a universal equation for every acid–base mixture.

Model limit: The approximation is most useful when both conjugate forms are present in meaningful amounts and activity effects are modest.

Capacity transfer extension

Compare two buffers with the same acid/base ratio but different total concentrations and predict which better resists a fixed addition of strong acid.

Worked Henderson–Hasselbalch extension

If pKa=4.76 and [A−]/[HA]=1.0, then pH=4.76. If the ratio is 10, pH=5.76; if the ratio is 0.10, pH=3.76. A tenfold ratio change shifts pH by one unit because the relationship is logarithmic.

Now compare two buffers with the same ratio but total concentrations of 0.10 M and 1.0 M. Their initial pH values can be similar, but the more concentrated buffer has greater capacity because it contains more moles of both partners available to consume added acid or base.
