Reading Buffer Curves

Use pH-versus-added-acid/base graphs to compare buffer performance.

Weekly EvidenceChemistry CourseScience Hall

Safety boundary

Use buffer simulations and supplied datasets only. Do not use biological or medical dosing contexts or unsupervised chemical mixtures.

Explicit chemistry instruction

A buffer curve shows how pH responds as acid or base is added.

A flatter region indicates stronger resistance to pH change over that range.

The point where the curve steepens signals that buffering capacity is being exhausted.

Vocabulary: buffer curve; slope; capacity; pH change; exhaustion.

Worked example

Buffer A changes 0.3 pH unit after a fixed acid addition while Buffer B changes 1.4; A shows greater resistance under those conditions.

Guided question

Which graphical feature best indicates buffering capacity over a tested range?

Independent work

  1. Compare two curves.
  2. Estimate where capacity is exceeded.
  3. Calculate ΔpH for three additions.

Misconception check

A perfectly flat line is not expected in real buffering data.

Formative check

What does a steepening curve suggest?

Reveal response

The buffer is approaching or exceeding its capacity.

Week 31 chemistry depth checkpoint

Phenomenon: Buffer titration curves contain a region where pH changes slowly because both conjugate forms coexist in substantial amounts.

Retrieval: Connect Week 30 titration curves with Week 31 buffer composition.

Quantitative/model transfer: Identify the buffer region and half-equivalence point, where [HA]≈[A⁻] and pH≈pKa under the usual weak-acid model.

Evidence-quality task: Distinguish a broad buffer region from a single exact “buffer point.”

Evidence revision: Correct the claim that maximum buffer capacity occurs whenever pH equals 7.

Model limit: Curve shape depends on acid/base strength, concentrations, stoichiometry, and temperature.

Buffer-curve transfer extension

Use a weak-acid titration curve to estimate pKa from the half-equivalence point and explain which graphical uncertainty affects the estimate.

Half-equivalence extension

During titration of a weak acid with strong base, half-equivalence occurs when half the original HA has been converted to A−. The two amounts are then equal, so the logarithmic ratio term is zero and pH≈pKa.

This point is useful for estimating pKa from experimental data, but the estimate depends on how accurately the equivalence volume is known and whether the system behaves ideally.

Buffer-range extension

The most useful buffer region is usually where both HA and A− are present in meaningful amounts, often within about one pH unit of pKa. Far outside that range, one component becomes too scarce for strong resistance in one direction.

Use the curve to distinguish pH position from capacity: being at pH=pKa identifies a 1:1 ratio, but total concentration determines how much strong acid or base the buffer can absorb.

Final curve check

Use the measured equivalence volume to locate half-equivalence before estimating pKa; do not assume it from visual symmetry alone.
