Language of Chemical Equations

Learning objective: Interpret coefficients, subscripts and physical state symbols without changing chemical identities.

45–60 minutes · Free · Offline capable · Public formative practice

Reading and understanding

Read an equation as a statement

Reactants are written to the left of the arrow and products to its right. A plus sign separates different substances. The arrow communicates a proposed or known chemical change under the stated conditions; it is not a claim that the change must occur instantaneously or safely at home. Chemists can add notes above arrows about catalysts, temperature and other conditions. An equation is a compact model, not a complete experiment report.

Multiply coefficients, preserve formulas

The formula H₂O represents one water molecule with two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom. The symbol 2H₂O represents two such molecules, for a total of four hydrogen and two oxygen atoms. A subscript lives inside the molecular formula and changes the substance when altered. Changing H₂O into H₂O₂ represents hydrogen peroxide, not 'fixed' water. When balancing an equation, change coefficients, never the underlying formulas.

What an atom ledger tells us

In 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O, the left contains four hydrogen atoms and two oxygen atoms; the right contains the same. There are three molecules on the left, but two on the right. This is not an error: the number of molecules does not have to match. It is the number of atoms of each element that must be the same. This difference is one of the most important habits for balancing equations.

Describe physical states honestly

The suffix (s) means solid, (l) liquid, (g) gas, and (aq) aqueous, or dissolved in water. These annotations communicate experimental context, but a formula alone cannot reveal reaction rate, final yield, or whether substances actually react. Even a perfectly balanced written equation can represent conditions that are not feasible in practice. Use equations for conservation bookkeeping and investigate reaction evidence separately.

Lab safety: These activities use data and symbolic chemistry only. Never seal a real gas-producing reaction vessel or mix unknown chemicals.

Vocabulary in context

coefficient
A number multiplying a chemical formula in an equation.
subscript
A number in a formula showing the count of an atom or group in that unit.
state symbol
Notation such as (s), (l), (g), or (aq) describing a substance's state.
chemical equation
A symbolic representation of substances and their relative amounts in a reaction.
formula unit
A representative unit of an ionic compound's simplest ion ratio.

Worked example

To balance H₂ + O₂ → H₂O, use 2H₂O on the right to account for both oxygen atoms in O₂. Then use 2H₂ on the left to account for four hydrogen atoms. Final: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O.

Independent notebook

Explain how the coefficient and subscript differ using 2H₂O and H₂O₂. Define all common state symbols and write a short explanation of why equation balancing alone is not proof of a reaction.
