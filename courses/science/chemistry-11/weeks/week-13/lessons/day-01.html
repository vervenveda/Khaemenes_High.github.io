Mixtures, Phases, and Separation Logic

Distinguish mixtures from pure substances and select separation methods based on physical properties.

Weekly EvidenceChemistry CourseScience Hall

Explicit science instruction

A mixture contains multiple substances physically combined. Components retain characteristic properties and can often be separated by exploiting differences such as particle size, solubility, boiling behavior, or affinity for a stationary phase.

Separation is a model-selection problem: the chosen method must target a property difference that actually exists between components.

A successful separation does not automatically identify the substances; separation and identification are related but distinct analytical goals.

Vocabulary: mixture; pure substance; physical property; separation; analytical method.

Worked example with reasoning

A mixture of two dissolved dyes cannot be separated by simple filtration because both pass through the filter with the solvent. A method based on differing affinities, such as chromatography, is more appropriate.

Guided question

Why is filtration effective for an insoluble solid in water but ineffective for two dissolved molecular solutes?

Independent work

  1. Classify four samples as mixture or pure substance from supplied evidence.
  2. Choose an appropriate separation principle for each mixture.
  3. State the property difference each method exploits.

Misconception check

A familiar separation technique is not automatically appropriate; the method must match the relevant property difference.

Formative understanding check

What should be identified before choosing a separation method?

Reveal response

The physical or chemical property difference that can distinguish the components.

Teacher guidance

Foundation: Match methods to obvious property differences.

Core: Defend separation choices from particle/property evidence.

Extended: Compare two plausible methods and explain which would produce cleaner evidence.
