Chromatography / Spectroscopy Data Investigation

Use supplied chromatographic or spectral evidence to separate, compare, and identify components.

Weekly EvidenceChemistry CourseScience Hall

Explicit science instruction

This investigation uses supplied or simulated data so the emphasis remains on analytical reasoning. Students preserve raw distances or signals before processing them.

For chromatography, calculate Rf values and compare patterns under the same conditions. For spectroscopy, use a supplied calibration or reference pattern.

A defensible conclusion distinguishes what the evidence directly shows from what is inferred about component identity or concentration.

Vocabulary: raw signal; processed data; reference; calibration; uncertainty.

Worked example with reasoning

Unknown chromatogram U has two spots with Rf 0.32 and 0.74. Reference mixture R contains spots at 0.31 and 0.75 under the same conditions. The pattern is strongly consistent, but measurement uncertainty should be reported.

Guided question

Which is stronger evidence of a match: one similar Rf value or a multi-component pattern under identical conditions? Explain.

Independent work

  1. Preserve the raw measurement table.
  2. Calculate all Rf or calibration-derived values.
  3. Compare unknown and reference patterns.
  4. Write a conclusion with uncertainty and one alternative explanation.

Investigation / Data Lab

Preserve raw distances/signals, processed ratios or concentrations, reference comparison, uncertainty, and one revision.

Misconception check

Processed ratios and concentrations should not replace raw observations in the evidence record.

Formative understanding check

Why preserve both raw and processed analytical data?

Reveal response

The processed result must remain traceable and reproducible from the original observations.

Mentor evidence conference

Ask Archaemenes to separate direct analytical evidence from inference and audit the uncertainty statement.

Teacher guidance

Foundation: Use a guided raw/processed table.

Core: Complete comparison and uncertainty analysis.

Extended: Evaluate an ambiguous unknown using multiple evidence streams.

Week 13 chemistry depth checkpoint

Phenomenon: Analytical conclusions become stronger when separation and detection methods provide complementary evidence.

Retrieval: Combine chromatography migration logic with spectroscopy calibration and pattern interpretation.

Quantitative transfer: Preserve raw migration distances or spectral signals, calculate normalized values, and compare them with standards using a stated tolerance.

Evidence-quality task: Track sample identity, blanks, standards, calibration checks, and any unmatched signal.

Evidence revision: If chromatography and spectroscopy disagree, investigate contamination, co-elution, calibration, or mislabeling before choosing one result.

Uncertainty: Converging methods increase confidence but do not eliminate measurement limits.

Analytical-method audit extension

Evaluate which method contributes stronger identification evidence for an ambiguous sample and propose one follow-up test that would be maximally discriminating.

Quality-control extension

Include blanks to detect background signal and standards to verify expected response. A method blank that shows a peak or spectral feature can reveal contamination from solvent, glassware, or the analytical system itself. A duplicate sample tests repeatability, while a spiked sample can reveal whether matrix effects suppress or enhance the signal.

These controls make disagreement interpretable instead of mysterious because they help locate whether the problem lies in separation, detection, calibration, or sample preparation.

Converging-evidence extension

When two analytical methods agree, ask whether they are genuinely independent evidence streams or share the same possible source of error. A mislabeled reference standard could bias both comparisons. When methods disagree, preserve the conflict and rank possible explanations by testability: contamination, co-elution, calibration drift, sample mix-up, or insufficient resolution. Choose the next measurement because it discriminates among those explanations, not merely because it repeats the same type of evidence.
