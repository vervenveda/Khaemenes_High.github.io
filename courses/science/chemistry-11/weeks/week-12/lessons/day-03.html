Dilution and Conservation of Solute

Use M₁V₁=M₂V₂ as a solute-conservation model for dilution.

Weekly EvidenceChemistry CourseScience Hall

Explicit science instruction

During an ideal dilution, solvent is added while the amount of solute remains constant. That leads to M₁V₁=M₂V₂ when both concentration terms use compatible units.

The equation is not a universal chemistry identity; it applies when solute amount is conserved between the initial aliquot and final diluted solution.

Always check direction: dilution increases volume and decreases concentration. If the calculation predicts both increasing, revisit the setup.

Vocabulary: dilution; aliquot; conserved solute; concentration; final volume.

Worked example with reasoning

25.0 mL of 2.0 M stock is diluted to 100.0 mL. M₂=(2.0×25.0)/100.0=0.50 M.

Guided question

A 10.0 mL sample of 1.5 M solution is diluted to 75.0 mL. Calculate the final molarity and perform a direction check.

Independent work

  1. Solve three dilution problems.
  2. For one, solve backward for required stock volume.
  3. Write the conservation statement that justifies M₁V₁=M₂V₂.

Misconception check

Dilution does not remove solute; it changes concentration by changing total volume.

Formative understanding check

Why does concentration fall during dilution even though solute moles stay constant?

Reveal response

The same solute amount is distributed through a larger solution volume.

Mentor evidence conference

Ask Archaemenes to check unit conversions, model choice, and whether the calculation matches the particle story.

Teacher guidance

Foundation: Use a before/after table.

Core: Solve forward and reverse dilution problems.

Extended: Identify cases where reaction or precipitation would invalidate the simple dilution model.

Week 12 chemistry depth checkpoint

Phenomenon: Dilution lowers concentration by increasing solution volume while conserving the amount of solute transferred.

Retrieval: Reuse Day 2 molarity and conservation reasoning before applying M1V1=M2V2.

Quantitative reasoning: Derive the dilution relationship from n=MV rather than treating it as an isolated shortcut.

Evidence-quality task: State that the relationship assumes the solute amount is conserved during transfer and dilution.

Evidence revision: If a calculated diluted concentration increases after adding solvent, audit algebra, volume assignment, and unit conversion.

Model limit: The simple dilution equation is inappropriate when solute reacts, precipitates, evaporates, or is not quantitatively transferred.

Dilution transfer extension

Design a two-step dilution from a concentrated stock. Show the conserved mole amount at each step and compare the final result with a direct one-step dilution.

Dilution calculation extension

Use a numerical check: transferring 25.0 mL of 2.00 M stock contains 0.0500 mol per liter ×? Audit carefully by writing n=M×V with V in liters: n=(2.00 mol/L)(0.0250 L)=0.0500 mol. If diluted to 100.0 mL, M=0.0500 mol/0.1000 L=0.500 M. The same mole amount must appear on both sides.

This explicit mole check catches common mistakes such as reversing initial and final volumes or treating added solvent volume as final volume.

Serial-dilution extension

For a serial dilution, verify each step independently with conserved moles in the transferred aliquot. Multiplying dilution factors should reproduce the overall concentration change. If the product of stepwise factors disagrees with the final molarity calculation, audit the transferred and final volumes before accepting the result.
