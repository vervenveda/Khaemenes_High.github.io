Solution-Concentration Investigation

Use supplied preparation or simulation data to compare target and actual concentrations.

Weekly EvidenceChemistry CourseScience Hall

Explicit science instruction

This investigation uses supplied mass, molar-mass, and final-volume data. Students calculate expected concentration, compare with a reference or simulated measurement, and explain discrepancies.

Preserve raw quantities before calculation. Report units and significant figures consistent with the supplied measurements.

A difference between target and measured concentration may reflect volume reading, transfer loss, incomplete dissolving, calibration, or model assumptions.

Vocabulary: target concentration; measured concentration; percent difference; calibration; uncertainty.

Worked example with reasoning

A target solution is 0.500 M while a simulated measurement gives 0.485 M. Percent difference relative to target is |0.485−0.500|/0.500×100=3.0%.

Guided question

Which raw measurement would most strongly affect calculated molarity if it has the largest relative uncertainty?

Independent work

  1. Calculate target molarity from supplied preparation data.
  2. Compare with a simulated measured concentration.
  3. Calculate percent difference.
  4. Identify one likely uncertainty source and one procedural revision.

Investigation / Data Lab

Preserve raw preparation data, calculations, target/measured comparison, uncertainty, and one revision.

Misconception check

Agreement with a target does not prove every procedural step was perfect; uncertainty still needs to be documented.

Formative understanding check

Why preserve both target and measured concentration?

Reveal response

Their difference is evidence about method performance and uncertainty.

Teacher guidance

Foundation: Use a calculation template with units.

Core: Complete target-versus-measured analysis and one revision.

Extended: Estimate which measurement contributes most to concentration uncertainty.

Week 12 chemistry depth checkpoint

Phenomenon: A concentration investigation is trustworthy only when mass, volume, transfer, and mixing are traceable.

Retrieval: Apply molarity and dilution reasoning from Days 2–3 to a supplied preparation dataset.

Quantitative transfer: Recalculate concentration from raw mass/mole/volume data, propagate a plausible volume-reading uncertainty qualitatively, and compare expected with reported concentration.

Evidence-quality task: Audit meniscus reading, volumetric-glassware choice, transfer completeness, contamination, and final-volume procedure.

Evidence revision: If the measured value differs from target, identify which error direction would raise or lower concentration.

Uncertainty: Report whether the discrepancy is large relative to measurement precision before claiming procedural failure.

Solution-preparation audit extension

Compare two preparation records and identify which one provides stronger evidence of the stated molarity. Name the single most consequential missing piece of information in the weaker record.

Preparation-quality extension

Distinguish precision from accuracy in solution preparation. Repeatedly reading a volume the same incorrect way can be precise but biased. A volumetric flask, pipette, or graduated cylinder also has different intended precision, so equipment choice should match the concentration claim being made.

Include one direction-of-error analysis: incomplete solute transfer lowers the actual concentration, overfilling the final volume lowers it, and stopping below the calibration mark raises it. Directional reasoning helps identify plausible causes of a discrepancy.

Error-direction extension

Distinguish random spread from systematic bias by asking whether repeated preparations scatter around the target or shift consistently in one direction. A consistent high concentration suggests a directional preparation error, while irregular spread suggests precision limits or variable technique.
