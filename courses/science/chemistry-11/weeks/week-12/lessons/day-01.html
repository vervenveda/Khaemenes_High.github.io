Solute, Solvent, and the Particle Model of Dissolving

Explain dissolving as a particle-level process distinct from melting or chemical reaction.

Weekly EvidenceChemistry CourseScience Hall

Explicit science instruction

A solution contains solute particles dispersed among solvent particles. Dissolving does not automatically mean a new substance forms; many solution processes are physical dispersions at the particle scale.

Whether a substance dissolves depends on the balance among solute–solute, solvent–solvent, and solute–solvent attractions. The phrase 'like dissolves like' is a useful shortcut, not a complete molecular theory.

Solubility and concentration are different. Solubility describes the maximum amount that can dissolve under specified conditions; concentration describes how much solute is present in a particular solution.

Vocabulary: solute; solvent; dissolving; solubility; concentration.

Worked example with reasoning

When sodium chloride dissolves in water, ions separate and become surrounded by polar water molecules. The particles are dispersed; the salt has not simply melted.

Guided question

How would you distinguish dissolving, melting, and reacting using particle-level evidence?

Independent work

  1. Draw particle models for solid solute, dissolved solute, and melted substance.
  2. Classify three scenarios as dissolving, melting, or reacting.
  3. State one limitation of the phrase 'like dissolves like.'

Misconception check

Dissolving is not synonymous with disappearing; the solute particles remain present in the solution.

Formative understanding check

Does a clear solution prove the solute particles are gone?

Reveal response

No. They are dispersed at scales too small to see directly but remain part of the solution.

Teacher guidance

Foundation: Use particle diagrams and vocabulary frames.

Core: Explain dissolving and distinguish solubility from concentration.

Extended: Evaluate a case where polarity alone does not fully predict solubility.

Week 12 chemistry depth checkpoint

Phenomenon: Dissolving reorganizes particles and interactions without making the solute disappear.

Retrieval: Connect Week 10 intermolecular-force reasoning to solute-solvent attractions and Week 6 ionic models to dissociation in solution.

Evidence-quality task: Distinguish dissolution from melting, reaction, and disappearance.

Quantitative/model transfer: Track particle counts before and after dissolving an ionic or molecular solute and identify which species remain conserved.

Evidence revision: Replace the phrase “water breaks everything apart” with a specific interaction model appropriate to the solute.

Model limit: Solubility depends on competing interactions, temperature, pressure for gases, and composition; polarity alone is not a complete rule.

Dissolution-model transfer extension

Compare an ionic solute and a molecular solute in water. Identify the particles present before and after dissolving and explain what evidence would distinguish dissociation from simple molecular dispersion.

Solvation-evidence extension

For an ionic solute, dissolving can separate ions and surround them with oriented solvent molecules; for a molecular solute, intact molecules may disperse without ion formation. Conductivity data can help distinguish these cases, but only when concentration and other dissolved ions are controlled. A clear solution by itself does not reveal whether the solute ionized.
