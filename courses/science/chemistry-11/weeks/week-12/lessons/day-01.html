Solute, Solvent, and the Particle Model of Dissolving

Explain dissolving as a particle-level process distinct from melting or chemical reaction.

Weekly EvidenceChemistry CourseScience Hall

Explicit science instruction

A solution contains solute particles dispersed among solvent particles. Dissolving does not automatically mean a new substance forms; many solution processes are physical dispersions at the particle scale.

Whether a substance dissolves depends on the balance among solute–solute, solvent–solvent, and solute–solvent attractions. The phrase 'like dissolves like' is a useful shortcut, not a complete molecular theory.

Solubility and concentration are different. Solubility describes the maximum amount that can dissolve under specified conditions; concentration describes how much solute is present in a particular solution.

Vocabulary: solute; solvent; dissolving; solubility; concentration.

Worked example with reasoning

When sodium chloride dissolves in water, ions separate and become surrounded by polar water molecules. The particles are dispersed; the salt has not simply melted.

Guided question

How would you distinguish dissolving, melting, and reacting using particle-level evidence?

Independent work

  1. Draw particle models for solid solute, dissolved solute, and melted substance.
  2. Classify three scenarios as dissolving, melting, or reacting.
  3. State one limitation of the phrase 'like dissolves like.'

Misconception check

Dissolving is not synonymous with disappearing; the solute particles remain present in the solution.

Formative understanding check

Does a clear solution prove the solute particles are gone?

Reveal response

No. They are dispersed at scales too small to see directly but remain part of the solution.
