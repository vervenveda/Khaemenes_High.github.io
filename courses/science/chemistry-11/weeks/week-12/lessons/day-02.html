Molarity and Quantitative Concentration

Calculate and interpret molarity as moles of solute per litre of solution.

Weekly EvidenceChemistry CourseScience Hall

Explicit science instruction

Molarity is defined as M=n/V, where n is moles of solute and V is total solution volume in litres.

Unit discipline matters: millilitres must be converted to litres before substitution. The denominator is solution volume, not solvent volume.

A concentration value is intensive: doubling both solute amount and final solution volume leaves molarity unchanged.

Vocabulary: molarity; mole; litre; concentration; intensive property.

Worked example with reasoning

A solution contains 0.50 mol solute in 2.00 L solution. M=0.50/2.00=0.250 mol/L.

Guided question

How many moles are present in 125 mL of a 0.40 M solution? Show the mL→L conversion.

Independent work

  1. Solve four molarity problems for M, n, or V.
  2. Perform a unit audit on each.
  3. Explain why 1 mol in 1 L and 2 mol in 2 L have the same molarity.

Misconception check

Using millilitres directly in M=n/V gives a result off by a factor of 1000.

Formative understanding check

What volume must appear in the molarity denominator?

Reveal response

The final total solution volume expressed in litres.

Mentor evidence conference

Ask Archaemenes to check unit conversions, model choice, and whether the calculation matches the particle story.

Teacher guidance

Foundation: Use a formula triangle and unit-conversion scaffold.

Core: Solve mixed molarity problems with units.

Extended: Analyze uncertainty when solution volume is reported with limited precision.

Week 12 chemistry depth checkpoint

Phenomenon: Two solutions can contain the same amount of solute but have different concentrations because their final solution volumes differ.

Retrieval: Use mole reasoning and unit discipline from prior quantitative chemistry before applying molarity.

Quantitative reasoning: Use M=n/V with volume in liters, convert between grams and moles when needed, and carry units through every step.

Evidence-quality task: Distinguish solute mass, solute amount, solvent volume, and final solution volume.

Evidence revision: Reject calculations that divide by milliliters without conversion or treat added solvent volume as automatically equal to final solution volume.

Model limit: Molarity depends on solution volume and therefore can change with temperature even when solute amount remains fixed.

Concentration transfer extension

Compare two preparation routes that yield the same number of solute moles but different final volumes. Predict concentration before calculating, then verify with M=n/V and explain the physical meaning of the result.

Molarity calculation extension

Work a complete example from mass to concentration: convert a supplied solute mass to moles using molar mass, convert final solution volume from milliliters to liters, then calculate molarity. Label each conversion factor so units cancel visibly. Before accepting the result, ask whether increasing final volume would make the concentration smaller, as the equation predicts.

Reverse problems are equally important. Given a target molarity and final volume, determine required moles and then required mass. This distinguishes preparation planning from retrospective concentration calculation.

Concentration-reasonableness audit

Before accepting a molarity result, estimate its scale from the particle story. The same number of moles in a smaller final volume must produce a larger molarity, while doubling both moles and final volume leaves concentration unchanged. Distinguish preparation quantities from measured quantities: a weighed solute mass and a volumetric-flask mark each contribute uncertainty. Report enough significant figures to reflect the limiting measurement rather than carrying calculator digits as if they were exact.
