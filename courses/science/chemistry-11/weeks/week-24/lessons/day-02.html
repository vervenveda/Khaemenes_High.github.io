Hess's Law & State Functions

Combine thermochemical equations to obtain the enthalpy of a target reaction.

Weekly EvidenceChemistry CourseScience Hall

Safety boundary

Use equation cards, simulations, and supplied energy data only. No combustion or reactive demonstrations are required.

Explicit chemistry instruction

Enthalpy is a state function: ΔH depends on initial and final states, not the route.

When equations are reversed, the sign of ΔH reverses; when coefficients are multiplied, ΔH scales by the same factor.

Adding valid reaction steps cancels intermediates and adds their ΔH values.

Vocabulary: Hess's law; state function; pathway; intermediate; thermochemical equation.

Worked example

If A→B has +20 kJ and B→C has −50 kJ, then A→C has −30 kJ.

Guided question

What happens to ΔH when a thermochemical equation is doubled?

Independent work

  1. Combine three two-step Hess problems.
  2. Reverse/scale equations correctly.
  3. Audit cancelled species.

Misconception check

Hess's law is not averaging pathway energies; it is algebraic addition of thermochemical steps.

Formative check

Why can different pathways give the same net ΔH?

Reveal response

Enthalpy depends only on initial and final states.

Week 24 chemistry depth checkpoint

Phenomenon: Hess's law works because enthalpy is a state function: overall ΔH depends on initial and final states, not the chosen pathway.

Retrieval: Reuse Week 23 reaction scaling and sign reversal.

Quantitative reasoning: Reverse, scale, and add thermochemical equations so intermediate species cancel and the target equation remains.

Evidence-quality task: Treat each algebraic equation operation as a corresponding operation on ΔH.

Evidence revision: If the chemical equations sum correctly but the enthalpy arithmetic does not mirror the same reversals/scaling, the Hess cycle is invalid.

Model limit: Hess's law is exact for properly defined thermodynamic states; experimental input values can still carry uncertainty.

Hess-law transfer extension

Construct the target reaction from two or three supplied equations and show every reversal, multiplier, species cancellation, and enthalpy operation.

Worked Hess-law extension

Suppose equation 1 must be reversed to align with the target; reverse its sign of ΔH as well. If equation 2 must be doubled, multiply both coefficients and ΔH by two. Only after those operations should the equations be added and intermediate species canceled.

Perform two audits: the summed chemical equation must equal the target exactly, and the enthalpy arithmetic must mirror every reversal and scaling operation. A correct numerical sum with the wrong target equation is not a valid Hess calculation.

Hess-cycle validation check

Write the target equation beside the summed intermediate equations and compare species, coefficients, and phases term by term. Only then finalize the enthalpy sum.

Path-independence extension

Build two different valid Hess pathways to the same target when data allow. If both use consistent state labels and thermochemical values, their summed ΔH values should agree within input uncertainty. This gives a powerful internal check on equation reversal and scaling.

Hess-path independence extension

Hess's law follows from enthalpy being a state function: only the initial and final states determine ΔH, not the imagined pathway used to connect them. Reverse an equation and reverse its ΔH; multiply an equation and multiply ΔH by the same factor; then cancel intermediates algebraically. Preserve physical state labels because changing H₂O(l) to H₂O(g), for example, changes the thermodynamic state and therefore the enthalpy accounting.
