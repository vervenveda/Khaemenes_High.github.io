Pathway Comparison & Energy Diagrams

Compare direct and indirect reaction pathways without confusing ΔH with activation energy.

Weekly EvidenceChemistry CourseScience Hall

Safety boundary

Use equation cards, simulations, and supplied energy data only. No combustion or reactive demonstrations are required.

Explicit chemistry instruction

Two pathways can share the same net ΔH while having different activation barriers.

Catalysts change pathway and activation energy but not the reactant/product enthalpy difference.

Energy diagrams should label reactants, products, transition barriers, and ΔH separately.

Vocabulary: pathway; activation energy; catalyst; reaction coordinate; ΔH.

Worked example

A catalyzed and uncatalyzed pathway both drop 40 kJ overall, but the catalyzed peak is lower.

Guided question

Why doesn't a catalyst change ΔH?

Independent work

  1. Annotate two energy diagrams.
  2. Compare barriers and net energy change.
  3. Explain one catalyst effect.

Misconception check

A lower activation barrier is not the same as a more negative ΔH.

Formative check

What quantity does a catalyst primarily alter?

Reveal response

Activation energy/pathway, not net ΔH.

Week 24 chemistry depth checkpoint

Phenomenon: Different reaction pathways can have different activation barriers while sharing the same overall enthalpy change.

Retrieval: Connect Week 21 energy profiles with Hess's state-function reasoning.

Quantitative/model transfer: Read reactant/product energy levels to identify ΔH, then compare pathway peaks without confusing activation energy with enthalpy change.

Evidence-quality task: Distinguish kinetic pathway information from thermodynamic state information.

Evidence revision: Correct any claim that a catalyst changes ΔH simply because it lowers the activation barrier.

Model limit: Reaction-coordinate diagrams are schematic and do not provide a full molecular trajectory.

Pathway-comparison extension

Compare catalyzed and uncatalyzed profiles and explain which quantities change, which remain invariant, and how Hess's law remains consistent with both.

Kinetics-versus-thermodynamics extension

A reaction can be exothermic yet slow if its activation barrier is large. Conversely, lowering the barrier with a catalyst speeds the approach to products without making ΔH more negative. This separates thermodynamic state difference from kinetic accessibility.

On an energy diagram, read ΔH from the vertical difference between product and reactant levels, not from the peak height. Read activation energy from the relevant barrier measured from the starting state of that step.

Pathway distinction check

Two pathways can have very different activation barriers yet identical overall ΔH. Use this distinction to separate kinetic accessibility from thermodynamic state difference.
