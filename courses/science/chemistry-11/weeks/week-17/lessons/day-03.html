Solution Stoichiometry & Equivalence

Use concentration and volume to determine reacting moles in solution.

Weekly EvidenceChemistry CourseScience Hall

Safety boundary

Use oxidation-number analysis, supplied datasets, and approved simulations. Any solution work should remain virtual or teacher-supervised with classroom-safe materials.

Explicit chemistry instruction

Solution stoichiometry begins by converting concentration and volume into moles using n=MV with V in litres.

Balanced-equation mole ratios then connect one dissolved reactant to another or to product.

At equivalence in a stoichiometric analysis, reacting amounts satisfy the balanced-equation ratio; equal volumes or equal concentrations are not universally required.

Vocabulary: solution stoichiometry; molarity; equivalence; aliquot; mole ratio.

Worked example

25.0 mL of 0.200 M NaOH contains 0.00500 mol NaOH. A 1:1 reaction therefore requires 0.00500 mol of the partner reactant at equivalence.

Guided question

How many moles are in 35.0 mL of 0.150 M solution?

Independent work

  1. Solve four n=MV problems.
  2. Use two balanced equations to convert solution moles.
  3. Explain why equal volumes do not necessarily mean equivalence.

Misconception check

Equivalence is a stoichiometric condition, not simply 'same volume.'

Formative check

What two pieces of information determine moles in a solution aliquot?

Reveal response

Molarity and volume in litres.

Week 17 chemistry depth checkpoint

Phenomenon: Solution stoichiometry connects measured volume and concentration to reacting mole amounts.

Retrieval: Combine Week 12 molarity with Week 15 mole ratios and Week 16 limiting reasoning.

Quantitative reasoning: Convert volume to liters, calculate n=MV for each solution, then use the balanced equation to compare mole requirements or product formation.

Evidence-quality task: Distinguish concentration and volume measurements from stoichiometric coefficients, which are exact model ratios.

Evidence revision: If a predicted equivalence point ignores stoichiometric coefficients, audit the mole ratio before changing concentration values.

Model limit: Equivalence is a stoichiometric condition; experimental endpoints may be offset by indicator behavior or measurement limits.

Equivalence transfer extension

Determine the volume of one solution required to react stoichiometrically with another and explain how the answer changes when the reaction ratio is not 1:1.

Worked solution-stoichiometry extension

If 25.0 mL of 0.200 M A reacts 1:1 with B, then n(A)=(0.200 mol/L)(0.0250 L)=0.00500 mol. A 1:1 equation therefore requires 0.00500 mol B. At 0.100 M B, the required volume is 0.0500 L or 50.0 mL.

If the balanced equation instead requires 2 mol A for 1 mol B, the required B amount changes accordingly. The coefficient ratio must be applied after converting measured solution data to moles.

Equivalence validation note

Before reporting an equivalence volume, recompute the reacting moles from both solution sides. The balanced stoichiometric ratio should be satisfied from either direction.

Unit-path check

Keep concentration in mol/L and volume in liters when computing moles. Then apply the stoichiometric ratio before converting back to a requested concentration or volume.

Equivalence-assumption extension

Equivalence is defined by stoichiometric mole ratios, not by visual change, equal volume, or equal concentration. Write the balanced equation first, calculate moles from each measured solution quantity, and only then compare the reacting ratio. When the reaction ratio is not 1:1, equal molar amounts are not equivalent. State whether any endpoint indicator or instrument response is being used as a proxy and distinguish that observation from the theoretical equivalence condition.
