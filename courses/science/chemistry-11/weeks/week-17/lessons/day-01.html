Oxidation Numbers & Electron Transfer

Use oxidation numbers to identify oxidation and reduction in simple reactions.

Weekly EvidenceChemistry CourseScience Hall

Safety boundary

Use oxidation-number analysis, supplied datasets, and approved simulations. Any solution work should remain virtual or teacher-supervised with classroom-safe materials.

Explicit chemistry instruction

Oxidation numbers are bookkeeping values that help track electron transfer. Oxidation is an increase in oxidation number; reduction is a decrease.

The species oxidized loses electron density in the bookkeeping model, while the reduced species gains it.

Oxidizing and reducing agents are named by what they cause in the other species.

Vocabulary: oxidation number; oxidation; reduction; oxidizing agent; reducing agent.

Worked example

In Zn + Cu²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Cu, Zn goes 0→+2 and is oxidized; Cu goes +2→0 and is reduced.

Guided question

For Mg + 2H⁺ → Mg²⁺ + H₂, identify what is oxidized and reduced.

Independent work

  1. Assign oxidation numbers in five simple species.
  2. Identify oxidation and reduction in three reactions.
  3. Name the oxidizing and reducing agents.

Misconception check

Oxidation does not require oxygen; it is defined by electron/oxidation-number change.

Formative check

What happens to oxidation number during oxidation?

Reveal response

It increases.

Ask Archaemenes to audit oxidation-number changes, mole accounting, equivalence assumptions, and uncertainty.

Teacher guidance

Week 17 chemistry depth checkpoint

Phenomenon: Redox reactions redistribute electrons, and oxidation numbers provide a bookkeeping model for tracking that change.

Retrieval: Connect Week 14 conservation and Week 16 stoichiometry to electron-transfer reasoning.

Quantitative/model transfer: Assign oxidation numbers systematically, verify that totals match the species charge, and compare reactant/product values to identify oxidation and reduction.

Evidence-quality task: Distinguish oxidation-number bookkeeping from literal localized electron position in covalent compounds.

Evidence revision: Correct any rule application that produces impossible total charge or labels the same species as both oxidized and reduced without justification.

Model limit: Oxidation states are formal electron-accounting tools; they are not always equal to measured partial charges.

Redox identification transfer extension

Analyze a reaction containing several elements, identify which oxidation numbers change, name oxidizing and reducing agents, and justify the labels from electron bookkeeping rather than memorized species names.

Worked oxidation-state extension

In Fe2O3, oxygen is assigned −2, so three oxygens contribute −6 total. The two iron atoms therefore contribute +6 total, giving +3 for each iron. In a reaction where Fe3+ becomes Fe2+, the oxidation number decreases by one, representing reduction in the bookkeeping model.

Use total-charge checks every time. For polyatomic ions, the sum of assigned oxidation numbers must equal the ion charge, not zero unless the species is neutral.

Oxidation-state validation note

After assigning oxidation numbers, sum them to the overall species charge. This quick validation catches many bookkeeping errors before they propagate into redox identification.

Oxidation-state validation extension

After assigning oxidation numbers, perform a charge-sum audit for every species before interpreting redox change. In a neutral compound the assigned values must sum to zero; in an ion they must sum to the ionic charge. Then compare only the elements whose oxidation states actually change. This guards against rule-memory errors and reinforces that oxidation numbers are formal bookkeeping devices rather than literal measured charges.
