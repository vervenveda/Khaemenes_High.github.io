Calorimetry & q=mcΔT

Calculate heat transfer from mass, specific heat, and temperature change.

Weekly EvidenceChemistry CourseScience Hall

Safety boundary

Use virtual calorimetry or teacher-supervised water-based calorimetry only. No unsupervised flames, reactive chemicals, or improvised heat sources.

Explicit chemistry instruction

For a substance with approximately constant specific heat, q=mcΔT.

Mass must match the units of specific heat, and ΔT=T_final−T_initial.

In simple calorimetry, heat lost by one part is approximated as heat gained by another when container/environment losses are negligible.

Vocabulary: calorimetry; specific heat; temperature change; energy conservation.

Worked example

100 g water warms by 5.0°C. With c=4.18 J/(g·°C), q=100×4.18×5.0=2090 J.

Guided question

A 50 g water sample cools by 8°C. Calculate q for the water and interpret the sign.

Independent work

  1. Solve four q=mcΔT problems.
  2. Perform a unit/sign audit.
  3. Write one assumption behind ideal calorimetry.

Misconception check

A negative ΔT does not mean negative temperature; it means the sample cooled.

Formative check

What determines the sign of q in q=mcΔT?

Reveal response

The sign of ΔT under the chosen convention.

Week 23 chemistry depth checkpoint

Phenomenon: Calorimetry estimates energy transfer from measurable mass and temperature change.

Retrieval: Reuse Day 1 system/surroundings reasoning before applying q=mcΔT.

Quantitative reasoning: Calculate ΔT=Tfinal−Tinitial, track mass and specific-heat units, and preserve the sign of temperature change.

Evidence-quality task: Separate heat gained by the calorimeter contents from heat lost or gained by the reaction system.

Evidence revision: If the solution warms during an exothermic reaction, qsolution is positive while qreaction is negative under the ideal energy-balance model.

Model limit: Simple coffee-cup calorimetry assumes negligible heat loss, known heat capacity, and adequate mixing.

Calorimetry transfer extension

Use q=mcΔT to compute heat absorbed by a solution, then infer reaction heat with the opposite sign and state which assumptions make that inference valid.

Worked calorimetry extension

For 100.0 g of water-like solution with c=4.184 J·g⁻¹·°C⁻¹ and ΔT=6.5°C, q=(100.0)(4.184)(6.5)=2.72×10³ J, or 2.72 kJ absorbed by the solution. If the reaction is the only significant energy source, qreaction≈−2.72 kJ.

Check units explicitly: g cancels g⁻¹ and °C cancels °C⁻¹, leaving joules. If calorimeter heat capacity is supplied, include qcal=CcalΔT rather than assuming the apparatus absorbs no energy.
