Calorimetry Investigation

Use virtual or supervised water calorimetry to estimate an energy change and evaluate heat-loss uncertainty.

Safety boundary

Use virtual calorimetry or teacher-supervised water-based calorimetry only. No unsupervised flames, reactive chemicals, or improvised heat sources.

Explicit chemistry instruction

Preserve raw mass and temperature observations before calculating heat.

Use q=mcΔT for the water/surroundings model, then infer the opposite sign for the reacting system when appropriate.

Compare repeated trials and discuss container heat capacity, environmental exchange, and sensor resolution.

Vocabulary: raw data; calorimeter; heat loss; trial; uncertainty.

Worked example

If water gains +2.1 kJ, an ideal two-part model assigns about −2.1 kJ to the reaction system.

Guided question

Which uncontrolled pathway would make the measured water heat smaller than the true released heat?

Week 23 chemistry depth checkpoint

Phenomenon: A calorimetry investigation tests whether measured temperature changes support a defensible energy-transfer estimate.

Retrieval: Combine q=mcΔT with system/surroundings signs and molar enthalpy reasoning.

Quantitative transfer: Preserve raw mass and temperature readings, calculate ΔT and q, then convert to ΔH per mole if reaction amount is known.

Evidence-quality task: Audit heat loss, thermometer resolution, calorimeter heat capacity, incomplete reaction, evaporation, and mixing.

Evidence revision: If replicate ΔH values drift, test for systematic heat loss or inconsistent starting conditions before averaging blindly.

Uncertainty: Small ΔT values can produce large relative uncertainty in q.

Calorimetry-investigation extension

Compare two trials, identify which is more trustworthy, and explain which experimental improvement would most reduce uncertainty in the calculated enthalpy.

Calorimetry quality-control extension

Use a temperature-versus-time record when possible. If the peak temperature is missed because measurement begins too late or the system cools during reading, the calculated |q| and |ΔH| will be underestimated. Extrapolating back toward the mixing time can sometimes reduce this bias.

Compare replicate ΔH values and calculate percent difference or spread. Consistent low-magnitude values across trials suggest systematic heat loss more strongly than random thermometer noise.

Heat-loss diagnostic check

If repeated trials all produce ΔH magnitudes smaller than a trusted reference, systematic heat loss is more plausible than random scatter alone. Use the direction of error to evaluate causes.

Heat-loss direction audit

Use the direction of discrepancy to diagnose likely bias. In an exothermic process, heat lost to the room or absorbed by unaccounted apparatus usually makes the measured temperature rise too small, causing the calculated magnitude of ΔH to be underestimated. A consistent low-magnitude result across replicates therefore points toward systematic loss more strongly than random scatter. State which bias direction each proposed error should produce before deciding which explanation is plausible.
