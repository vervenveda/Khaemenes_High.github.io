Experimental Design & Controls

Design a kinetics test that isolates one variable and preserves reproducibility.

Safety boundary

Use supplied datasets and approved simulations. Any physical demonstration must be teacher-supervised and explicitly classroom-safe; no improvised reactive mixtures.

Explicit chemistry instruction

Change one target variable while holding relevant others constant.

Specify the observable used to estimate rate, the measurement interval, and how repeated trials will be handled.

A design should include a plan for uncertainty and data preservation before data collection.

Vocabulary: control; repeatability; measurement plan; uncertainty; reproducibility.

To test temperature, keep concentration, volume, surface area, and measurement method fixed across trials.

Week 22 chemistry depth checkpoint

Phenomenon: Experimental design determines whether a rate comparison supports a causal conclusion.

Retrieval: Reuse Weeks 19–20 controlled-variable reasoning and Week 22 Day 1 competing predictions.

Quantitative reasoning: Define the manipulated variable, response variable, control condition, replicate plan, and analysis metric before collecting data.

Evidence-quality task: Hold temperature, concentration, mixing, surface area, catalyst amount, and timing procedure constant unless one is intentionally manipulated.

Evidence revision: If multiple variables change, redesign the experiment rather than overinterpreting the result.

Uncertainty: Replication estimates random variation; controls address alternative explanations.

Design-quality extension

Audit a flawed kinetics experiment and identify at least three confounders. Rewrite the design so one causal comparison becomes interpretable.

Full experimental-design extension

Write the design before collecting data: define the independent variable, dependent rate measure, controlled variables, baseline condition, replicate count, timing procedure, sample preparation, and analysis method. Include a prediction for each competing model.

Use at least three levels of the manipulated variable when possible rather than only “low” and “high.” A concentration series can reveal linear, quadratic, threshold, or saturating behavior that a two-point comparison cannot.

Randomize or alternate trial order when drift is plausible. If temperature, sensor response, or reagent condition changes over time, running all low-condition trials first and high-condition trials later can create a false treatment effect.

Plan the quality controls as part of the experiment, not after it: blanks detect background response, replicate trials estimate random variation, and a known-condition check verifies that the measurement method behaves as expected.

Design-replication note

Include enough replicate trials to estimate random variation and enough variable levels to reveal whether the response is linear or more complex.

Design-power extension

A strong inquiry design uses enough variable levels and replicates to distinguish the competing predictions. Two points may show a difference but cannot reveal whether the relationship is linear, curved, saturating, or threshold-like. Plan at least three levels when feasible, define replicate handling in advance, and randomize or alternate trial order when drift is plausible. The design should be powerful enough to discriminate models, not merely capable of generating data.
