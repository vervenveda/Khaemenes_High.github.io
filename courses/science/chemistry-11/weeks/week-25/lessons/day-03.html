Energy vs Entropy Tradeoffs

Explain why enthalpy alone cannot predict every spontaneous direction.

Weekly EvidenceChemistry CourseScience Hall

Safety boundary

Use particle-distribution simulations and supplied data only. No new chemical mixing is required.

Explicit chemistry instruction

Some endothermic processes can proceed because entropy gain is large enough.

Some exothermic processes may still be limited by other thermodynamic factors.

Spontaneity is a thermodynamic direction statement, not a speed statement.

Vocabulary: spontaneity; enthalpy; entropy; direction; kinetics.

Worked example

Ice can melt above its melting point even though melting absorbs heat because the combined thermodynamic balance favors the liquid state.

Guided question

Why can a spontaneous process be slow?

  2. Separate spontaneity from rate.
  3. Write one bounded direction claim.

Misconception check

Spontaneous does not mean instantaneous.

Reveal response

Kinetics/activation barriers.

Week 25 chemistry depth checkpoint

Phenomenon: Chemical direction reflects a tradeoff between enthalpy and entropy rather than a single “energy downhill” rule.

Retrieval: Integrate Week 24 ΔH with Days 1–2 entropy direction.

Quantitative reasoning: Use ΔG=ΔH−TΔS conceptually and numerically with consistent units, converting entropy to compatible energy units when needed.

Evidence-quality task: Distinguish thermodynamic favorability from reaction rate.

Evidence revision: Correct the misconception that negative ΔH guarantees spontaneity or that positive ΔG means a reaction is impossible.

Model limit: Standard-state ΔG predictions depend on temperature and composition assumptions; actual conditions can shift direction.

Free-energy transfer extension

Classify the four sign combinations of ΔH and ΔS and explain which are always favorable, never favorable, or temperature dependent under the simplified standard-state model.

Worked free-energy extension

Suppose ΔH=+20 kJ/mol and ΔS=+100 J·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹. Convert ΔS to 0.100 kJ·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹ before using ΔG=ΔH−TΔS. At 298 K, TΔS=29.8 kJ/mol, so ΔG≈−9.8 kJ/mol. The process is thermodynamically favored under those stated conditions even though it is endothermic.

At lower temperature, the entropy term contributes less. This is why sign combinations involving positive ΔH and positive ΔS can be temperature dependent.

ΔG unit-and-sign extension

Before combining ΔH and TΔS, convert units so both terms use the same energy basis. Then interpret sign carefully: ΔG<0 indicates thermodynamic favorability in the forward direction under the stated conditions, ΔG>0 favors the reverse direction, and ΔG=0 corresponds to equilibrium. None of these signs directly states how fast the process occurs.

Temperature-threshold check

When ΔH and ΔS have the same sign, solve ΔG=0 to estimate the temperature where direction changes, using compatible units throughout.

Final ΔG note

Check that ΔH and TΔS use compatible units and that the final sign is interpreted for the stated reaction direction.

Final unit check

Convert entropy units before calculating TΔS, then verify that the resulting ΔG units match the enthalpy units used in the same expression.

Free-energy unit audit

Before using ΔG=ΔH−TΔS, put ΔH and TΔS on the same energy basis. Entropy data are often reported in J·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹ while enthalpy is in kJ·mol⁻¹, so a factor-of-1000 error is easy to introduce. After computing ΔG, interpret the sign for the reaction as written and under the stated conditions. Then separately state whether kinetics could still make the thermodynamically favored process slow.
