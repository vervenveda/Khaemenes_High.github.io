Phase Change & Entropy

Relate phase and temperature to qualitative entropy trends.

Weekly EvidenceChemistry CourseScience Hall

Safety boundary

Use particle-distribution simulations and supplied data only. No new chemical mixing is required.

Explicit chemistry instruction

Solids constrain particle positions more than liquids; gases generally allow many more positional arrangements.

Melting and vaporization therefore usually increase entropy of the substance.

Cooling or condensation can reduce entropy locally while total surroundings/system effects still matter.

Vocabulary: phase; entropy change; melting; vaporization; surroundings.

Worked example

Liquid water becoming vapor greatly increases positional freedom and typically increases entropy of the water.

Guided question

Does freezing always mean the entropy of the entire universe decreases?

Independent work

  1. Rank solid/liquid/gas entropy.
  2. Analyze three phase changes.
  3. Distinguish system from surroundings.

Misconception check

A local entropy decrease can occur while total entropy still increases overall.

Formative check

Which phase usually has greatest molecular positional freedom?

Reveal response

Gas.

Week 25 chemistry depth checkpoint

Phenomenon: Phase changes alter particle freedom and the number of accessible arrangements.

Retrieval: Use Day 1 dispersal reasoning and Week 23 heat-flow concepts together.

Quantitative/model transfer: Rank solid, liquid, and gas states qualitatively by entropy under comparable conditions and connect melting/vaporization to increased accessible motion.

Evidence-quality task: Distinguish phase-state entropy from heat absorbed during the transition; related does not mean identical.

Evidence revision: Correct the idea that freezing is “forbidden” because system entropy decreases; surroundings entropy can compensate.

Model limit: Simple phase ranking is useful but actual entropy values depend on substance, temperature, pressure, and molecular complexity.

Phase-entropy transfer extension

Explain why vaporization generally increases system entropy while condensation decreases it, then state what must be considered before predicting spontaneous direction at a given temperature.

Phase-change thermodynamics extension

At a phase-transition temperature, system entropy and surroundings entropy can offset so the two phases coexist at equilibrium. Below or above that temperature, the balance changes. This helps explain why melting can be favorable at one temperature and freezing at another without violating thermodynamic principles.

Keep pressure conditions visible as well, because phase stability is not determined by temperature alone.

Phase-transition balance extension

At the normal melting point, solid and liquid are in equilibrium and ΔG for the phase change is zero. Because ΔG=ΔH−TΔS, the transition temperature reflects a balance between enthalpy and entropy terms. This makes phase-change spontaneity a condition-dependent statement rather than an absolute property of melting or freezing.

Phase-equilibrium check

At coexistence, neither phase has a net thermodynamic advantage. A small temperature change can tip the balance because the enthalpy and entropy terms respond differently to T.

Final phase note

Always state temperature and pressure conditions when judging which phase is thermodynamically favored.

Phase-equilibrium extension

At a phase transition under fixed pressure, the forward and reverse phases can coexist when ΔG for the transition is zero. This does not mean ΔH or ΔS are zero; rather, the enthalpy and entropy contributions balance at that temperature. Use this to explain why heating can favor melting or vaporization while cooling favors the reverse transition without treating one phase change as universally spontaneous.
