Common-Ion & Coupled Equilibrium

Explain how adding a common ion can shift a dissolution equilibrium.

Weekly EvidenceChemistry CourseScience Hall

Safety boundary

Use solubility curves, supplied datasets, and simulations only. Do not perform unsupervised precipitation chemistry or mix household chemicals.

Explicit chemistry instruction

Adding an ion already present in a dissolution equilibrium changes Qsp immediately.

The system can respond by favoring the solid phase, reducing additional dissolution in the simplified common-ion model.

This is an application of equilibrium response, not a separate law.

Vocabulary: common ion; coupled equilibrium; Qsp; shift; solubility.

Worked example

Adding X⁻ to MX(s)⇌M⁺+X⁻ raises Qsp and can favor formation of more solid MX.

Guided question

Why can adding a soluble salt containing X⁻ reduce the solubility of MX?

Independent work

  1. Predict three common-ion effects.
  2. Connect each to Qsp/Ksp.
  3. Identify one case with no common ion.

Misconception check

The common-ion effect changes equilibrium composition; it does not change Ksp at constant temperature.

Formative check

What stays constant during a common-ion perturbation at fixed temperature?

Reveal response

Ksp.

Week 28 chemistry depth checkpoint

Phenomenon: Adding a common ion can reduce molar solubility because the ion product changes relative to Ksp.

Retrieval: Connect Week 27 disturbance reasoning with Day 2 Ksp.

Quantitative/model transfer: Use Qsp versus Ksp to predict whether dissolution continues, equilibrium remains, or precipitation occurs.

Evidence-quality task: Distinguish common-ion effects from dilution or unrelated ionic-strength effects.

Evidence revision: Correct the claim that adding more solid changes Ksp; at fixed temperature it changes amount of solid present, not Ksp.

Model limit: Coupled equilibria can involve acid-base reactions, complexation, and activity effects beyond a simple common-ion model.

Common-ion transfer extension

Compare the solubility of the same salt in pure water and in a solution already containing one of its ions, then explain the result using Qsp/Ksp.

Common-ion worked extension

For AgCl in a solution already containing 0.10 M Cl⁻, Ksp=[Ag⁺][Cl⁻]. Because [Cl⁻] starts large, the equilibrium [Ag⁺] required to satisfy Ksp is much smaller than in pure water. Thus the molar solubility decreases.

Use Qsp for precipitation: if the current ion product exceeds Ksp, precipitation is favored; if Qsp<Ksp, more solid can dissolve if present.

Coupled-equilibrium extension

A common-ion effect is one example of coupled equilibrium. If one ion also participates in acid-base chemistry or complex formation, its free concentration can change, which in turn changes Qsp and the apparent solubility.

This is why a simple common-ion calculation is a model with boundaries. State when other equilibria are being neglected rather than presenting the result as universally exact.

Final common-ion check

State which ion is shared and how its added concentration changes Qsp relative to Ksp.

Coupled-equilibrium boundary extension

The common-ion model assumes the added ion remains available at the concentration used in Qsp. If that ion also participates in acid-base chemistry, complex formation, or another equilibrium, its free concentration can change substantially. State when those coupled processes are being neglected. This keeps a simple Ksp calculation useful without presenting it as exact for every aqueous system.
