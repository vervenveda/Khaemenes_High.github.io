Network Solids, Molecular Solids, and Ionic Solids

Compare how different extended structures produce different material properties.

Weekly EvidenceChemistry CourseScience HallPreviousNext

Explicit science instruction

Solids can be organized as ionic lattices, metallic structures, network covalent frameworks, or collections of discrete molecules. The relevant structural unit changes the property model.

Network covalent solids contain extensive covalent connectivity, so disrupting the solid can require breaking strong bonds. Molecular solids are held together between molecules by intermolecular forces.

Classifying a material requires converging evidence because real substances may be disordered, mixed, layered, or otherwise more complex than ideal categories.

Vocabulary: network covalent; molecular solid; ionic solid; structure; bonding.

Worked example with reasoning

Diamond and graphite are both carbon-based, but their bonding networks differ. Their different structures help explain why their hardness and electrical behavior differ strongly despite identical elemental composition.

Guided question

Why is elemental composition alone insufficient to predict material properties?

Independent work

  1. Create a comparison table for ionic, metallic, network, and molecular solids.

Same composition does not guarantee same properties; structure and bonding arrangement matter.

Week 9 chemistry depth checkpoint

Phenomenon: Solids with very different microscopic structures can all appear rigid while differing dramatically in melting point, conductivity, and brittleness.

Retrieval: Reuse ionic, covalent, and molecular bonding models from Weeks 6–8 before classifying solids.

Evidence-quality task: Distinguish network-covalent, molecular, ionic, and metallic solids using converging evidence rather than one property.

Quantitative transfer: Rank supplied melting-point and conductivity data and compare which structural model best explains the full pattern.

Evidence revision: Revise classifications when an apparent outlier suggests mixed bonding, defects, or a misleading single-property shortcut.

Model limit: Solid-type categories are useful simplifications; real materials can contain disorder, defects, partial covalency, and multiple phases.

Solid-classification transfer extension

Build an evidence matrix for four anonymous solids using melting behavior, conductivity, hardness, and solubility. Identify the strongest competing classification for the most ambiguous sample.

Solid-structure evidence extension

Compare the kinds of particles and attractions present in each solid type. Molecular solids contain discrete molecules held together by intermolecular forces; network solids connect atoms through extended covalent bonding; ionic solids contain extended arrays of oppositely charged ions; metallic solids combine ion cores with mobile electronic states. These distinctions predict different responses to melting and electrical testing.

Hardness alone is especially weak as a classifier. Diamond is hard because of its extended covalent network, while many ionic solids are hard yet brittle for a different reason. Use multiple properties and a particle-level mechanism before assigning a structural category.
