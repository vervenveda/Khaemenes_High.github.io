Concentration Stress

Predict how equilibrium composition responds to changing reactant or product concentration.

Weekly EvidenceChemistry CourseScience Hall

Safety boundary

Use virtual equilibrium perturbations and supplied datasets only; no unsupervised color-change chemistry.

Explicit chemistry instruction

A concentration disturbance changes Q immediately while K remains fixed at constant temperature.

The system then evolves in the direction that brings Q back toward K.

Le Châtelier language is a shortcut; Q/K reasoning gives the stronger mechanistic basis.

Vocabulary: stress; concentration; shift; Q; equilibrium position.

Worked example

Adding reactant changes Q immediately and can drive a compensating composition change while K stays fixed.

Guided question

Why does K stay unchanged after a concentration perturbation at constant temperature?

Independent work

  1. Predict four concentration shifts.
  2. Explain one with Q/K.
  3. Separate immediate change from subsequent response.

Misconception check

The system does not 'know' how to oppose stress; rates respond to changed concentrations.

Formative check

What changes immediately after adding reactant: K or Q?

Reveal response

Q changes; K does not at fixed temperature.

Mentor evidence conference

Ask Archaemenes to audit whether Q, K, and rate effects are being kept distinct.

Week 27 chemistry depth checkpoint

Phenomenon: Adding or removing a species from an equilibrium mixture changes the reaction quotient immediately, which can drive net reaction until Q returns to K.

Retrieval: Connect Week 26 Q-versus-K reasoning to concentration disturbances.

Quantitative/model transfer: Predict how changing one concentration alters the numerator or denominator of Q before using any verbal shift rule.

Evidence-quality task: Distinguish the instantaneous concentration change caused by the intervention from the slower composition change caused by reaction response.

Evidence revision: Replace vague “system opposes stress” explanations with explicit Q-versus-K logic whenever possible.

Model limit: Concentration shifts do not change K at fixed temperature.

Concentration-disturbance transfer extension

For a reversible reaction, predict the effect of adding a reactant and removing a product by identifying how Q changes relative to K in each case.

Worked concentration-disturbance extension

For A⇌B with K=[B]/[A], suppose an equilibrium mixture has equal concentrations and K=1. If A is suddenly doubled while B is unchanged, Q becomes 0.5. Because Q<K, net forward reaction is predicted until the ratio returns to 1. The first change is caused by the intervention; the later change is caused by chemistry.

This two-stage description prevents the common mistake of treating the final “shift” as instantaneous.

Concentration-ratio extension

For more complex reactions, inspect every exponent in Q before predicting direction. If 2A⇌B, then Q=[B]/[A]^2, so doubling A changes the denominator by a factor of four rather than two. This makes stoichiometric exponents central to disturbance reasoning.

After the system re-equilibrates, both A and B may differ from their original concentrations even though K remains unchanged at the same temperature.

Final Q/K check

State explicitly whether the intervention changes Q, K, or both before predicting the net reaction direction.

Concentration-perturbation audit

When a species is added or removed, identify exactly which term in Q changes first. Then determine whether Q rises or falls relative to K before predicting net reaction direction. The immediate concentration jump is not itself the equilibrium shift; the shift is the later composition change caused by unequal forward and reverse rates. Keeping those stages separate makes the reasoning stronger than the phrase “the system opposes the stress.”
