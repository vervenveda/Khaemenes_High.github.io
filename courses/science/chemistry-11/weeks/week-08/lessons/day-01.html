Electron Domains and VSEPR Geometry

Use electron-domain repulsion to predict basic molecular geometry.

Weekly EvidenceChemistry CourseScience HallArchaemenesNext

Explicit science instruction

VSEPR is a geometric model: electron domains around a central atom arrange to reduce repulsion. A single, double, or triple bond counts as one bonding domain; each lone pair counts as one lone-pair domain.

Two domains give a linear electron-domain arrangement, three give trigonal planar, and four give tetrahedral. Molecular geometry names the positions of atoms, so lone pairs can change the molecular shape while retaining the same electron-domain geometry.

The model predicts shape, not exact bond angles for every molecule. Lone pairs and multiple bonds can shift measured angles.

Vocabulary: electron domain; VSEPR; molecular geometry; lone pair; bond angle.

Worked example with reasoning

CO₂ has two bonding domains and no lone pairs on carbon, so the central atom is linear. H₂O has four electron domains around oxygen—two bonds and two lone pairs—so its molecular geometry is bent.

Guided question

Compare CH₄, NH₃, and H₂O: all have four electron domains around the central atom. Why do their molecular geometries differ?

Independent work

  1. Determine electron-domain count and molecular geometry for CO₂, BF₃, CH₄, NH₃, and H₂O.
  2. Draw one three-dimensional wedge/dash sketch.
  3. For one molecule, state where the VSEPR model may not give an exact measured angle.

Misconception check

A double bond counts as one electron domain in basic VSEPR; it does not create two separate directions around the central atom.

Formative understanding check

Why is NH₃ trigonal pyramidal rather than tetrahedral as a molecular shape?

Reveal response

Nitrogen has four electron domains but one is a lone pair, so only three atomic positions define the molecular geometry.
