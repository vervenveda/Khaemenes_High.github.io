pH as a Logarithmic Scale

Interpret pH as a logarithmic measure of hydrogen-ion concentration.

Weekly EvidenceChemistry CourseScience Hall

Safety boundary

Use virtual titration or teacher-supervised neutralization in a properly equipped lab only. Never titrate household cleaners or unknown solutions.

Explicit chemistry instruction

pH=-log10[H3O+] in the simplified aqueous model.

A one-unit pH change corresponds to a tenfold change in hydrogen-ion concentration.

pH is not a linear percentage scale.

Vocabulary: pH; hydronium; logarithm; concentration; decade.

Worked example

If [H3O+]=1×10^-3 M, pH=3. A solution at pH 4 has ten times lower [H3O+] than pH 3.

Guided question

How many times does [H3O+] change between pH 2 and pH 5?

Independent work

  1. Convert four powers-of-ten concentrations to pH.
  2. Compare relative acidity from pH.
  3. Explain the logarithmic step.

Misconception check

A pH of 2 is not merely twice as acidic as pH 4.

Formative check

What concentration factor corresponds to one pH unit?

Reveal response

A factor of 10.

Mentor evidence conference

Ask Archaemenes to audit logarithmic reasoning, stoichiometry, units, and curve interpretation.

Teacher guidance

Foundation: Use powers-of-ten examples.

Core: Calculate simple pH values.

Extended: Interpret non-integer pH with calculator support.

Week 30 chemistry depth checkpoint

Phenomenon: pH compresses a wide range of hydronium concentrations onto a logarithmic scale.

Retrieval: Connect Week 29 aqueous acid–base chemistry to hydronium concentration.

Quantitative reasoning: Use pH=−log[H₃O⁺] and [H₃O⁺]=10^(−pH), keeping concentration units in mol/L.

Evidence-quality task: Distinguish a one-unit pH change from a one-unit concentration change; each pH unit corresponds to a tenfold change in [H₃O⁺].

Evidence revision: Correct the misconception that pH 2 is only “twice as acidic” as pH 4.

Model limit: Simple concentration formulas are approximations when activities differ significantly from concentrations.

pH transfer extension

Compare solutions at pH 3 and pH 5 and quantify the hydronium concentration ratio before interpreting the chemical difference.

Worked pH extension

If [H3O+]=1.0×10⁻³ M, then pH=3.00. If pH=5.00, then [H3O+]=1.0×10⁻⁵ M. The first solution has 100 times the hydronium concentration of the second because the pH difference is two logarithmic units.

Use significant figures appropriately: decimal places in pH correspond to significant figures in the concentration used to calculate it.

Logarithmic-scale extension

Use ratios to interpret pH differences. A pH change from 6 to 4 represents a 100-fold increase in hydronium concentration; from 8 to 5 represents a 1000-fold increase. The scale is logarithmic because [H3O+] spans many orders of magnitude in aqueous systems.

Reverse calculations should be treated as exponent work, not linear subtraction. A pH of 2.30 corresponds to [H3O+]≈5.0×10⁻3 M, not 2.30 M or 10⁻2.30 without evaluating the exponent.

pH interpretation extension

Compare differences by powers of ten, not by subtraction alone. Two solutions separated by 3.0 pH units differ by a factor of 1000 in hydronium concentration. When pH values are non-integers, use the inverse logarithm to determine the actual ratio rather than rounding prematurely.

Logarithm reasonableness extension

After calculating pH, perform an inverse check using [H₃O⁺]=10^(−pH). A one-unit pH change should correspond to a tenfold concentration change, so order-of-magnitude reasoning can catch sign and calculator errors quickly. Keep logarithm inputs dimensionless in the mathematical model while reporting the underlying concentration in mol/L. A pH answer that contradicts the estimated hydronium scale should be rechecked before interpretation.
