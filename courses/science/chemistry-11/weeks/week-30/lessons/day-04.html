Virtual Titration Investigation

Use a titration curve to locate equivalence and evaluate indicator choice.

Weekly EvidenceChemistry CourseScience Hall

Safety boundary

Use virtual titration or teacher-supervised neutralization in a properly equipped lab only. Never titrate household cleaners or unknown solutions.

Explicit chemistry instruction

A titration curve plots pH against titrant volume.

The steep region helps locate equivalence for many strong acid/base cases.

Indicator selection should match its transition range to the steep pH-change region.

Vocabulary: titration curve; equivalence; indicator range; endpoint; buret.

Worked example

If the steep rise centers near 25.0 mL, an indicator changing within that pH jump can provide a useful endpoint.

Week 30 chemistry depth checkpoint

Phenomenon: A titration curve records how pH changes as titrant is added and reveals different chemical regions.

Retrieval: Use pH/pOH and titration stoichiometry from Days 1–3.

Quantitative transfer: Record titrant volume and pH across the curve, identify the steepest region, and estimate equivalence volume from the inflection region rather than one arbitrary point.

Evidence-quality task: Distinguish instrument readings from the inferred equivalence point and indicator endpoint.

Evidence revision: If replicate curves disagree, inspect buret reading, probe calibration, mixing, and overshoot.

Uncertainty: Large pH changes near equivalence make small volume-reading errors especially important.

Virtual-titration extension

Compare two titration runs and identify which one gives the more defensible equivalence volume and why.

Titration-curve region extension

Interpret the curve by regions: initial pH reflects the analyte, the pre-equivalence region changes as titrant neutralizes it, the steep region contains equivalence for a strong acid/strong base pair, and the post-equivalence region is controlled by excess titrant.

For weak-acid titrations, the curve also includes a buffer region and the equivalence pH need not be 7.00. This prevents overgeneralizing one titration shape.

Curve-resolution extension

Near equivalence, smaller titrant-volume increments give a more defensible estimate of the inflection region. Large increments can skip over the steepest section and make the apparent equivalence volume depend on sampling resolution rather than chemistry.

For weak-acid/strong-base titration, recognize the half-equivalence region as chemically informative because the acid and conjugate-base amounts are comparable; this connects the curve to buffer behavior that follows in the next unit.

Endpoint-versus-equivalence extension

Equivalence is the stoichiometric condition where reacting amounts satisfy the balanced equation. Endpoint is the experimental signal used to estimate that condition, such as an indicator color change or instrument threshold. The two need not coincide exactly. Preserve the observed endpoint volume separately from the calculated equivalence condition and discuss how indicator range, reading resolution, or overshoot can create systematic bias.
