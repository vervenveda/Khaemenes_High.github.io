Temperature & Rate

Use particle-energy distributions to explain why temperature often changes reaction rate.

Weekly EvidenceChemistry CourseScience Hall

Safety boundary

Use simulations and supplied datasets only unless a teacher provides an explicitly approved classroom-safe demonstration. Do not mix household chemicals or improvise reactive systems.

Explicit chemistry instruction

Higher temperature increases average kinetic energy and changes the fraction of particles above an activation-energy threshold.

Rate can increase strongly even when average kinetic energy rises modestly because the high-energy tail changes.

Temperature affects rate; it does not change stoichiometric coefficients.

Vocabulary: temperature; kinetic energy; activation threshold; energy distribution; rate ratio.

Worked example

A simulated reaction rate rises from 0.020 to 0.050 M/s when temperature increases under otherwise identical conditions; the rate ratio is 2.5.

Guided question

Calculate the rate ratio when rate changes from 0.012 to 0.036 M/s.

Independent work

  1. Compare three temperature-rate datasets.
  2. Calculate rate ratios.
  3. Explain the trend using the energy-distribution model.

Misconception check

Temperature does not simply 'make particles bigger' or change the balanced reaction.

Formative check

What key distribution change helps explain faster reaction at higher temperature?

Reveal response

A greater fraction of particles have energy at or above the activation threshold.

Mentor evidence conference

Ask Archaemenes to audit controlled variables, particle-level reasoning, rate ratios, and model scope.

Teacher guidance

Foundation: Use energy-distribution sketches.

Core: Compare rate ratios and explanations.

Extended: Evaluate where the simple model needs Arrhenius-style refinement.

Week 20 chemistry depth checkpoint

Phenomenon: Increasing temperature often increases reaction rate more strongly than a simple increase in collision frequency would predict.

Retrieval: Use Day 1 energy-threshold reasoning and Week 11 kinetic-temperature concepts.

Quantitative/model transfer: Compare rate values at two temperatures and interpret the ratio without assuming every 10-degree change has a universal effect.

Evidence-quality task: Distinguish increased average kinetic energy from the increased fraction of particles exceeding an activation-energy threshold.

Evidence revision: Correct any explanation that temperature changes the activation energy of an uncatalyzed reaction.

Model limit: Temperature-rate relationships are reaction-specific and may require an Arrhenius model for quantitative prediction.

Temperature-rate transfer extension

Use a simple energy-distribution sketch to explain why a modest temperature increase can create a larger relative increase in successful collisions.

Activation-energy distribution extension

A temperature increase shifts and broadens the kinetic-energy distribution so a larger fraction of particles can exceed the activation-energy threshold. The threshold itself is associated with the reaction pathway and is not raised by heating.

Compare rate ratios rather than memorized “doubling” rules. Different reactions have different activation energies, so the same temperature change can produce very different rate responses.

Temperature-model note

Heating changes the energy distribution, increasing the fraction above the activation threshold; it does not lower the uncatalyzed activation energy.

Temperature comparison check

Compare rates under otherwise identical conditions so the temperature effect is not confounded by concentration, catalyst, mixing, or surface-area changes.

Final evidence note

State the controlled variables before attributing a rate difference to temperature.
