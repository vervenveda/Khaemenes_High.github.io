Week 1 · Five Reading Lessons

Day 1 · Original short-short story

The List on the Door

Mara found a sign at the library entrance: 'Some rooms are closed for repairs.' She assumed the whole library was unavailable and turned away. A younger visitor read the sign more slowly, noticed the word 'some,' and stepped inside to ask which rooms remained open. By afternoon, Mara had returned with a notebook. She wrote her first inference, crossed it out, and recorded what the sign actually said.

Close-reading question: Explain why the word 'some' changes Mara's initial conclusion.

Day 2 · Original short-short story

The Empty Seat

A new student left an empty seat between himself and the others at lunch. Lena thought he wanted to be alone. Later she saw him move his backpack so another student could sit beside him. The empty seat, she realized, had carried a meaning she invented. She wrote both observations in her journal, marking the first judgment with a question mark.

Close-reading question: Distinguish direct observations from unsupported assumptions.

Day 3 · Original short-short story

The Library Key

When the school library closed early, a brass key remained hanging behind the desk. Theo imagined it had been forgotten. At the next meeting, the librarian explained that the key was an old display, not the one used for the door. Theo kept his earlier guess in his notes because it showed how a familiar object could produce a convincing but mistaken story.

Close-reading question: Identify the detail that overturns Theo's inference.

Day 4 · Original short-short story

The Notice Revised

A student bulletin declared that everyone wanted a longer recess. Its author had spoken to four classmates, all in the same group. During revision, she replaced 'everyone' with 'four students interviewed on Tuesday.' Her headline became less impressive but her report became more trustworthy. The teacher asked whether another group might answer differently, and the author added that question to her next survey.

Close-reading question: Revise an overgeneralization into a supported claim.

Day 5 · Original short-short story

The Final Margin

At week's end, Amir compared his first reading notes with his newest ones. He still liked quick answers, but now he circled the phrases that made a conclusion uncertain. 'I learned to be less sure,' he told his teacher. She answered that careful evidence need not destroy confidence: it gives confidence something to stand on.

Close-reading question: Explain how Amir's habits changed, citing a specific action.

The Locked Cabinet

Nora saw an empty cabinet and told her classmates that the lost trophies had been stolen. The custodian showed her a work order: the shelves were being repaired and the trophies had been moved. Day 1 · Original Academy literature

The Fire Keeper

When the valley first grew cold, villagers told of a child who carried a coal across the mountain. Every retelling named a different child, but all agreed that the flame was shared rather than hoarded. The story gave the village a way to praise generosity whenever winter returned.

Literary evidence question: Explain what cultural value the myth communicates.

The River's Question

In a village tale, the river asked travelers to surrender one precious thing before crossing. A merchant offered a coin, while a healer offered the hour she needed to help a stranger. The river opened for the healer. No one knew whether the river could speak; the tale made the value of time visible.

Literary evidence question: Explain how an impossible event conveys a human concern.

The Star Path

An elder told children that the stars were footprints left by people who traveled before them. Each generation added a new constellation to the story. When a young navigator challenged the map, the elder invited her to name what the old story still taught and what actual navigation required.

The Borrowed Crown

A hero borrowed a crown to end a dispute, then refused to return it after the danger passed. The people remembered his bravery but also the moment power changed him. The tale ended with the crown placed on an empty chair, waiting for someone willing to serve without owning it.

Day 5 · Original Academy literature

The Story at Dawn

Two villages claimed the same mountain story, each naming its own ancestor as the first climber. At a gathering, they agreed to preserve both tellings with their speakers credited. The mountain belonged to neither tale alone, and the stories became a reason to ask questions rather than settle ownership.

Literary evidence question: Explain why differing versions should be documented rather than erased.
