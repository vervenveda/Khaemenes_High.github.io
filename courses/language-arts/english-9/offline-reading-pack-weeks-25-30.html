WEEK 25 · UNIT 09

Launch a Sustained Reading Inquiry

Model passage · The Clockmaker’s Window

On the first morning in the new town, Mara noticed the clockmaker’s window before she noticed the clock. Twelve watches hung in a neat circle, all stopped at different minutes. The shopkeeper told her they were “waiting for their owners to remember them.” Mara laughed, but three chapters later she returned after receiving a letter she did not want to answer. This time she noticed one watch stopped at 4:17—the same minute written at the top of the letter.

Sustained-reading lesson

A long work creates meaning through recurrence and change. The first appearance of an object may establish curiosity; later appearances can revise its significance. Readers should record chapter/page locator, literal event, recurring image or idea, character response, and a tentative interpretation rather than waiting until the end to remember patterns.

Worked model

A defensible early claim is not “the watch symbolizes regret” as a permanent fact. A better note is: “The repeated stopped-watch image begins as comic mystery but becomes connected to Mara’s avoidance after the letter; later chapters may confirm, complicate, or overturn that interpretation.”

Independent task

Create a reading plan for your approved novel. Record one character question, one emerging motif, one conflict and one contextual question. Preserve exact page/chapter locators and mark interpretations as provisional.

WEEK 26 · UNIT 09

Track Character, Motif & Competing Interpretations

Model passage · Return to the Window

Mara returns to the shop after arguing with her brother. The 4:17 watch is gone. She asks whether its owner remembered it. The clockmaker replies, “No. Someone else decided it was time to move.” Mara says nothing, but later tears the unopened letter into four pieces and keeps one.

Interpretive problem

One reader may argue that the missing watch represents release; another may argue that Mara is trying to control time rather than accept it. Both interpretations require textual evidence and must account for details that do not fit easily.

Worked comparison

The line “someone else decided it was time to move” complicates a simple recovery reading because agency belongs to an unnamed person, not necessarily the owner. Mara’s decision to keep one piece of the letter also resists a total-break interpretation.

Independent task

For your novel, build a two-column interpretation record: strongest evidence for your current reading and strongest evidence that complicates it. Add one character-change claim that uses evidence from at least two separated points in the book.

WEEK 27 · UNIT 09

From Reading Record to Literary Analysis

Model evidence chain

Observation: the stopped-watch motif recurs near moments of avoidance. Pattern: its meaning shifts from curiosity to deliberate control. Counterevidence: the clockmaker’s explanation suggests other people can alter the object’s meaning. Interpretive claim: the novel uses stopped time to explore how memory is managed rather than simply escaped.

Analysis architecture

A developed literary analysis should make a claim about how a pattern, character choice, structure, image or conflict develops across the work. Organize by ideas and evidence, not by retelling chapters. Each paragraph should connect exact textual details to the larger interpretation.

Revision check

Test whether every paragraph answers “So what does this evidence reveal?” Remove plot summary that does not serve the claim. Add a fair counterreading and explain why your interpretation remains more persuasive or how it must be qualified.

Independent task

Draft a thesis and evidence map for the 1,200–1,500-word literary analysis. Use at least three points from different parts of the novel, one complication/counterreading, and a short reflection on how your interpretation changed during sustained reading.

WEEK 28 · UNIT 10

Frame the Issue Before Taking a Side

Case file · Library access proposal

A fictional school board is considering whether to extend library hours from 4:30 p.m. to 7:30 p.m. three evenings per week. A student survey reports 68 of 104 respondents would use later hours. The library director estimates two additional staff-hours per evening. The last public bus currently leaves at 6:50 p.m.

Reasoning problem

“Students want later hours” is not yet a complete policy argument. A fair issue frame must name the decision, affected groups, constraints, evidence needed, and criteria for judging options.

Worked model

A stronger question is: “Should the school test later library hours on selected evenings if staffing, transportation and safety conditions can be met?” This frame narrows the action and makes room for evidence that could support, qualify or reject the proposal.

Independent task

Create a two-sided argument map with claim, evidence, warrant, assumptions, three stakeholders, two fair criteria, one uncertainty, and one piece of evidence that could change your position.

WEEK 29 · UNIT 10

Evidence, Rebuttal & Ethical Persuasion

Source A

A follow-up survey finds 41 of 68 interested students would need transportation after 7:00 p.m.

Source B

A staffing memo says coverage is possible on Tuesdays and Thursdays but uncertain on Wednesdays.

Source C

An editorial argues that “any delay proves the school does not care about students.”

Worked evaluation

Source A is relevant to access, Source B narrows feasibility, and Source C uses emotionally loaded language that does not itself establish policy facts. A fair rebuttal answers the strongest opposing reason rather than the weakest wording.

Independent task

Write one concession, one rebuttal, one qualified recommendation and one sentence that replaces loaded language with a claim that can be tested.

WEEK 30 · UNIT 10

Debate, Listening & Position-Paper Revision

Debate record

Team A argues for a six-week pilot. Team B argues that transportation must be resolved before any pilot begins. During questioning, Team A concedes that access would be unequal without a late ride option; Team B concedes that a limited trial could produce better evidence than continued speculation.

Revision lesson

Debate is evidence for revision, not merely performance. The strongest post-debate paper should preserve the best opposing point, revise overstatements, and explain why the final position changed—or why it did not.

Worked model

Before debate: “The school should immediately extend hours.” After debate: “The school should run a six-week two-evening pilot only if a late transportation option and minimum staffing level are confirmed.” The second thesis is narrower because the debate exposed real conditions.

Independent task

Revise a 1,200–1,600-word position-paper plan using at least one concession, one rebuttal, one condition, and one paragraph explaining how debate evidence changed your reasoning.
