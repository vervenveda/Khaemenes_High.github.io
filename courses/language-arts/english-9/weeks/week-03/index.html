Unit 01 · Scholarly Foundations · Week 03

Inquiry, Dialogue & Synthesis

How do careful readers test, refine, and defend interpretations with other minds and multiple texts?

Weeks 1 and 2 built close reading, claims, evidence, and analysis. This week asks students to do something more scholarly: ask better questions, compare defensible interpretations, listen closely, revise thinking through dialogue, synthesize ideas across texts, and demonstrate the full Unit 1 cycle independently.

Weekly Targets

By the end of this week, I can…

  • Ask interpretive questions that require evidence, reasoning, and more than one possible answer.
  • Compare two defensible interpretations by evaluating their claims, evidence, assumptions, and reasoning.
  • Participate in academic dialogue by listening accurately, responding to ideas, and using evidence rather than volume.
  • Synthesize ideas from more than one text into a new conclusion instead of merely listing similarities.
  • Paraphrase, summarize, and attribute ideas responsibly without disguising someone else’s thinking as my own.
  • Control fragments, run-ons, comma splices, and coordination in analytical writing.
  • Independently complete the Unit 1 Scholarly Demonstration at 80% mastery or revise the weakest area and resubmit.

Day 11InquiryRL.9-10.1SL.9-10.1

Ask Better Questions

Today’s Question: What kind of question creates thinking instead of merely checking memory?

Learning Target

I can ask interpretive questions that require evidence, allow more than one defensible answer, and lead to deeper reading.

Launch · Three Kinds of Questions

Classify each question:

  1. Where does Jonah sit?
  2. Why does Jonah cross out his first reply?
  3. When should a reader allow another person’s interpretation to change their own?

The first checks recall. The second asks for interpretation. The third transfers an idea beyond the literal events of the story.

I Do · The Question Ladder
  1. Literal: What happened?
  2. Interpretive: Why or how might this detail matter?
  3. Analytical: What pattern, contrast, shift, or choice creates meaning?
  4. Transfer: What larger idea does the text help us examine?

A strong scholarly question is open, evidence-demanding, text-connected, and worth answering.

Model

Weak: Did Jonah change his mind?

Stronger: How does Jonah’s decision to ask for page numbers change the nature of his disagreement?

The stronger question cannot be answered with yes/no and requires the reader to connect an action to a larger idea.

We Do

Read Text A. Generate one literal, one interpretive, and one analytical question. Test the analytical question: Can two careful readers answer it differently and still use evidence?

You Do

Write three possible interpretive questions about Text A. Choose the strongest and explain in 3–4 sentences why it is worth investigating.

Language Lab · Fragments

A question note such as “Because Jonah disagrees with the ending” is a fragment, not a complete analytical sentence. Repair three fragmentary notes by adding the missing independent clause. Then explain how the repair changes clarity.

Original Course Text · Used Days 11–14

Text A · The Note in the Window Seat

Jonah chose the same library seat every lunch period because the window beside it made the fluorescent lights easier to ignore. On Monday, a paperback novel was already there. A square of notebook paper stuck out from the final chapter. In careful blue ink someone had written, “I think the ending is wrong.”

Jonah had read the book twice. He did not think the ending was wrong. He turned the note over and wrote, “It makes perfect sense if you paid attention to the brother.” Then he stared at the sentence until the words felt louder than he intended. He crossed them out.

Under the black line, he wrote a different question: “Which part makes you think that?” He left the note in the book and returned it to the same place on the shelf.

On Tuesday the book was back on the window seat. Three page numbers appeared beneath his question. Jonah opened to the first. Then the second. At the third, he paused over a sentence he remembered but had never connected to the ending. He still disagreed with the note’s first claim, but now his disagreement had become harder to explain in one sentence.

Before lunch ended, Jonah added two page numbers of his own. Beneath them he wrote, “I still read it differently. I see why you don’t.” He closed the book, but instead of shelving it, he left it on the window seat.

Day 12Competing interpretationsRL.9-10.1RL.9-10.3

Compare Interpretations

Today’s Question: How can two readers disagree without one of them reading carelessly?

Learning Target

I can compare two defensible interpretations by examining the claim, evidence, reasoning, and assumptions behind each.

Launch

Consider two interpretations of Jonah:

Interpretation A: Jonah’s crossed-out response shows intellectual humility.

Interpretation B: Jonah’s crossed-out response shows uncertainty and fear of direct disagreement.

Neither is automatically correct. Both must earn credibility through evidence and reasoning.

I Do · Compare the Structure, Not Just the Conclusion
TestInterpretation AInterpretation B
ClaimHe becomes more intellectually humble.He avoids direct confrontation.
Best evidenceHe replaces an accusation with a question.He crosses out what he first wanted to say.
ReasoningQuestions make room for evidence that may challenge him.Softening the response may show discomfort with conflict.
What would strengthen it?Evidence that he genuinely considers the other view.Evidence that he consistently avoids disagreement.
We Do

Find one piece of evidence that strengthens Interpretation A and one that strengthens Interpretation B. Then identify one detail that complicates each interpretation.

You Do

Write a 180–250 word comparison explaining which interpretation currently has the stronger case. You must acknowledge what the weaker interpretation notices correctly before explaining why your chosen interpretation is better supported.

Language Lab · Comparison & Coordination

Practice joining related ideas with but, yet, while, although, however, in contrast, similarly. Repair one comma splice and one run-on from the comparison below, then use at least two comparison transitions in your own response.

Optional Video · Compare & Contrast

Comparison Rule

Do not ask only, “Which one is right?” Ask: What does each interpretation explain well, what does it overlook, and which has the stronger evidence-reasoning chain?

Day 13Academic discussionSL.9-10.1SL.9-10.3

Academic Discussion & Listening

Today’s Question: What does good listening contribute to strong thinking?

Learning Target

I can listen accurately, paraphrase another person’s reasoning fairly, respond with evidence, and revise my own interpretation when the discussion reveals a stronger explanation.

Launch · Listening Test

Before responding to a classmate, complete this sentence accurately: “Your interpretation is that ___, and your strongest reason is ___.” If the speaker says you misrepresented the idea, your next job is to listen again—not defend your paraphrase.

I Do · Four Moves of Scholarly Dialogue
  1. Represent: State the other idea fairly.
  2. Probe: Ask for evidence or clarification.
  3. Respond: Add, challenge, or complicate using the text.
  4. Revise: State what changed—or why your view remains stronger.

Academic dialogue is not a contest for speaking time. Its product is better reasoning.

We Do · Evidence Round

Use one question from Day 11. Each participant must make one claim, cite one detail, and ask one follow-up question before speaking a second time.

You Do · Dialogue Record

After the discussion, write:

  1. One idea you represented accurately.
  2. One piece of evidence another reader noticed that you had missed.
  3. One claim you strengthened, weakened, or revised.
  4. One question that remains genuinely unresolved.
Language Lab · Sentence Boundaries in Discussion Notes

Discussion notes are often fragments and run-ons because thinking happens quickly. Convert four shorthand notes into complete analytical sentences. Use a period, semicolon, or conjunction intentionally rather than adding commas between independent clauses.

Optional Video · Academic Dialogue

Asset: ELA-ACADEMIC-DIALOGUE-001. Optional reinforcement for collaborative reasoning and active listening.

Discussion Protocol

1 · Enter with evidence.
Bring a marked text and one tentative claim.
2 · Paraphrase before challenging.
Show that you heard the actual idea.
3 · Challenge reasons, not people.
Use the text to test the claim.
4 · Record revision.
Growth is visible when thinking changes for a reason.

PROSE Move

No PROSE during the dialogue. Afterward, preserve your own discussion record as W3D13 Dialogue Reflection.

Original Informational Text · Used Day 14

Text B · What Disagreement Can Do

Disagreement is often treated as a sign that thinking has failed. In serious inquiry, disagreement can do the opposite. A competing explanation forces a claim to reveal what supports it, what it assumes, and what evidence could change it.

This works only when the purpose of disagreement is examination rather than victory. If a reader listens only long enough to prepare a reply, the other interpretation becomes a target instead of information. Productive disagreement requires a person to represent another view accurately before criticizing it.

A strong thinker is therefore not someone who changes position every time another person objects. Nor is a strong thinker someone who refuses to change. The important question is whether the change—or the refusal to change—can be explained by evidence and reasoning.

In this sense, intellectual independence does not mean thinking alone. It means remaining responsible for your own judgment even while allowing other people’s questions, evidence, and interpretations to test it.

Day 14SynthesisRI.9-10.1W.9-10.9

Synthesis: Build a New Understanding

Today’s Question: How is synthesis different from simply putting two summaries next to each other?

Learning Target

I can combine ideas from a literary text and an informational text to form a new conclusion that is supported by both.

Launch · Summary or Synthesis?

Summary: Text A shows Jonah exchanging notes with another reader. Text B explains that disagreement can improve thinking.

Synthesis: Together, the texts suggest that disagreement becomes intellectually useful when a person replaces the urge to win with a willingness to ask for evidence while still taking responsibility for a final judgment.

The synthesis says something new about the relationship between the sources.

I Do · The Synthesis Bridge
  1. What does Source A contribute?
  2. What does Source B contribute?
  3. Where do they reinforce, complicate, or qualify one another?
  4. What conclusion can I draw because I considered them together?
We Do

Complete a two-source note: one idea from Text A, one idea from Text B, and one relationship word: reinforces, complicates, qualifies, extends, contrasts. Turn that relationship into one synthesis sentence.

You Do

Write a 250–350 word synthesis response to this question: What do Text A and Text B together suggest about the relationship between disagreement and intellectual responsibility? Use evidence from both texts and explain the relationship between them.

Academic Integrity Lab · Paraphrase, Summary & Attribution

Paraphrase restates a specific idea in genuinely new wording and structure. Summary compresses a larger section to its central idea. Attribution makes the source of an idea visible. Copying distinctive wording while changing only a few words is patchwriting, not responsible paraphrase.

Practice: paraphrase one sentence from Text B without looking at it while drafting. Then compare your version to the original and revise any borrowed structure or distinctive wording. Attribute the idea: Text B argues that…

Optional Video · Synthesis

Asset: ELA-SYNTHESIS-001. Optional reinforcement for combining ideas and forming original conclusions.

Integrity Rule
Borrowed ideas still need attribution even when the wording is yours. Your synthesis must make clear which ideas come from the texts and which conclusion is your own.

Unit 01 Scholarly Demonstration

Today’s Question: Can I independently turn unfamiliar evidence into a defensible, synthesized interpretation?

Learning Target

I can independently read unfamiliar paired texts, ask a meaningful question, develop a defensible claim, use precise evidence from both texts, synthesize their ideas, and communicate my reasoning clearly.

Conditions

Complete the first response without PROSE, sentence-level assistance, or a generated outline. You may use your Unit 1 strategy notes, but the reading, question, claim, evidence choices, and reasoning must be yours.

Independent Process
  1. Read Text C for gist.
  2. Read Text D for gist.
  3. Reread and annotate both.
  4. Write one interpretive or synthesis question.
  5. Develop one defensible claim.
  6. Select at least two precise pieces of evidence from Text C and at least one from Text D.
  7. Write 450–650 words.
  8. Complete the conventions check.
  9. Use the rubric before submitting.
Prompt

Develop an interpretation of what the paired texts suggest about perspective and responsible judgment. Your response should make a defensible claim, use evidence from both texts, explain relationships among the evidence, and show synthesis rather than two separate summaries.

Corrective Learning

8/10 or higher = mastery. If the response is below 8/10, identify the lowest rubric row, revise that exact weakness, explain what changed and why, then resubmit. Corrective learning improves the same intellectual work; it does not lower the standard.

Unfamiliar Literary Text · Day 15

Text C · Two Maps

Sera found both maps in the top drawer of the cabin desk. One was printed, folded into exact rectangles, and marked with road numbers. The other was drawn in pencil on the back of an old grocery list. It showed a creek, a split oak, a hill shaped “like a sleeping dog,” and a line that said, “If the road floods, listen for water before you look for it.”

Her brother Owen held up the printed map. “This one is real.”

“They’re both real,” Sera said. “They’re just answering different questions.”

By late afternoon rain had covered the lower road. The printed map still showed a clean blue line where the pavement disappeared beneath brown water. Owen traced a detour with his finger, but the turn he wanted was blocked by a fallen tree.

Sera unfolded the grocery-list map. The split oak stood uphill from them, half hidden by vines. Beyond it, she could hear the creek before she could see it. The hand-drawn map did not show every road, and the printed map did not show the narrow footpath beside the water. Together, the two maps gave them enough information to find the ridge trail back to the cabin.

That night Owen flattened both maps on the table. “I still like this one better,” he said, tapping the printed sheet.

Sera smiled. “Better for what?”

Unfamiliar Informational Text · Day 15

Text D · Every Map Selects

A map may appear to be a neutral picture of a place, but every map is built through selection. A road map emphasizes routes and labels. A weather map emphasizes atmospheric conditions. A transit map may distort physical distance so that connections are easier to understand.

Selection does not automatically make a map dishonest. A useful map cannot include every fact about a place. Its value depends partly on whether the information it emphasizes fits the question a person is trying to answer.

This is also why responsible judgment often requires more than one representation. A source can be accurate and still be incomplete for a particular purpose. Comparing perspectives allows a person to notice what one source makes visible and what another leaves out.

The goal is not to collect as many perspectives as possible without judging them. The goal is to understand what each perspective contributes, test it against evidence and purpose, and then make a decision that remains your own responsibility.
