Unit 07 · Week 19 · Reading Hall Week A

Research Questions, Observation & Search Strategy

What do I actually know—and what am I already assuming?

Anchor reading: Guy de Maupassant, The Necklace.
Research: Move from curiosity to an answerable investigation while learning to keep textual observation, inference, contextual question, and externally verified fact distinct.

PreviousDaily LessonsNext

Weekly Targets

Observe before researching; question before concluding.

  • Distinguish textual observation, inference, personal reaction, contextual question, and verified contextual fact.
  • Refine a researchable question without embedding the desired conclusion in its wording.
  • Build keyword families and mutate searches as new vocabulary appears.
  • Select source types according to the claim being investigated.
  • Preserve a transparent search log including dead ends and question changes.
  • Identify evidence that would support, weaken, or force revision of an initial interpretation.
  • Use accurately: inquiry, scope, keyword, primary source, secondary source, database.

Five-Day Inquiry Laboratory

Read → observe → question → search → defend the inquiry.

Day 1 · First Reading

The Observation / Inference Ledger

Begin The Necklace. Record consequential details without immediately turning them into judgments about character or morality.

  • Create two columns: “The text establishes…” and “I infer…”
  • For each inference, identify the exact detail that triggered it.
  • Mark emotional reactions separately; a reaction is real evidence about the reader, not automatically evidence about the character.
  • Write three questions the text cannot answer by itself.
Day 2 · Question Ladder

A Judgment Is Not Yet a Research Question

Transform curiosity raised by the story into contextual questions without assuming Mathilde, her husband, society, wealth, gender expectations, or another factor is already the cause.

  • Generate questions about money, work, social status, material culture, education, marriage, class mobility, or another relevant context.
  • Reject loaded formulations such as questions that already declare who is to blame.
  • Label questions lookup, descriptive, comparative, causal, interpretive, or evaluative.
  • Identify what evidence would actually be needed.
Day 3 · Scope & Search Vocabulary

Turn Story Questions into Searchable Concepts

Build historically appropriate keyword families. Do not assume modern terminology maps cleanly onto the story's setting.

  • Extract concepts and generate synonyms, names, dates, broader/narrower terms, and period-specific vocabulary discovered in background research.
  • Separate terms supplied by the story/edition from terms introduced by modern researchers.
  • Complete the scored inquiry/language check at 80% or higher.
Day 4 · Query Forge

Search Is an Experiment

Test multiple queries and source environments. Treat result patterns as information about the search—not proof of the underlying claim.

  • Run substantially different queries.
  • Record what each query retrieves and misses.
  • Harvest useful terminology from credible background sources.
  • Preserve provenance immediately.
  • Record one search that caused the question to change.
Day 5 · Inquiry Defense

Can the Question Survive Challenge?

Defend the research question and the current literary interpretation as two related but distinct things.

  • State one current interpretation of the story and label its confidence level.
  • Name evidence that challenges or complicates it.
  • Explain which contextual question matters and why.
  • State what evidence could make you change your interpretation.
  • Preserve the question, search log, observation ledger, challenge notes, and reflection.

Sovereign Critical-Thinking Lab

The Assumption Lantern

Illuminate the Claim

Write one judgment about a character or event. Underline every word that goes beyond direct observation.

Expose the Assumption

For each underlined phrase ask: what must I believe for this conclusion to follow?

Find the Break Point

Describe a plausible piece of textual or contextual evidence that would force qualification or revision. If nothing could change the claim, it is not being treated as an evidence-responsive judgment.
