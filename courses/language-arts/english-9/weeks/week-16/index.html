Unit 06 · Week 16

The Rhetorical Situation

How do audience, purpose, context, and occasion shape a message?

Reading: Public-domain speeches, letters, and essays.
Writing: Analyze rhetorical situations, write a rhetorical précis, and adapt one effective strategy for a genuinely different audience.

Weekly Targets

By the end of this week, the student can…

  • Identify speaker/writer, audience, purpose, exigence, context, occasion, constraints, and message using textual and contextual evidence.
  • Explain how rhetorical choices respond to a particular situation rather than labeling devices in isolation.
  • Analyze ethos, pathos, and logos as overlapping rhetorical dimensions—not a three-bin sorting exercise.
  • Distinguish persuasive effectiveness from factual accuracy, logical soundness, and ethical responsibility.
  • Adapt a rhetorical strategy for a new audience while changing diction, evidence, explanation, tone, and emphasis as needed.
  • Use sentence types and placement to control emphasis.
  • Use accurately: rhetoric, audience, purpose, exigence, ethos, pathos, logos.
  • Complete the rhetorical précis, audience adaptation, and defined scored checks at 80% or higher.

Five-Day Rhetorical Situation Laboratory

Situation → choice → effect → adaptation → defense.

Day 1 · Situation Before Strategy

Who Is Speaking to Whom—and Why Now?

Read an assigned public-domain text and reconstruct its rhetorical situation before naming appeals or techniques.

  • Identify speaker/writer, intended audience, purpose, exigence, occasion, and relevant constraints.
  • Cite the text for what it reveals directly.
  • Label contextual claims separately when they require outside verification.
  • Write one sentence explaining why the message takes this form in this situation.
Day 2 · Choice & Effect Microscope

Rhetoric Is More Than Ethos, Pathos, Logos

Analyze a short passage for diction, evidence, structure, syntax, repetition, figurative language, address, tone, and appeals. Connect each significant choice to audience and purpose.

  • Select three consequential rhetorical choices.
  • Use Evidence Studio to map choice → passage → audience/purpose → likely effect.
  • For ethos/pathos/logos, explain the reasoning rather than simply assigning a label.
  • Identify one place where multiple rhetorical dimensions operate together.
Day 3 · Sentence Emphasis Lab

Syntax Directs Attention

Experiment with simple, compound, complex, and compound-complex sentences; opening and closing position; length; interruption; and deliberate variation.

  • Rewrite the same proposition in at least three sentence structures.
  • Move the most important information among opening, middle, and closing positions.
  • Explain which version best fits a specified rhetorical situation.
  • Use all seven weekly vocabulary terms accurately.
  • Complete the scored rhetoric/language check at 80% or higher; review and retry below 80%.
Day 4 · Audience Switchboard

The Strategy Must Change When the Situation Changes

Take one defensible message and adapt it for two meaningfully different audiences. Do not merely swap greetings or vocabulary.

  • Define each audience's likely knowledge, concerns, expectations, and constraints without stereotyping.
  • Decide what evidence, context, tone, explanation, and emphasis each version needs.
  • Draft both versions in PROSE and preserve the adaptation plan.
  • Write a rhetorical précis for one assigned source.
  • Explain one strategy retained from the model text and how it was transformed rather than copied.
Day 5 · Rhetorical Defense

Can You Explain Why the Choice Fits?

Present the rhetorical-situation map, précis, and audience adaptation. Defend choices with evidence and revise where the situation was misunderstood.

  • Explain the exigence without reducing it to a generic topic.
  • Defend one audience assumption with evidence or qualify it.
  • Separate “this may persuade” from “this claim is accurate/sound/ethical.”
  • Use peer, teacher, or Eiren challenge to revise one rhetorical choice.
  • Preserve annotations, situation map, language check, précis, adaptations, challenge notes, and reflection.

Sovereign Rhetoric Game

Audience Switchboard

No external application is required. One message enters; the rhetorical situation changes; the student must decide what should change with it.

Dial 1 · Audience

Shift from an informed specialist audience to newcomers. Decide what must be defined, explained, demonstrated, or removed without assuming one audience is more intelligent.

Dial 2 · Purpose

Keep the topic but change the purpose from informing to persuading or from persuading to commemorating. Identify which structural and language choices no longer fit.

Dial 3 · Occasion

Move the message from a private letter to a public speech or from an immediate crisis to a later reflection. Reconsider evidence, tone, urgency, direct address, and what the audience already knows.

Eiren · Language Arts Mentor

Eiren asks what changed—and why.

Bring a rhetorical-situation map, précis, passage analysis, or audience adaptation first. Eiren can challenge weak audience assumptions, device-labeling without effect analysis, and unsupported claims about persuasion.

Open Eiren
Try: “Ask me to distinguish topic from exigence.” · “Challenge my audience assumptions if I cannot support them.” · “Make me explain how this rhetorical choice serves this audience and purpose.” · “Ask whether I am confusing persuasive effectiveness with truth.” Eiren cannot write the assessed précis/adaptation, invent source evidence, assign verified scores, or override mastery.
