Unit 05 · Week 14

Duty, Power & Consequence

When obligations conflict, how should a person decide?

Reading: Sustained reading of Antigone or Julius Caesar with attention to competing duties, rhetoric, choices, power, reversal, recognition, and consequence.
Writing: Build a character-argument map and literary analysis that represents competing claims fairly before evaluating them.

Weekly Targets

By the end of this week, the student can…

  • Identify competing obligations and values without flattening the drama into a simple good-person/bad-person conflict.
  • Trace a character's reasoning through claim, motive, evidence/assumption, choice, immediate consequence, and later consequence.
  • Analyze how institutional, social, political, familial, moral, or personal power affects what choices are available.
  • Evaluate rhetoric by audience, purpose, appeal, language, context, and effect.
  • Recognize reversal and recognition where supported by the assigned drama and explain their dramatic consequences.
  • Use subordination to express cause, condition, concession, contrast, time, and qualification precisely.
  • Use accurately: tragedy, catharsis, reversal, recognition, rhetoric, consequence.
  • Complete the character-argument map, literary-analysis draft, and defined scored checks at 80% or higher.

Five-Day Dramatic Argument Laboratory

Obligation → reasoning → power → consequence → evaluation.

Day 1 · Competing Duties Map

What Does Each Character Believe They Owe?

Read the assigned section and identify obligations each central character recognizes: to law, family, community, state, friendship, honor, conscience, survival, reputation, or another value actually supported by the text.

  • Record each obligation in the character's own terms where possible.
  • Cite evidence showing why the obligation matters to that character.
  • Mark where two duties become incompatible.
  • Write the strongest fair statement of each competing position before evaluating either.
Day 2 · Power & Rhetoric Lab

Who Can Speak, Decide, Refuse, or Punish?

Analyze a consequential exchange or speech. Separate rhetorical effectiveness from moral correctness and examine how power shapes the scene.

  • Identify speaker, audience, immediate purpose, and constraints.
  • Trace two rhetorical choices and their likely effects.
  • Map formal authority, social influence, knowledge, vulnerability, and capacity for consequence.
  • Identify what choices are realistically available to each character—and what makes them costly.
Day 3 · Consequence & Language Lab

Choices Travel Forward

Build a consequence chain from one pivotal decision. Then use subordination to express causal and logical relationships accurately rather than stacking disconnected claims.

  • Map choice → immediate effect → response → secondary effect → later consequence.
  • Distinguish consequence supported by the text from hindsight speculation.
  • Revise ten sentences using cause, condition, concession, contrast, time, or qualification clauses.
  • Use all six weekly vocabulary terms accurately.
  • Complete the scored drama/language check at 80% or higher; review and retry below 80%.
Day 4 · Character-Argument Studio

Represent Before You Judge

Build the character-argument map and begin the literary analysis. The student must accurately represent at least one serious competing interpretation or character claim.

  • Map claim → reasons → assumptions → textual evidence → action → consequence.
  • Use Evidence Studio to distinguish evidence from inference.
  • Draft in PROSE with a thesis that permits complexity rather than announcing a simplistic verdict.
  • Include counterevidence or a competing reading.
  • Revise one paragraph so subordination accurately expresses the relationship among ideas.
Day 5 · Dramatic Cross-Examination

Can the Interpretation Survive Questions?

Present the character-argument map and defend the developing analysis through evidence-based questioning.

  • Answer one question about the strongest evidence for the interpretation.
  • Answer one question about counterevidence or a competing duty.
  • Identify one point where the character's reasoning changes, fails, or becomes more complicated.
  • Use peer, teacher, or Eiren challenge to revise one overstatement or unsupported causal claim.
  • Preserve annotations, argument map, consequence chain, language check, analysis draft, challenge notes, and reflection.

Sovereign Decision Game

The Obligation Table

This is not a game for finding the “correct” moral answer. It makes competing obligations and consequences visible using only the assigned text.

Round 1 · Duty Cards

Write each text-supported obligation on a separate paper card. Assign the cards to characters and rank them according to evidence about that character—not personal preference.

Round 2 · Collision

Place incompatible duties face-to-face. For each collision, identify what must be sacrificed if the character prioritizes one obligation over the other.

Round 3 · Consequence Test

Change one decision while holding the dramatic situation constant. Predict the first likely consequence, then label the prediction strongly supported, plausible, or speculative and defend that rating.

Eiren · Language Arts Mentor

Eiren cross-examines the reasoning, not the student's values.

Bring a character-argument map, consequence claim, rhetorical analysis, or draft thesis first. Eiren should expose missing evidence, unfair representations, false certainty, or overlooked alternatives.

Open Eiren
Try: “Make me state the opposing duty as strongly as the text allows.” · “Which part of my consequence chain is evidence and which part is inference?” · “Challenge my thesis if I have reduced this character to a villain or hero.” Eiren cannot decide the moral conflict for the student, write the assessed analysis, assign verified scores, or override mastery.
