Debate & Position Paper

How should an argument change after serious engagement with opposition?

Speaking & Listening: Conduct a structured debate in which listening, questioning, evidence tracking, fair refutation, and revision matter as much as prepared speaking.
Writing: Use what the debate revealed to substantially revise and publish a 1,200–1,600-word position paper.

Weekly Targets

By the end of this week, the student can…

  • Prepare a concise opening position grounded in the verified evidence dossier rather than a memorized performance detached from sources.
  • Listen actively enough to record an opponent's actual claim, reason, evidence, warrant, concession, and unanswered question.
  • Ask cross-examination questions that clarify definitions, assumptions, evidence, scope, causal reasoning, or consequences rather than merely scoring rhetorical points.
  • Refute the argument actually presented and distinguish refutation from contradiction, interruption, ridicule, or repetition of the opening statement.
  • Recognize when opposition exposes a weakness that requires concession, qualification, synthesis, or revision of the student's own position.
  • Participate in deliberation aimed at understanding tradeoffs and possible common ground without treating consensus as mandatory.
  • Separate speaking fluency, confidence, popularity, and charisma from argument quality.
  • Edit the final position paper for concision, emphasis, consistency, source integrity, and logical sequence.
  • Use accurately: cross-examination, refutation, synthesis, deliberation, consensus, revision.
  • Complete the structured debate, listening/flow record, post-debate revision memo, 1,200–1,600-word position paper, and defined Unit 10 scored evidence at 80% or higher.

Five-Day Debate & Revision Laboratory

Prepare → listen → question → deliberate → revise.

Day 1 · Debate Architecture Lab

Prepare an Argument That Can Move

Convert the Week 28–29 work into a debate brief designed for live reasoning rather than recitation.

  • State the current claim, scope, definitions, strongest reasons, and evidence.
  • Identify the strongest known counterclaim and legitimate concession.
  • Prepare source/location references that can be retrieved quickly.
  • Write three genuine questions whose answers could affect the argument.
  • Prepare a pre-debate position-paper draft so later substantive change can be demonstrated.
Day 2 · Listening & Flow Laboratory

You Cannot Rebut What You Did Not Hear

Practice argument flowing with short sample exchanges or peer briefs before the formal debate.

  • Record claim → reason → evidence → warrant rather than transcribing every sentence.
  • Mark concessions, qualifications, unanswered questions, and source references.
  • Separate what the speaker actually said from the listener's inference.
  • Summarize the opposing argument accurately before responding.
  • Complete the scored listening/refutation/language check at 80% or higher; review and retry below 80%.
Day 3 · Cross-Examination Workshop

Questions Should Produce Information

Build and test questions that clarify or stress-test reasoning without harassment, trick wording, or personal attack.

  • Clarify ambiguous definitions and scope.
  • Ask what evidence would change the speaker's conclusion.
  • Probe missing warrants, causal assumptions, comparison groups, costs, and tradeoffs.
  • Use follow-up questions when an answer creates a new relevant issue.
  • Use all six weekly vocabulary terms accurately.
Day 4 · Structured Debate & Deliberation

Argument Quality Over Performance Theater

Conduct the debate with accessible participation options and a preserved evidence trail.

  • Opening positions remain concise and sourced.
  • Participants flow opposing claims while listening.
  • Cross-examination follows the argument rather than a prewritten script when new information matters.
  • Refutations address claims/evidence/warrants; personal attacks and ridicule do not count as argumentative responses.
  • Close with deliberation: identify strongest surviving disagreements, genuine common ground, unresolved evidence questions, and any position changes.
Day 5 · Post-Debate Revision Studio

If Nothing Can Change, It Wasn't Deliberation

Use the debate record—not memory alone—to revise the final 1,200–1,600-word position paper in PROSE.

  • Write a revision memo identifying at least one argument heard and its effect on the paper.
  • Revise thesis, scope, evidence, warrant, concession, rebuttal, organization, or recommendation where the debate warrants change.
  • Edit for concision, emphasis, terminology consistency, transitions, and source integrity.
  • Do not manufacture a change of position merely to appear open-minded; explain when the evidence strengthened the original position too.
  • Preserve pre-debate draft, flow sheet, debate evidence, revision memo, final paper, and reflection.

Sovereign Debate Game

The Argument Relay

You may respond only after carrying the previous speaker's argument accurately across the handoff.

Handoff 1 · Carry

Speaker A makes a brief claim with one reason and evidence. Speaker B must restate the claim and reason accurately enough for Speaker A to accept the summary before responding.

Handoff 2 · Question

Speaker B asks one question that could clarify or alter the argument. Speaker A answers directly or identifies what remains unknown.

Handoff 3 · Move

Speaker B may rebut, concede, qualify, synthesize, or revise—but must name which part of Speaker A's argument caused that move. Then roles reverse.

Eiren · Language Arts Mentor

Eiren becomes the pre-debate sparring partner and post-debate revision examiner.

Bring the debate brief, source map, cross-examination question, flow record, or paper draft first. Eiren can test whether questions are fair, simulate a serious counterargument from supplied evidence, identify evasive responses, and ask what the debate genuinely changed.

Open Eiren
Try: “Make me restate this counterclaim accurately before I answer it.” · “Is this cross-examination question seeking information or just trying to trap the speaker?” · “Which argument on my flow sheet deserves a response in the final paper?” · “Show me where my revision is cosmetic instead of substantive.” Eiren cannot impersonate a real stakeholder, fabricate evidence or debate statements, score charisma as reasoning, choose the student's civic position, write the assessed position paper, assign verified scores, or override mastery.
