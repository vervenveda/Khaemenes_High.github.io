Debate case file · Community media lab

Motion. “The school should convert one general computer room into a supervised community media lab after regular class hours.”

Evidence for the motion. A student interest form records 46 requests for video editing, audio production, and digital publishing access. A faculty note states that the room is unused after 4:30 p.m. on four weekdays. Neither source addresses supervision cost, equipment wear, or access for students without evening transportation.

Evidence against the motion. A facilities estimate projects $6,400 in first-year upgrades. A technology coordinator warns that shared equipment needs an accountability procedure. These concerns establish constraints, not proof that the proposal cannot work.

Worked model. A strong debate case states a precise resolution, defines terms, organizes reasons, cites evidence, anticipates a serious countercase, and answers the countercase without changing the original standard midstream. Cross-examination should clarify evidence and assumptions rather than trap or embarrass a speaker.

Revision test. After debate, identify the strongest point made against your position. Revise one paragraph of the position paper so the opposing evidence is represented accurately before you answer it.

Transfer challenge. Build a three-part case: claim, evidence with source limits, and warrant. Add one fair question you would ask the opposing side and one answer that could genuinely change your view.

Day 1

Organize a case

Direct instruction

Order reasons by the reader’s questions, with explicit criteria and evidence; do not simply follow source order.

Apply and preserve

Outline a 700–900-word paper with two reasons, a counterclaim and a conditional recommendation.

Check: Answer the task, cite a precise detail, explain the connection and disclose a limitation.

Day 2

Fair questions

Direct instruction

Cross-examination tests assumptions and asks for evidence. A question should not force acceptance of an accusation.

Apply and preserve

Write six neutral questions and predict which answers could change your position.

Check: Answer the task, cite a precise detail, explain the connection and disclose a limitation.

Day 3

Accountable listening

Direct instruction

Paraphrase before responding. Separate disagreements about facts from disagreements about values.

Apply and preserve

Conduct a timed or typed exchange; record two paraphrases, two evidence requests and a concession.

Check: Answer the task, cite a precise detail, explain the connection and disclose a limitation.

Day 4

Substantive revision

Direct instruction

A stronger position may become narrower after discussion. Explain what the exchange changed.

Apply and preserve

Revise a reason and counterclaim paragraph. Preserve and explain before/after excerpts.

Check: Answer the task, cite a precise detail, explain the connection and disclose a limitation.

Day 5

Defend the paper

Direct instruction

A defense tests reasoning, not speaking speed or memorization. Accessible modes use the same evidence standard.

Apply and preserve

Submit the paper, source map, dialogue record and revision trail; answer two evaluator questions.

Evidence-Based English Architecture · Unit 10

Debate & Position Paper · Retrieval & Transfer

Grade 9 formalizes civic argument by requiring fair issue framing, evidence testing, accurate counterclaims, respectful rebuttal, and revision after debate.

Day 1 · Prepare

Build a debate map with claim, strongest evidence, counterclaim, rebuttal, definitions, and one question you expect from the other side.

Day 2 · Debate

Present the position and respond directly to opposing evidence without changing the subject or attacking the speaker.

Day 3 · Listen

Record the strongest point made by the other side and identify whether it changes, narrows, or leaves your position intact.

Day 4 · Revise

Use debate evidence to revise the position paper, especially overstatement, weak rebuttal, or ignored evidence.

Day 5 · Deliberate

Submit a final civic-reasoning statement: claim, strongest evidence, strongest opposing point, revision made, and remaining uncertainty.

Mastery boundary: These prompts strengthen argument, research writing, publication, audience adaptation, accessibility, professional communication, and reflection only. They do not change assessment answers, thresholds, learner-record authority, or completion rules.
