Unit 11 · Week 31

Audience, Genre & Multimodal Composition

How does a real audience change what and how a writer creates?

Reading: Literary magazines, digital exhibits, visual essays, audio features, and other mentor publications.
Creating: Define a real audience and purpose, analyze genre/platform affordances, transform meaning across modes, establish editorial roles and style, and produce a publication proposal with an accessibility-aware editorial calendar.

Weekly Targets

By the end of this week, the student can…

  • Distinguish a real or realistically defined audience from “everyone” and identify what that audience needs, knows, expects, and can access.
  • Analyze how genre conventions create expectations without treating genre as a rigid formula.
  • Explain platform affordances and constraints: what a page, screen, image, audio track, live presentation, or sequence makes easier or harder to communicate.
  • Choose modes because they contribute distinct meaning rather than because multimedia looks impressive.
  • Transform the same core idea across genres or modes while preserving factual/ethical integrity and explaining what changes.
  • Plan accessibility from the beginning through readable structure, meaningful text alternatives, captions/transcripts where needed, non-color-dependent meaning, and restrained motion.
  • Develop a style sheet that supports editorial consistency without erasing intentional authorial voice.
  • Assign production/editorial roles with clear responsibilities, handoffs, deadlines, and author approval boundaries.
  • Curate work according to a stated publication purpose rather than personal popularity or aesthetic sameness.
  • Use accurately: multimodal, genre, audience, editorial, curation, platform.
  • Complete mentor-publication analysis, audience/genre brief, multimodal transformation, style sheet, publication proposal/editorial calendar, and defined scored checks at 80% or higher.

Five-Day Publishing House Laboratory

Observe → define → transform → organize → pitch.

Day 1 · Mentor Publication Autopsy

How Does the Publication Teach Us to Read It?

Study several mentor publications as designed experiences, not just containers for individual pieces.

  • Identify intended/likely audience, purpose, genre family, platform, navigation/sequence, and recurring editorial choices.
  • Trace where attention goes first, second, and third.
  • Identify what words accomplish that images/audio/layout do not—and vice versa.
  • Record one accessibility strength and one barrier or risk.
  • Separate evidence from inference when describing the intended audience.
Day 2 · Audience & Genre Design Brief

“Everyone” Is Not an Audience Plan

Define the publication's audience, purpose, scope, genre mix, circulation context, and success criteria before choosing decoration or software.

  • Describe what the audience is likely to know and what must be explained.
  • Identify accessibility needs that should shape the design from the beginning.
  • Choose genre conventions worth keeping, adapting, or deliberately breaking.
  • Identify what is outside the publication's scope.
  • Create an audience/genre brief that another editor could actually use.
Day 3 · Multimodal Transformation Lab

Change the Mode; Track the Meaning

Transform one short core message or student-created piece into two different modes or genres and compare the results.

  • Identify what each version gains, loses, emphasizes, or makes ambiguous.
  • Do not use an image merely to repeat the adjacent sentence.
  • Write meaningful text alternatives or a transcript/caption plan where relevant.
  • Test whether essential meaning depends only on color, sound, motion, or an unlabeled visual.
  • Use all six weekly vocabulary terms accurately.
  • Complete the scored audience/genre/multimodal/language check at 80% or higher; review and retry below 80%.
Day 4 · Editorial Board & Style Lab

Consistency Without Flattening Voice

Build the small publishing house: define roles, workflow, style decisions, author boundaries, and deadlines.

  • Possible roles include managing editor, copy editor, design/layout editor, accessibility editor, source/credit checker, and contributor; combine roles when the group is small.
  • Define what editors may change directly and what requires author consultation/approval.
  • Create a concise style sheet covering recurring decisions such as headings, punctuation, capitalization, titles, credits, file naming, and accessibility conventions.
  • Design handoffs so no single contributor silently controls the entire publication.
  • Preserve role map and style-sheet rationale.
Day 5 · Publication Pitch Conference

Prove the Publication Has a Reason to Exist

Draft the proposal and editorial calendar in PROSE, then defend the plan to a peer, teacher, or Eiren.

  • State audience, purpose, theme/scope, genre mix, platform/output, editorial standards, accessibility plan, roles, milestones, and final deliverables.
  • Explain the curation principle: why a piece belongs beyond “we liked it.”
  • Identify foreseeable production risks and a fallback plan that preserves learning if a preferred tool is unavailable.
  • Revise after the conference.
  • Preserve proposal, calendar, style sheet, feedback, revision, and reflection.

Sovereign Multimodal Game

The Meaning Switchboard

Every mode must earn its channel. Students route one message through different forms and discover what each medium changes.

Channel 1 · Strip It

Express the core meaning in plain text only. Identify the information that absolutely must survive every later transformation.

Channel 2 · Re-route It

Transform the message into another mode or genre: visual sequence, audio script, captioned image concept, exhibit card, spoken introduction, or another appropriate form.

Channel 3 · Blackout Test

Remove one channel—color, image, sound, motion, or layout cue. Can the essential meaning still be accessed? Repair the composition when one mode has become an unnecessary barrier.

Eiren · Language Arts Mentor

Eiren becomes the audience and editorial design examiner.

Bring the audience brief, mentor-publication observation, multimodal transformation, style rule, role map, or publication proposal first. Eiren can challenge vague audience assumptions, decorative modes, inaccessible choices, inconsistent genre decisions, or editorial rules that flatten student voice.

Open Eiren
Try: “My audience is too broad. Ask me questions until I can define it usefully.” · “What does this image contribute that the words do not?” · “Remove one mode and tell me what meaning becomes inaccessible.” · “Does this house-style rule create consistency or erase intentional voice?” Eiren cannot invent audience research, grant rights or consent, certify accessibility without testing, take authorship of student work, make final curation decisions for the editorial board, assign verified scores, or override mastery.
