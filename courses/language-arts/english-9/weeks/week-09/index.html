Narrative Workshop

What makes a short story purposeful rather than merely eventful?

A story is more than a sequence of things that happen. A purposeful story creates pressure around something a character wants, chooses which moments deserve full scenes, compresses what does not, and uses detail, dialogue, pacing, and structure to make change visible. This week you will design, draft, revise, and publish an original short story.

Weekly Targets

By the end of this week, I can…

  • Design a short-story arc around a character goal, meaningful pressure, a decisive turn, and a consequence or changed understanding.
  • Choose deliberately between scene and summary instead of dramatizing every event at the same speed.
  • Use dialogue to reveal character, relationship, tension, or subtext while applying standard dialogue punctuation.
  • Control pacing through scene length, information flow, sentence rhythm, and selective compression.
  • Use concrete action and sensory detail where vividness serves the story without treating “show, don’t tell” as an absolute rule.

Day 41Story ArcW.9-10.3

Give the Story a Reason to Exist

Today’s Question: What turns a list of events into a story?

Learning Target

I can design a narrative around a character goal, pressure, a meaningful choice, and a consequence or changed understanding.

Launch · Eventful Is Not the Same as Purposeful

Event list: A student misses a bus, walks home, loses a key, gets caught in rain, and finds the key later. Many things happen, but nothing yet tells us what matters. A story begins to become purposeful when events pressure a desire, relationship, belief, fear, or decision.

Mentor Microtext · The Spare Key

Nadia had carried her grandmother’s spare key for eight months without using it. The brass had worn a pale oval through the lining of her backpack pocket. On Thursday, when the landlord’s notice appeared beneath the apartment door, her mother folded it twice and set it under the sugar bowl.

“Don’t call Grandma yet,” she said.

At 6:10 the next morning, Nadia stood outside her grandmother’s building with the key in her fist. The front door had no keyhole.

I Do · Story Spine
  1. Character: Who is positioned to make the important choice?
  2. Want: What does the character believe they need now?
  3. Pressure: What makes getting it difficult, costly, or complicated?
  4. Turn: What decision, discovery, refusal, or action changes the direction?
  5. Aftermath: What is different because of that turn?

A climax is not simply the loudest moment. It is the point where the central pressure reaches a meaningful turn.

We Do

Map The Spare Key: What might Nadia want? What pressures that want? Propose two different possible turns and explain how each would create a different story.

You Do · Story Seed

Create three possible story seeds. For each, write one sentence for character, want, pressure, and possible turn. Choose the seed with the strongest decision pressure, not the one with the most events. Build a 5-beat arc map.

Prove It

Write your story’s current purpose sentence: This story explores ___ by placing ___ under pressure to ___. You may revise it later.

Day 42Scene & SummaryPacing

Choose Scene, Summary & Pace

Today’s Question: Which moments deserve the reader’s full attention?

Learning Target

I can distinguish scene from summary and use both deliberately to control emphasis and pacing.

I Do · Two Narrative Speeds

Scene unfolds a consequential moment with immediate action, dialogue, thought, sensory detail, and specific beats. Summary compresses repeated, transitional, background, or lower-priority time. Good stories usually need both.

Expand into scene when…

a choice is being made; a relationship changes; conflict turns; new information changes understanding; exact words/actions matter.

Compress into summary when…

time passes; routine repeats; travel or setup is necessary but not central; the reader needs context without living every minute.

Pacing Is Emphasis

Fast does not always mean exciting and slow does not always mean boring. Writers control pace through sentence length, scene length, information density, dialogue, summary, reflection, and where they cut away.

We Do

Return to The Spare Key. Decide which moment should receive the most page space: the notice, the walk to Grandma’s, the discovery that the door has no keyhole, or what follows. Defend the choice based on story purpose.

You Do · Scene Map + Opening
  1. List 4–7 story segments.
  2. Mark each S scene or Σ summary.
  3. Star the decisive scene.
  4. Estimate relative space: short / medium / long.
  5. Draft the opening 250–350 words.
Prove It

Explain one moment you intentionally compressed and one you expanded. Your explanation must name the effect on emphasis, pacing, or reader understanding.

Day 43DialogueSubtext

Dialogue, Subtext & Concrete Detail

Today’s Question: How can a scene reveal more than its characters directly say?

Learning Target

I can write purposeful dialogue and concrete detail that reveal character, relationship, tension, and setting without unnecessary explanation.

Mentor Exchange · Two Versions

Flat: “I’m angry that you sold Dad’s camera,” Mira said. “I sold it because we needed money,” Theo said. “I understand but I am still upset,” Mira said.

With subtext: Mira set the empty camera case on the table. “Did you keep the strap?”

Theo looked at the sink. “It was cracked.”

“That wasn’t what I asked.”

I Do · Dialogue Has Jobs

Dialogue can reveal voice, relationship, conflict, concealment, power, misunderstanding, decision, or plot information. If a line exists only to explain facts both characters already know, look for a more natural method.

Subtext is the meaning operating beneath or around the literal words. Silence, evasion, repetition, interruption, gesture, and topic changes can carry it.

Language Lab · Dialogue Conventions

Use quotation marks for spoken words; place commas/periods correctly with dialogue tags; begin a new paragraph when the speaker changes; distinguish a dialogue tag from an action beat. Then vary sentence rhythm around the exchange so every line does not march at the same speed.

Sensory Detail With Purpose

Concrete detail is evidence for the reader. Do not inventory all five senses mechanically. Choose the detail this particular viewpoint character would notice and that supports atmosphere, pressure, memory, or action.

You Do · Complete Draft 1

Continue your story to a complete first draft of approximately 900–1,400 words. Include at least one purposeful dialogue exchange or, if the story intentionally contains no dialogue, create an equivalent character-revealing interaction through action/thought. Mark two places where sensory detail does real narrative work.

Prove It

Label one line of dialogue with its job and one concrete detail with its job. If you cannot name the job, revise or remove it.

Day 44RevisionStyle

Revision Changes the Story

Today’s Question: What should a writer change before correcting commas?

Learning Target

I can revise globally for purpose, structure, character, and pacing before editing locally for style and conventions.

I Do · Four Revision Passes
  1. Purpose: What pressure/change is the story actually about? Remove or reshape events that compete with it.
  2. Structure: Is the decisive turn earned? Are scenes and summaries proportioned to importance?
  3. Experience: Where does the reader need action, dialogue, image, sensory evidence, or strategic withholding?
  4. Language: Only now refine sentences, dialogue punctuation, verbs, pronouns, repetition, and proofreading.
“Show, don’t tell” is a choice, not a commandment. Showing expands a moment. Telling can compress, orient, transition, or move quickly through lower-priority information. Revise based on narrative purpose.
We Do · Revision Diagnosis

Take the sentence “Jalen was terrified when he entered the basement.” Create one expanded version that shows fear through concrete evidence. Then identify a context where the concise original might actually be the better choice because the story needs to move quickly.

You Do · Global Revision
  1. Read Draft 1 without editing sentences.
  2. Write a one-paragraph diagnosis naming the weakest global dimension.
  3. Make at least three structural/craft revisions, not merely word substitutions.
  4. Then make a local conventions pass.
  5. Preserve Draft 1 and the revised version separately.
PROSE Window

Now move into PROSE. Preserve the original as U3 Narrative — Draft 1; revise; then preserve U3 Narrative — Revised. Keep a revision note identifying what changed and why.

Prove It

Choose your strongest revision and write: Before → After → Why this changes the reader’s experience.

Unit 03 Narrative Demonstration

Today’s Question: Can I make purposeful narrative choices and explain what they accomplish?

Assessment Package
  1. Polished original short story: approximately 900–1,400 words.
  2. Craft map: annotate at least six deliberate choices across the story.
  3. Craft commentary: 250–350 words explaining how at least three choices shape the reader’s experience.
  4. Revision evidence: Draft 1 + revision note + final.
  5. Self-score: use the rubric before submitting.
Craft Map Categories

Your six annotations may include point of view, characterization, motivation, setting, internal/external conflict, scene/summary, pacing, dialogue/subtext, sensory detail, flashback/foreshadowing, suspense/irony, sentence rhythm, or another defensible craft decision. Do not force six different devices. Annotate choices that actually matter.

Craft Commentary Prompt

How did your most important narrative choices help the story become purposeful rather than merely eventful? Discuss at least three choices, quote or point to specific moments in your own story, and explain one meaningful revision between Draft 1 and final.

Final Read

Read the story aloud or use silent line-by-line tracking. Check speaker changes, dialogue punctuation, pronoun consistency, accidental tense shifts, repeated words, missing words, sentence boundaries, and paragraphing.

Mastery & Corrective Learning

8/10 = mastery. Below 8/10: identify the lowest rubric row, revise that exact dimension, explain the change, and resubmit. Do not start over unless the writer chooses to; correction targets the weakest craft decision while preserving the same standard.

Differentiation Without Lowering the Standard

Support the process, preserve authorship.

Support

Use a story-spine organizer, scene/summary cards, dialogue punctuation reference, teacher conference, reduced choice set, and sentence-level revision checklist. The student still owns the premise, character decisions, wording, and final craft choices.

Core

Complete the full planning, drafting, conferencing, global revision, local editing, craft map, and commentary sequence with normal course supports.

Extension

Experiment deliberately with nonlinear structure, unreliable narration, motif, restrained ambiguity, or a second viewpoint—then explain why the added complexity improves rather than merely decorates the story.
