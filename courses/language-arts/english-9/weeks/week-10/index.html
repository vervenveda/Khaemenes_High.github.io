Unit 04 · Week 10

Image, Diction & Figurative Language

How does a poet make an idea sensuous and memorable?

Reading: Short lyric poems across periods and cultures.
Writing: Analyze how individual words and images create tone and meaning, then compose and revise two image-rich poems.

Weekly Targets

By the end of this week, the student can…

  • Trace how a specific word, image, comparison, or pattern contributes to tone and meaning.
  • Distinguish denotation from connotation and explain why near-synonyms are not interchangeable.
  • Analyze metaphor, simile, personification, and imagery by effect rather than identification alone.
  • Use sensory detail selectively instead of decorating every line.
  • Revise diction for precision, implication, sound, and tonal consistency.
  • Use accurately: imagery, metaphor, simile, personification, diction, tone.
  • Preserve close-reading evidence and two original poems; reach 80% or higher on defined scored checks.

Five-Day Poetry Laboratory

Notice → test → manipulate → compose → defend.

Day 1 · Image Microscope

Read With the Senses

Read a short lyric once for experience and again for construction. Mark concrete sensory language, abstract language, repeated images, and places where an image changes significance.

  • Sort details by sight, sound, touch, taste, smell, movement, or internal sensation where appropriate.
  • Select one image cluster and trace its development.
  • Write a claim explaining what the image pattern contributes—not merely what it depicts.
  • Identify one line where removing an image would materially change the poem.
Day 2 · Diction & Connotation Lab

Synonyms Are Not Twins

Choose several consequential words from an assigned poem. Test replacements with similar dictionary meanings and observe what changes in implication, register, sound, rhythm, or tone.

  • Record denotation and at least two plausible connotations.
  • Build a word-family / semantic-neighborhood map.
  • Replace one key word with three alternatives and explain why the poet's choice may be more effective.
  • Use Evidence Studio when mapping a word-choice claim to several lines or patterns.
Day 3 · Figurative Language & Wordplay

Identify Less; Explain More

Move beyond “this is a metaphor.” Examine what two domains a comparison brings together, what qualities transfer, where the comparison breaks, and how it affects tone or understanding.

  • Analyze metaphor, simile, or personification through source → target → transferred qualities → effect.
  • Complete the Connotation Ladder challenge below.
  • Write six original comparisons; keep only the two that create genuinely useful perception.
  • Complete the scored vocabulary/diction check at 80% or higher; review and retry below 80%.
Day 4 · Poetry Composition Studio

Image Before Explanation

Draft two short poems from different constraints. One begins with an observed object/place; the other begins with an abstract idea that must be rendered through concrete image rather than direct explanation.

  • Create a sensory/image bank before drafting.
  • Choose a controlling image or image pattern for each poem.
  • Draft in PROSE and preserve the initial version.
  • Replace at least five vague/general words with more exact choices where appropriate.
  • Read aloud and revise one line for sound as well as meaning.
Day 5 · Close Reading & Poet's Defense

Every Word Has to Earn Its Place

Submit the close-reading annotation and two poems. Defend one analytical interpretation and one compositional decision using specific language evidence.

  • Explain how one word/image shapes the assigned poem.
  • Identify one deliberate diction choice in an original poem and the rejected alternative.
  • Use peer, teacher, or Eiren questioning to test whether the explanation matches the actual language.
  • Revise one weak analytical warrant or poetic line after feedback.
  • Preserve annotations, drafts, revision evidence, scored check, final poems, and reflection.

Sovereign Word Game

Connotation Ladder

No external tool is required. The goal is to experience how tiny diction changes alter voice and tone.

Round 1 · Neutral

Start with a plain word such as walk, house, look, old, or quiet. Generate five plausible alternatives.

Round 2 · Arrange

Place the alternatives on a tonal ladder—from gentle to harsh, formal to intimate, energetic to exhausted, or another defensible scale.

Round 3 · Prove

Use two alternatives in the same poetic line. Explain exactly what changed in image, implication, rhythm, speaker, or tone. A partner may challenge the ordering.

Eiren · Language Arts Mentor

Eiren asks what the language actually does.

Bring an annotated line, diction question, figurative-language interpretation, or original stanza first. The mentor's job is to pressure-test attention and reasoning—not provide a ready-made analysis or poem.

Open Eiren
Try: “Ask me what changes if this word is replaced with its nearest synonym.” · “Make me prove the tone using actual diction.” · “Ask whether my metaphor clarifies perception or only sounds poetic.” Eiren cannot author assessed poems, supply the close-reading answer, assign the verified score, or override mastery.
