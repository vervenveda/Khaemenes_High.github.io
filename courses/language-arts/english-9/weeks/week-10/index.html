Unit 04 · Poetry & Voice · Week 10

Image, Diction & Figurative Language

How does a poet make an idea sensuous and memorable?

Poetry is not a puzzle box with one hidden answer. It is concentrated language arranged to create experience, pressure, voice, pattern, sound, and meaning. This week you will learn how to enter an unfamiliar poem without panic, distinguish the poet from the speaker, trace imagery and diction, test figurative interpretations against evidence, and write two original poems whose craft choices are deliberate rather than decorative.

Day 46Close ReadingRL.9-10.1

How to Enter a Poem

Today’s Question: What can I do when a poem does not explain itself immediately?

Learning Target

I can begin an unfamiliar poem by separating what I can observe from what I infer, then build an interpretation from patterns in the language.

Launch · Poems Are Not Combination Locks

A poem can be difficult without hiding one secret answer. On a first read, do not ask, “What does this mean?” as though meaning were a password. Ask smaller questions: Who seems to be speaking? What is literally happening? What can I see, hear, touch, or locate? Which words repeat? Where does the language shift? What do I genuinely not understand yet?

Original Course Poem · After Rain

The sidewalk keeps the storm in shallow silver pieces. A bus passes and breaks the sky into rings beneath my shoes. At the corner, someone has left one red glove on the hydrant. Its fingers point nowhere. The morning keeps moving.

I Do · Five Entry Moves
  1. Situation: State what is literally present without explaining it.
  2. Notice: Mark concrete nouns, verbs, sensory details, repetitions, contrasts, and shifts.
  3. Question: Identify what feels important but uncertain.
  4. Pattern: Connect at least two details before claiming significance.
  5. Interpret: Offer a tentative reading and name the evidence that supports it.
We Do

Separate observations from interpretations: “red glove,” “hydrant,” and “morning keeps moving” are textual observations. “The glove symbolizes loneliness” is an interpretation that still needs evidence. What other readings could the glove support? What does the poem actually permit us to say?

You Do

Annotate After Rain with five observations, two genuine questions, one pattern connecting at least two details, and one tentative interpretation. Then write 120–180 words explaining how your interpretation grows from the pattern.

Prove It

Complete this sentence with evidence: My interpretation became stronger when I connected ___ with ___ because ___.

Day 47SpeakerVoice

Poet, Speaker & Persona

Today’s Question: Who is speaking inside a poem, and what evidence tells me who that voice is?

Learning Target

I can distinguish the poet from the poem’s speaker and analyze the speaker’s perspective, attitude, and limits using textual evidence.

Original Course Poem · Borrowed Coat

I still wear your coat in weather that does not quite deserve it. The sleeves cover half my hands. One pocket holds a train receipt from a city I have never seen. When people say it suits me, I say thank you. I do not tell them I am practicing the weight of leaving.

I Do · Do Not Collapse Author Into Speaker

The poet is the real-world writer. The speaker is the voice constructed inside the poem. A speaker may resemble the poet, but readers should not assume biography from first-person pronouns alone. Analyze what the voice says, notices, withholds, exaggerates, or cannot know.

We Do · Voice Evidence

What can we infer about this speaker from the phrases does not quite deserve it, I say thank you, and practicing the weight of leaving? Which inference is strongest? Which remains uncertain?

Language Lab · Tone Is Not Mood

Tone is the speaker’s or writer’s attitude as created through language. Mood describes an emotional atmosphere or reader experience. Avoid one-word labeling without evidence. Instead write: The speaker sounds restrained because…

You Do

Write 180–240 words analyzing the speaker’s attitude toward the coat and toward leaving. Use at least two precise word-level observations. End by naming one thing the poem does not allow you to know for certain about the poet.

Think About It

Why might a poet invent a speaker who sees the world differently from the poet?

Day 48ImageryDictionL.9-10.5

Imagery, Diction & Figurative Meaning

Today’s Question: How can a small word choice change what a poem lets us see and feel?

Learning Target

I can analyze how denotation, connotation, imagery, and figurative language work together to shape tone and meaning.

Original Course Poem · Kitchen Light, 2:13 A.M.

The refrigerator hums like someone trying not to wake the house. On the counter, three peaches soften inside a blue bowl. The clock advances without mercy, though nothing here is urgent. I drink water from a chipped glass and listen to the pipes carry other rooms away.

I Do · Four Layers of Word Choice
  1. Denotation: the conventional literal meaning.
  2. Connotation: associations and emotional or cultural resonance.
  3. Image: language that creates a sensory or physical experience.
  4. Figurative move: a comparison or nonliteral use that changes how we understand the literal scene.

“The refrigerator hums” is concrete sound imagery. “Like someone trying not to wake the house” turns that sound into a controlled simile. “Without mercy” gives the clock an attitude it literally cannot possess.

Word Laboratory · Change One Word
soften
What happens if it becomes ripen?
chipped
What happens if it becomes clear?
carry
What happens if it becomes connect?

For each substitution, explain what changes in image, implication, or tone. Precision matters because near-synonyms are rarely identical in effect.

We Do · Image Is Not Automatically Symbol

A blue bowl is first a literal object. It may become symbolic only if the poem gives readers a defensible reason to connect it to a larger idea. Repetition alone is evidence of pattern, not proof of symbolism.

You Do

Write 220–300 words explaining how two word choices and one figurative move shape the speaker’s experience of the late-night kitchen. Include one sentence that distinguishes what is literal from what is inferred.

Prove It

Revise one bland sentence from your own writing by replacing a vague noun, weak verb, and generic modifier with more precise language. Then explain the effect.

Day 49CompositionW.9-10.3d

Build Two Poems From Deliberate Choices

Today’s Question: How can changing speaker, image, or tone change the poem itself?

Learning Target

I can draft two original poems from deliberate constraints, then revise at least one meaningful craft choice in each.

Launch · Begin With Pressure, Not Decoration

Choose something concrete: an object, place, action, sound, texture, or recurring image. Then give it pressure: memory, conflict, uncertainty, desire, change, distance, responsibility, belonging, loss, wonder, or another tension. Do not begin by deciding, “I need a metaphor.” Begin by deciding what experience deserves attention.

Poem 1 · Image-Led Free Verse

Write 12–20 lines. Include at least three concrete images, precise verbs, and one figurative move that changes understanding. Do not name the central emotion in the first draft. Let image and action carry it.

Poem 2 · Change the Lens

Write 8–16 lines about the same subject or image field, but change at least one controlling condition: speaker, distance, tone, time, addressee, or structure. The purpose is not to make a “better” poem; it is to discover what changes when the lens changes.

Revision Pass
  1. Circle the most generic noun or verb; replace it if precision improves the poem.
  2. Underline one image doing real work; strengthen or reposition it.
  3. Box one figurative phrase. Keep it only if it changes understanding.
  4. Read aloud. Mark one place where rhythm or line break changes emphasis.
  5. Preserve Draft 1 before the meaningful revision.
Studio Rule

Choose any route below. Tools are equal options, not levels. You may also use plain paper or a standard text editor. Writer’s Grove may generate constraints only. It may not generate your poem. PROSE may preserve and revise a draft only after you have made the intellectual and creative decisions.

Handwriting Lab

Use handwriting if physical spacing, line breaks, arrows, marginal notes, or revision marks help you think. Preserve the intellectual work in your portfolio afterward.

Week 10 Poetry Demonstration

Today’s Question: Can I explain how poetic language creates meaning and make those choices deliberately in my own writing?

Part A · Unfamiliar Poem

Read the original transfer poem below independently. Annotate before drafting your claim.

Inventory at Dusk

One bent spoon in the sink. Two envelopes unopened. The basil on the sill leans hard toward the glass. Downstairs, a bicycle bell rings once, then disappears. I write milk on the list, then cross it out. Outside, the first streetlight clicks awake in an empty car.

Analysis Prompt · 300–450 Words

Develop a defensible interpretation of the speaker’s experience in Inventory at Dusk. Analyze how at least two precise diction or imagery choices and one structural or figurative feature create tone and meaning. Distinguish textual evidence from inference. Do not assume the speaker is the poet.

Part B · Original Poetry Portfolio

Submit both Day 49 poems in revised form. For each, preserve or describe one meaningful revision. You may not receive extra points for using a tool, a particular form, rhyme, or a certain number of literary devices.

Part C · Craft Note · 120–180 Words

Choose one poem and explain three deliberate choices: one in image/diction, one in speaker/tone or perspective, and one in structure/line/rhythm. Name one choice you removed or resisted because it did not serve the poem.

Mastery Rule

8/10 = mastery. Below 8, identify the lowest rubric row, complete a targeted corrective revision, and resubmit that component. The standard stays fixed; the pathway back to mastery stays open.

Which is harder for you right now: trusting a poem enough to stay with uncertainty, or revising your own poem enough to change something you initially liked?
