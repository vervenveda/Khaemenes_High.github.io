Reasoning, Evidence & Fallacies

How can a reader test whether persuasion is responsible and sound?

Reading: Arguments with stronger and weaker evidence, warrants, assumptions, causal claims, concessions, and rebuttals.
Writing: Build and repair argument maps rather than merely hunting for fallacy labels.

Weekly Targets

By the end of this week, the student can…

  • Separate a claim, premise/reason, evidence, warrant, assumption, qualification, counterclaim, concession, and rebuttal.
  • Evaluate whether evidence is relevant, sufficient, credible, representative, and accurately connected to the claim.
  • Distinguish correlation from a demonstrated causal relationship and identify missing causal steps or alternative explanations.
  • Diagnose reasoning errors by explaining what fails, not merely attaching a fallacy name.
  • Recognize that a fallacious argument may reach a true conclusion and a valid-looking argument may rely on false or weak premises.
  • Represent a counterclaim fairly and revise a claim through concession, rebuttal, or qualification.
  • Use transitions to signal reasoning relationships precisely.
  • Use accurately: premise, warrant, fallacy, concession, rebuttal, qualification.
  • Complete the argument map, reasoning-repair task, and defined scored checks at 80% or higher.

Five-Day Reasoning Laboratory

Map → test → diagnose → repair → defend.

Day 1 · Argument Anatomy

What Connects the Evidence to the Claim?

Read an assigned argument and reconstruct its reasoning before evaluating whether you agree with its conclusion.

  • Identify the central claim and major premises/reasons.
  • Mark evidence separately from assertion.
  • Write the warrant that connects one piece of evidence to the claim.
  • Surface one unstated assumption the argument appears to require.
  • Note where the argument qualifies its own conclusion—or should.
Day 2 · Evidence Stress Test

Evidence Must Do More Than Exist

Evaluate evidence for relevance, sufficiency, credibility, representativeness, and fit with the claim. When sources are used, distinguish source quality from whether a particular inference is justified.

  • Ask: Does this evidence actually bear on the claim?
  • Ask: Is there enough evidence for the strength of the conclusion?
  • Ask: Is the source appropriate and represented accurately?
  • Ask: Could omitted evidence materially change the conclusion?
  • Use Evidence Studio to map evidence → warrant → claim and flag weak links.
Day 3 · Causation & Language Lab

After This Does Not Automatically Mean Because of This

Study causal claims and transitions that signal reasoning. Students practice distinguishing correlation, chronology, mechanism, and demonstrated causation.

  • Identify whether a passage establishes association, sequence, or a causal mechanism.
  • Generate at least one plausible alternative explanation for a causal claim.
  • Revise transitions so they accurately signal addition, contrast, cause, concession, example, result, or qualification.
  • Use all six weekly vocabulary terms accurately.
  • Complete the scored reasoning/language check at 80% or higher; review and retry below 80%.
Day 4 · Fallacy Repair Shop

Explain the Failure, Then Repair It

Diagnose flawed reasoning without turning fallacies into a vocabulary scavenger hunt. A label counts only when the student can explain the structural problem.

  • Analyze examples involving distorted alternatives, personal attack, misrepresentation, circularity, overgeneralization, irrelevant appeal, or unsupported causal inference where present.
  • State exactly what reasoning step fails.
  • Repair the argument by narrowing the claim, improving evidence, supplying a missing warrant, or removing the invalid step.
  • Construct the strongest fair counterclaim and decide whether concession, rebuttal, or qualification is warranted.
  • Draft/revise the argument map and repair explanation in PROSE.
Day 5 · Reasoning Tribunal

Does the Argument Survive Inspection?

Present one mapped argument and one repaired argument. Defend the analysis through evidence-based questioning rather than defending a preferred conclusion at all costs.

  • Identify the strongest link in the reasoning chain.
  • Identify the weakest link and explain why.
  • Answer one challenge involving source quality, warrant, assumption, causation, or counterevidence.
  • Use peer, teacher, or Eiren challenge to revise one overclaim.
  • Preserve annotations, evidence stress test, language check, argument map, repair task, challenge notes, and reflection.

Sovereign Reasoning Game

The Broken Bridge

An argument is a bridge from evidence to conclusion. Students inspect the span instead of guessing whether they like the destination.

Span 1 · Evidence

Given a claim and several evidence cards, choose which evidence is relevant. Reject impressive-sounding information that does not actually support the claim.

Span 2 · Warrant

Supply the missing reasoning that explains why the selected evidence should support the claim. If no defensible warrant exists, mark the bridge broken.

Span 3 · Load Test

Add counterevidence or an alternative explanation. Decide whether the claim survives unchanged, needs qualification, or should be abandoned. The goal is structural integrity, not winning.

Eiren · Language Arts Mentor

Eiren attacks weak reasoning, not people or viewpoints.

Bring an argument map, source/evidence judgment, causal claim, fallacy diagnosis, or counterclaim first. Eiren can test assumptions and ask what would falsify or qualify the conclusion.

Open Eiren
Try: “Ask me what warrant connects this evidence to my claim.” · “Give me an alternative explanation I must test.” · “Make me explain the reasoning error without naming a fallacy.” · “Challenge my argument even if you agree with its conclusion.” Eiren cannot write the assessed map/repair task, invent evidence, determine truth by persuasion, assign verified scores, or override mastery.
