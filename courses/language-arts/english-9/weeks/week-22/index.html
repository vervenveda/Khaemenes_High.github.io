Unit 08 · Week 22

Structures of Explanation

Which structure best helps a reader understand a complex idea?

Reading: Definition, classification, sequence, comparison/contrast, cause/effect, and mixed-structure explanatory texts.
Writing: Design and draft an explanatory article whose structure, headings, examples, evidence, and transitions serve a real reader's understanding.

Weekly Targets

By the end of this week, the student can…

  • Identify definition, classification, sequence, comparison/contrast, cause/effect, and mixed explanatory structures from how ideas relate—not from signal words alone.
  • Explain why a particular structure serves a particular audience, purpose, and subject.
  • Distinguish sequence from causation and correlation from demonstrated cause.
  • Create useful classifications based on explicit criteria and recognize overlapping or imperfect categories.
  • Use analogy to illuminate an unfamiliar idea while identifying where the analogy stops matching.
  • Design headings and subheadings as an information hierarchy rather than decorative labels.
  • Use parallel list structure, consistent terminology, transitions, examples, and evidence to create coherence.
  • Combine structures when the content genuinely requires more than one relationship.
  • Use accurately: classification, sequence, causation, analogy, definition, coherence.
  • Complete the explanatory architecture, first article draft, reader test, and defined scored checks at 80% or higher.

Five-Day Explanation Laboratory

Diagnose → compare → engineer → draft → reader-test.

Day 1 · Structure Autopsy

Find the Relationship Under the Paragraphs

Read several explanatory passages and reverse-engineer how each one organizes understanding.

  • Mark what is being defined, grouped, sequenced, compared, or explained causally.
  • Draw the underlying relationship as a simple map.
  • Identify places where a text changes structure.
  • Explain why signal words are clues but not proof of structure.
  • Save one annotated structure map with textual evidence.
Day 2 · Structure Swap

Same Information, Different Architecture

Take one information set and reorganize it two different ways. Compare what each version makes easier or harder for a reader to understand.

  • Build two defensible structures from the same material.
  • Identify which information becomes prominent or obscured in each.
  • Distinguish chronological sequence from causal explanation.
  • Test a classification for clear criteria and troublesome edge cases.
  • Defend which structure best serves a named audience and purpose.
Day 3 · Information Architecture & Language Lab

Headings Should Reveal the Map

Engineer the explanatory article before drafting it. Build hierarchy, parallel lists, transitions, definitions, and an analogy whose limits are visible.

  • Write headings/subheadings that communicate content and hierarchy.
  • Revise lists for grammatical and conceptual parallelism.
  • Define unfamiliar terms at the point a reader needs them.
  • Use all six weekly vocabulary terms accurately.
  • Complete the scored structure/language check at 80% or higher; review and retry below 80%.
Day 4 · Explanatory Article Studio

Draft for Understanding, Not for Word Count

Draft the explanatory article in PROSE using the architecture from Day 3. Bring in verified evidence where factual explanation requires it.

  • Open with the reader's necessary orientation rather than empty generalities.
  • Use examples strategically to make abstract relationships concrete.
  • Integrate evidence and attribution without allowing citations to interrupt coherence.
  • Combine structures only where the content relationship changes.
  • Preserve the architecture and an earlier draft before substantive revision.
Day 5 · Reader Navigation Test

Can a Reader Reconstruct the Idea?

Give the draft to a reader without explaining it aloud. The reader reconstructs the article's hierarchy and main relationships from the page itself.

  • Reader writes the apparent purpose of each section from headings and content.
  • Reader identifies one place where sequence, causation, category, comparison, or definition is unclear.
  • Writer compares intended architecture with the reader's reconstruction.
  • Revise one structural problem—not merely sentence-level wording.
  • Preserve the outline, first draft, reader-test evidence, revision, language check, and reflection.

Sovereign Structure Game

The Explanation Architect

No external application is required. Students discover that structure changes what information a reader can see.

Round 1 · Card Sort

Write facts or ideas from one topic on separate cards. Arrange them by sequence, classification, comparison, or cause/effect. State the rule governing each arrangement.

Round 2 · Structure Sabotage

Deliberately place one card where it violates the organizing logic. A partner identifies the break and explains why it damages coherence.

Round 3 · Hybrid Build

Design an explanation that legitimately changes structure—for example, definition → classification → cause/effect. Mark the transition where the reader needs to understand that the relationship has changed.

Eiren asks why this structure serves this reader.

Bring the source text, structure map, outline, heading hierarchy, analogy, or draft first. Eiren can challenge false causation, arbitrary categories, weak analogies, redundant headings, and structural choices that do not match the purpose.
