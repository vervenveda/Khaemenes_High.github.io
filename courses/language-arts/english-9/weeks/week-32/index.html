Unit 11 · Week 32

Editing, Layout & Visual Rhetoric

How do design choices guide attention and shape interpretation?

Reading: Layouts, typography, image-text relationships, captions, credits, and visual/data displays.
Creating: Copyedit without erasing voice, build readable hierarchy, test image context and captions, document rights/credits, audit accessibility, and produce an edited spread or digital prototype.

Weekly Targets

By the end of this week, the student can…

  • Distinguish substantive editing, copyediting, and proofreading and apply the appropriate level without silently rewriting an author's ideas.
  • Use hierarchy, proximity, alignment, repetition, contrast, and white space to guide attention and reveal relationships.
  • Explain how typography affects readability, emphasis, tone, pacing, and perceived authority without treating typeface as proof of credibility.
  • Write captions that identify, contextualize, and credit rather than merely repeat what a viewer can already see.
  • Analyze how cropping, scale, sequencing, juxtaposition, labels, axes, and omitted context can change the interpretation of images or graphics.
  • Distinguish aesthetic editing from manipulations that materially misrepresent documentary or evidentiary content.
  • Apply the Week 31 house style consistently while preserving justified authorial exceptions.
  • Document source/creator, rights or permission basis, modifications, and credit requirements for visual material used in the prototype.
  • Test readability and accessibility without relying on color alone, tiny text, decorative contrast, unlabeled images, inaccessible motion, or visual clutter.
  • Use accurately: layout, hierarchy, typography, white space, caption, rights.
  • Complete copyediting exercises, visual-rhetoric analysis, rights/credit record, accessibility audit, edited prototype, and defined scored checks at 80% or higher.

Five-Day Editorial Design Laboratory

Edit → structure → contextualize → test → prototype.

Day 1 · Editorial Triage

What Kind of Edit Does This Piece Need?

Use a short student or mentor passage to distinguish revision, copyediting, and proofreading before making changes.

  • Mark meaning/organization concerns separately from sentence-level correctness.
  • Apply house style only where the rule genuinely applies.
  • Preserve intentional dialect, register, rhythm, fragments, punctuation, or typography when they serve the author's purpose and remain understandable.
  • Flag changes that require author consultation instead of silently imposing them.
  • Create a before/after record with a reason for consequential edits.
Day 2 · Attention Architecture

Design the Reading Path

Build two layouts from the same content and compare how hierarchy changes what a reader notices and understands.

  • Map title, deck/subtitle, body, pull quote, image, caption, credit, navigation, and supporting material as appropriate.
  • Use size, weight, position, spacing, alignment, and grouping intentionally.
  • Use white space as organization rather than treating every empty area as wasted space.
  • Test whether visual prominence matches informational importance.
  • Explain one design choice that improves comprehension rather than decoration.
Day 3 · Image, Caption & Context Forensics

A True Image Can Still Create a False Impression

Study how selection and presentation alter interpretation even when an image itself has not been fabricated.

  • Compare full-frame and cropped versions and identify lost context.
  • Examine scale, sequence, juxtaposition, labels, chart axes, and captions for framing effects.
  • Write a caption that identifies what is shown, supplies necessary context, and credits the source/creator.
  • Distinguish illustrative imagery from documentary/evidentiary imagery.
  • Record source, rights/permission basis, modifications, and credit requirements.
  • Use all six weekly vocabulary terms accurately and complete the scored editing/visual-rhetoric/language check at 80% or higher; review and retry below 80%.
Day 4 · Accessibility & Stress Test

Beautiful Is Not Enough If the Reader Cannot Use It

Audit the prototype under deliberately constrained conditions.

  • Check reading order and heading hierarchy.
  • Check text size/readability and whether essential distinctions survive without color.
  • Check images for meaningful alternatives/captions where needed.
  • Check audio/video plans for transcript/caption needs and avoid essential information available only through sound.
  • Reduce clutter, unnecessary motion, dense blocks, or decorative elements that compete with meaning.
  • Record each detected barrier and the repair.
Day 5 · Prototype Editorial Board

Defend Every Consequential Choice

Assemble an edited spread or digital prototype and conduct a formal editorial review before Week 33 publication.

  • Review copy against house style and author-approved exceptions.
  • Review hierarchy and reading path.
  • Review every visual for rhetorical purpose, context, caption, rights/credit, and accessibility.
  • Ask another editor to locate one misleading, confusing, inaccessible, or unnecessary design choice.
  • Revise and preserve prototype, edit log, rights/credit record, accessibility audit, feedback, and reflection.

Sovereign Editorial Game

The Attention Budget

Every page has limited reader attention. Students must spend that attention on meaning rather than noise.

Round 1 · Rank

Rank every element on a sample spread from essential to optional. Then predict the order in which a reader will actually notice them.

Round 2 · Mismatch

Find places where visual prominence and informational importance disagree. A giant decorative element may be consuming attention that belongs to the central idea.

Round 3 · Rebalance

Change only hierarchy, spacing, grouping, captioning, or sequence—not the underlying words—and test whether the page now communicates the intended priorities more accurately.

Eiren · Language Arts Mentor

Eiren becomes the skeptical copy chief and visual-rhetoric examiner.

Bring the original copy, proposed edit, layout description, image/crop, caption, rights record, accessibility finding, or prototype first. Eiren can challenge whether an edit changes voice, whether hierarchy matches importance, whether a visual creates a misleading implication, and whether a caption supplies enough context.

Open Eiren
Try: “Is this a correction, a style choice, or a substantive rewrite?” · “What will a reader probably notice first, and should they?” · “What meaning changes when this image is cropped this way?” · “Does my caption contextualize the image or merely describe it?” Eiren cannot grant copyright permission, determine legal rights conclusively, invent creator/source information, certify accessibility without testing, silently rewrite student voice, make final publication decisions, assign verified scores, or override mastery.
