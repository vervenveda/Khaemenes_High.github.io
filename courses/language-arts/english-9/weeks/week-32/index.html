Editing laboratory · Student journal feature

Draft excerpt. “The courtyard project was obviously a huge success, students really liked it and the new space made everything better. The team also planted native plants which helps the environment, and there was less trash after lunch.”

Editor’s evidence notes. A student poll received 86 responses: 61 rated the courtyard “better than before,” 17 selected “about the same,” and 8 selected “worse.” Custodial logs show an average of 14 fewer litter pickups per week during the four weeks after reopening. The plant list identifies eight locally native species. No data measure whether the project “made everything better.”

Worked revision. Replace unsupported certainty with evidence: “In an 86-response student poll, 61 respondents rated the renovated courtyard better than before. Custodial logs also recorded fewer litter pickups during the first four weeks after reopening.” This revision improves precision before sentence-level polishing begins.

Visual-rhetoric check. If the poll becomes a chart, the title should state the population and sample size, categories should remain proportional, and decorative graphics should not imply causal proof the data do not provide.

Accessibility check. Use descriptive headings, readable contrast, meaningful link text, a logical reading order, and alt text that communicates the informational purpose of an image rather than merely naming it.

Transfer challenge. Perform one substantive revision, one sentence-level edit, and one layout/accessibility improvement. Preserve before-and-after evidence so the evaluator can see what changed and why.

Day 1

Revision before editing

Direct instruction

Revision changes ideas and structure; editing repairs expression. An unsupported claim cannot be fixed with a nicer font.

Apply and preserve

Audit missing facts, unclear order and sentence errors; prioritize three substantive repairs.

Day 3

Sentence relationships

Direct instruction
