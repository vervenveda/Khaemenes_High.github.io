Portfolio laboratory · Evidence of growth

Candidate artifacts. A student has six possible portfolio pieces: an early diagnostic paragraph, a revised literary analysis, an argument map, a research-source evaluation, a multimodal publication, and a final reflection. The strongest-looking piece is not automatically the most useful evidence.

Curation principle. A portfolio should prove growth and breadth. Select artifacts because they demonstrate specific skills, revision, independence, or transfer—not because they received the highest score or have the most attractive design.

Worked model. Pair an early paragraph with its later revision and explain exactly what changed: for example, evidence moved from summary to analysis, the claim narrowed, attribution became precise, or counterevidence was incorporated. “I got better at writing” is too general to function as evidence.

Provenance check. Each artifact should record title, date or unit, purpose, whether it is original or collaborative, what feedback informed revision, and which course skill it demonstrates.

Completeness check. A polished portfolio with no research evidence or no revision history can still be incomplete. Curation requires representative evidence across reading, writing, speaking/listening, language, source use, and reflection where those categories were taught.

Transfer challenge. Choose four artifacts, write a one-sentence evidence claim for each, and identify one missing type of evidence you still need before the growth conference.

This scenario describes fictional sample records; students should use their own preserved coursework.

Day 1

Inventory skills

Direct instruction

A selected artifact should demonstrate a named skill. A high score does not itself show improved writing.

Apply and preserve

Inventory six artifacts across drama, research, explanation, argument and publication.

Check: Answer the task, cite a precise detail, explain the connection and disclose a limitation.

Direct instruction

Include early and later work and one challenging piece, not only favorites. Comparisons reveal decisions.

Apply and preserve

Day 3

Evidence-based reflection

Direct instruction

A growth claim becomes credible when a before/after passage shows the changed reasoning.

Apply and preserve

Write two 180-word commentaries comparing earlier work with revision.

Check: Answer the task, cite a precise detail, explain the connection and disclose a limitation.

Day 4

Completeness and provenance

Direct instruction

Artifacts need attribution, feedback and revision evidence. A score screenshot is not a writing sample.

Apply and preserve

Audit each entry. Recover a missing draft or disclose the gap honestly.

Check: Answer the task, cite a precise detail, explain the connection and disclose a limitation.

Day 5

Growth conference

Direct instruction

Recognizing a remaining weakness and planning a measurable step is part of growth. Completion alone does not prove mastery.

Apply and preserve

Submit the portfolio and a 400-word letter with two evidenced strengths and two specific next steps.

Check: Answer the task, cite a precise detail, explain the connection and disclose a limitation.

Evidence-Based English Architecture · Unit 12

Portfolio Curation & Reflection · Retrieval & Transfer

Grade 9 closes with visible evidence of growth: students curate work, revise major pieces, synthesize learning across the year, complete the cumulative assessment, and defend their choices orally.

Day 1 · Curate Evidence

Select artifacts that demonstrate growth across reading, writing, speaking, research, and language—not simply the highest scores.

Day 2 · Map Standards

Connect each selected artifact to the skill or standard it demonstrates and cite the specific evidence within the work.

Day 3 · Compare

Place an early and later artifact side by side and identify the most important change in reasoning, craft, evidence use, or independence.

Day 4 · Revise

Choose one major artifact for substantive revision based on current skill rather than cosmetic correction alone.

Day 5 · Reflect

Write a portfolio reflection naming strongest growth, persistent challenge, most important revision, and evidence supporting each judgment.

Mastery boundary: These prompts strengthen portfolio curation, capstone synthesis, cumulative transfer, corrections, oral defense, readiness planning, and reflection only. They do not change assessment answers, thresholds, learner-record authority, or completion rules.
