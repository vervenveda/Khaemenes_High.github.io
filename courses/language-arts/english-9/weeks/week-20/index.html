Unit 07 · Week 20 · Reading Hall Week B

Credibility, Bias & Counterreading

How do I investigate a source—and my own first interpretation?

Anchor reading: Guy de Maupassant, The Necklace.
Research: Investigate provenance, expertise, incentives, evidence lineage, context, corroboration, uncertainty, and limitations while deliberately testing the Week 19 literary judgment against serious counterevidence and alternative explanations.

PreviousDaily LessonsNext

Weekly Targets

Investigate both the source and the thinker.

  • Establish source provenance and use lateral reading before deciding how a source can responsibly support a claim.
  • Evaluate evidence for a specific claim separately from a source's overall reputation.
  • Distinguish genuinely independent corroboration from repeated dependence on one upstream source.
  • Identify bias, incentives, assumptions, missing context, and uncertainty without treating bias as automatic proof of falsehood.
  • Construct a credible counterreading of The Necklace that the text can support.
  • Separate historical/contextual evidence from textual evidence and avoid using either beyond its proper scope.
  • Revise or qualify a literary judgment when contrary evidence warrants it.
  • Use accurately: credibility, bias, corroboration, provenance, currency, expertise.

Five-Day Source & Interpretation Forensics Lab

Trace → corroborate → challenge → compare → revise.

Day 1 · Provenance Before Trust

Who Says This—and What Can It Establish?

Select one contextual source connected to the Week 19 question. Build its provenance record before using it to interpret the story.

  • Identify creator, publisher, date, source type, relevant expertise, stated purpose, and discoverable affiliations/incentives.
  • Mark claims known, inferred, unknown, or irrelevant.
  • Identify exactly which contextual claim the source can support.
  • Write one thing the source cannot establish about Mathilde or Maupassant's meaning.
Day 2 · Lateral Reading

Leave the Page

Investigate the source and one consequential contextual claim elsewhere.

  • Compare self-description with independent descriptions.
  • Trace one statistic, quotation, historical assertion, image, or cited claim toward its originating evidence.
  • Determine whether apparently separate sources share the same upstream evidence.
  • Return and revise the credibility/use judgment.
Day 3 · Counterreading Laboratory

Try to Defeat Your First Interpretation

Return to the Week 19 judgment. Build the strongest textually defensible competing interpretation you can.

  • State the original interpretation fairly.
  • Locate details it explains well.
  • Locate details it explains poorly or ignores.
  • Construct an alternative reading without caricaturing the first.
  • Identify assumptions required by both readings.
  • State what evidence would discriminate between them, if the text permits discrimination.
Day 4 · Context Without Determinism

Historical Evidence Informs; It Does Not Dictate

Compare verified contextual evidence with the competing literary interpretations.

  • Ask whether historical context strengthens, weakens, complicates, or is irrelevant to a particular claim.
  • Do not treat a general fact about a period as proof of one fictional character's motive.
  • Do not impose a modern norm without identifying it as a modern frame.
  • Use calibrated language: supports, suggests, is consistent with, complicates, does not establish.
  • Complete the scored source/language check at 80% or higher.
Day 5 · Judgment Revision Board

What Survived the Challenge?

Present the source audit and the literary judgment after adversarial testing.

  • Defend one source as useful for a specific purpose and identify its limits.
  • Explain one bias/incentive without claiming it automatically makes the source false.
  • Present the strongest competing interpretation of the story.
  • State whether your original judgment survived, weakened, strengthened, or changed—and why.
  • Identify what remains genuinely uncertain.

Sovereign Critical-Thinking Game

The Mirror & Window Board

The source is the window; the researcher's assumptions are the mirror. Good inquiry examines both.

Window

List what the source/text actually provides: words, events, provenance, data, context, evidence.

Mirror

List expectations you brought: ideas about wealth, happiness, marriage, responsibility, status, honesty, fairness, or success. Do not label them wrong; make them visible.

Collision

Find one place where the evidence resists your expectation. Decide whether to revise the expectation, qualify the interpretation, seek more evidence, or preserve uncertainty.

Eiren · Adversarial Mentor

Eiren should make a strong interpretation harder to earn.

Bring the source trail and both literary readings. Ask Eiren to challenge source independence, expose reputation shortcuts, identify certainty stronger than the evidence, or steelman the interpretation you currently reject. Eiren cannot declare which interpretation is correct, fabricate context, or write the assessed review.

Useful prompts: “What evidence am I ignoring?” · “Steelman the other reading without inventing evidence.” · “Which of my contextual claims exceeds its source?” · “Am I treating a modern assumption as historical fact?” · “What would make these two interpretations distinguishable?”

“Biased” and “I disagree” are not source evaluations.

Required evidence: provenance records, lateral-reading trail, source/evidence lineage, corroboration analysis, source/language check, three-source credibility audit, original interpretation, credible counterreading, context-boundary analysis, revision notes, and reflection.
