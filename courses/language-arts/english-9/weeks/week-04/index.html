Day 16Myth & genreRL.9-10.2L.9-10.6

What Is Myth?

Today’s Question: If myth does not simply mean “a false story,” what does the word help us study?

Learning Target

I can explain myth as a traditional meaning-making narrative and distinguish it from related categories without treating cultural stories as false or primitive.

Launch · Two Meanings, One Word

Compare these sentences: “It is a myth that humans use only ten percent of the brain.” and “The class is studying creation myths.” In the first sentence, myth means a false belief. In literary and cultural study, myth often means a traditional narrative that carries important meanings about origins, powers, values, identities, duties, or the unknown.

I Do · A Useful Working Definition

A myth is a traditional narrative through which a community can express or explore foundational questions. Myths may be sacred, historical, literary, performed, adapted, or still meaningful within living traditions. The category is analytical, not a ranking of truth or intelligence.

Myth: often connected to origins, sacred order, cosmic relationships, exemplary figures, or foundational meaning.
Legend: often connected to remembered people, places, or events, though shaped through retelling.
Folktale: often circulated as communal storytelling and may emphasize humor, danger, cleverness, morality, or social behavior.

These boundaries can overlap. Responsible readers use categories as tools, not cages.

We Do · Function Sort

Sort six possible story functions: explain an origin; preserve memory; model a value; warn against a danger; connect people to place; explore a mystery. Then discuss which functions could appear in more than one genre.

You Do

Write a 150–200 word explanation answering: Why is “myth = false story” an inadequate definition for literary study? Use at least three of today’s vocabulary terms accurately.

Respect Rule: When a narrative belongs to a living cultural or religious tradition, describe what the source and community say about it. Do not announce from outside the tradition what its spiritual claims “really” mean.

Day 17Oral traditionRL.9-10.2SL.9-10.1

Oral Tradition & Worldview

Today’s Question: What can a story preserve even when its exact wording changes?

Learning Target

I can explain how oral tradition can preserve patterns, values, relationships, and memory while allowing variation across tellings.

Launch · Memory Is Not a Photocopy

Retell yesterday’s definition of myth without looking. Compare your wording with a partner. Which ideas remained? Which wording changed? Oral tradition is not simply “bad copying.” Skilled oral traditions can use repetition, rhythm, formula, performance, audience knowledge, and communal correction to carry meaning.

Text A · Stories Before Pages

Long before a story is printed, it can live in voices. A teller does not carry a page inside the mind. The teller carries relationships: who acts, what is repeated, where the danger enters, which promise cannot be broken, which name the audience expects to hear, and what the ending asks the listeners to remember.

This means that stability and change can exist together. A repeated phrase may remain for generations while a local detail changes. A performance may answer the needs of a new audience without erasing the older structure beneath it. The story survives not because every word is frozen, but because a community recognizes what must remain meaningful.

For a reader, oral tradition creates an important question: when a pattern repeats, is it merely decorative, or is the repetition carrying memory, value, warning, identity, or relationship?

I Do · Worldview

Worldview means a framework of assumptions and relationships through which people understand reality. In narrative, worldview can appear through what a story treats as powerful, dangerous, sacred, ordinary, human, nonhuman, just, shameful, possible, or impossible.

Do not reduce worldview to one sentence such as “this culture believes X.” Instead ask: What relationships does this story emphasize, and what evidence supports that observation?

We Do · Reread for What Persists

Reread Text A twice. On the second reading, mark repeated oppositions: stable/change, word/pattern, individual/community, performance/page. Explain how one opposition supports the text’s central idea.

You Do

Create a four-row “worldview lens” for a story you already know: power, duty, relationship, consequence. For each row, write one evidence-based observation instead of a stereotype.

Optional Video · Reread

Asset ELA-READING-REREAD-001. Use it only as strategy reinforcement. The required work is the rereading and analysis on this page.

Think About It

Variation does not automatically mean corruption. Ask what a community preserves through repetition, structure, performance, and recognition.

Support

Core

Day 18Pattern & symbolRL.9-10.2RL.9-10.4

Archetype, Motif & Symbol

Today’s Question: How can repeated patterns help interpretation without replacing interpretation?

Learning Target

I can distinguish archetype, motif, and symbol and use each term to build—not substitute for—an evidence-based interpretation.

I Do · Three Different Tools

Archetype: a recurring broad pattern in characters, situations, images, or narrative roles across many texts. Examples might include a difficult journey, a threshold, a mentor, a descent, or a return.

Motif: a repeated element inside a particular text or group of texts that gains meaning through recurrence.

Symbol: a concrete object, place, action, or image that carries meaning beyond its literal role within a specific context.

Calling something “the hero archetype” or “a symbol” is only the beginning. The analytical question is: What does this pattern do here?

Text B · The Keeper of the Last Flame

Original course myth created for analytical practice; it does not represent a real cultural tradition.

When winter first learned to remain, every village fire went dark except one flame kept beneath a stone bowl at the edge of the valley. The keeper, Sela, was told never to carry it beyond the ridge. “The fire belongs where it was given,” the elders said.

Then travelers appeared on the northern road, carrying a child whose hands had turned blue with cold. Sela lifted the bowl. The flame bent toward the road as if it had heard the child breathe.

At the ridge, the wind struck hard. Sela could return and preserve the last fire exactly as she had received it, or cross the ridge and risk losing it. She stepped forward. The wind split the flame into a hundred sparks. For one terrible moment, the bowl was empty.

Then lights appeared across the dark valley—one in the travelers’ hands, three among the pines, dozens on distant hills. Sela returned carrying no flame at all. The elders looked at the empty bowl and understood that keeping was not always the same as preserving.

We Do · Pattern Before Label

First list literal repetitions and contrasts: flame/darkness, keep/share, bowl/landscape, one/many, obedience/judgment. Only then decide whether any element functions as motif, symbol, or archetypal pattern.

Language Lab · Pronoun Reference & Agreement

Repair ambiguity: “When Sela told the elder about the traveler, she was afraid.” Who is she? Rewrite so the reference is unmistakable. Then check: indefinite pronouns and their antecedents should agree in number, and every pronoun should have a clear referent.

You Do

Write one analytical paragraph explaining how one repeated pattern and one symbol work together to develop the story’s idea about preservation.

Do Not Overlabel

Not every fire is automatically “rebirth,” every road “the journey,” or every elder “the mentor.” Context decides meaning.

Support

Core

Extension

Day 19Responsible comparisonRL.9-10.2RL.9-10.9

Origins, Floods & Cultural Meaning

Today’s Question: How can we compare traditional narratives without flattening them into sameness?

Learning Target

I can compare two traditional narrative contexts by identifying a meaningful similarity, an important difference, and what each source emphasizes within its own cultural setting.

Comparison Rule: Similar motifs do not prove that two traditions mean the same thing. Compare function, relationship, emphasis, and context—not just plot resemblance.

Source Snapshot A · K’iche’ Maya Creation

The Popol Vuh (or Popol Wuj) records K’iche’ Maya creation narratives, Hero Twins traditions, genealogies, and community history. In the creation account summarized by the Smithsonian National Museum of the American Indian, earlier attempts to create humans fail before humans are successfully formed from yellow and white maize. The source emphasizes maize as sacred and connected to ancestry, life, and the keeping of days.

Read for: relationships among creators, humans, maize, time, ancestry, and responsibility.

Smithsonian NMAI · Creation Story of the Maya

Source Snapshot B · Mesopotamian Flood Tradition

Tablet XI of the Epic of Gilgamesh, preserved on a Neo-Assyrian clay tablet in the British Museum, includes a flood account in which the gods determine to send a destructive flood. Ea warns Utnapishtim, who is instructed to build a boat and preserve life. Gilgamesh hears the account during his own search for escape from death.

Read for: relationships among divine power, human vulnerability, survival, mortality, warning, and limits.

British Museum · The Flood Tablet
I Do · Compare in Two Directions

Weak comparison: “Both stories are myths, so they show that ancient people explained nature with gods.”

Stronger comparison: “Both narratives connect human life to powers larger than the individual, but they emphasize different relationships: the K’iche’ Maya creation account foregrounds successful human creation through maize and a continuing relationship to community and sacred time, while the Gilgamesh flood episode places human vulnerability and mortality inside a story about destructive divine power and the limits of escaping death.”

We Do · Similarity / Difference / Significance
  1. Name one genuine similarity.
  2. Name one difference that matters.
  3. Explain why the difference changes interpretation.
  4. Identify one claim you should not make because the evidence is too limited.
Language Lab · Pronoun Precision

Revise: “When the source describes the creators and the people, they show what they value.” Replace vague they with exact nouns. In comparative writing, pronoun ambiguity can accidentally assign a belief to the wrong group, narrator, scholar, or text.

You Do

Write 220–300 words comparing the two source snapshots. Your paragraph must include one similarity, one important difference, and one sentence explaining why responsible comparison requires context.

Support

Use the three-part frame: similarity → difference → significance.

Core

Write a full comparison with careful attribution to each source.

Extension

Identify a tempting overgeneralization and explain why the available evidence cannot support it.

Day 20Week masteryRL.9-10.1–2W.9-10.9

Cultural-Function Analysis

Today’s Question: Can I explain what work a myth performs using pattern, symbol, and evidence?

Learning Target

I can independently read an unfamiliar course-created myth, identify a defensible cultural function, and support my interpretation with precise evidence and reasoning.

Transfer Text · The River’s Promise

Original course myth created for assessment practice; it does not represent a real cultural tradition.

In the first years of the valley, the river changed its path whenever people argued about who owned its water. One season it ran beside the eastern fields. The next it bent toward the western orchards. Each village built walls to keep the water close, and each wall pushed the river farther away.

A young mason named Aro discovered a stone beneath the riverbed marked with a circle split into four open gates. His grandmother told him the mark was older than any village boundary. “The river remembers the whole valley,” she said. “We remember only the bank where we stand.”

Aro carried the stone to the four villages. Each council demanded that he place it on their side of the river as proof that the water belonged there. Instead, he set the stone in the dry center of the valley and broke every wall he had helped build.

That night the river flooded. It crossed fields, filled old channels, and ruined three storehouses. By morning, however, water stood in all four village wells for the first time in years.

The councils ordered Aro to rebuild the walls. He refused. “A wall can hold water for a season,” he said, “but it can also teach us to forget where the river must go.” The villages argued until winter. When spring came, they built gates instead of walls.

Independent Process
  1. Annotate the text for repetition, contrast, choice, consequence, and symbol.
  2. Write one interpretive question.
  3. Choose one primary cultural function the story performs: explain, preserve, model, warn, connect, or question.
  4. Form a defensible claim about that function.
  5. Select at least two precise pieces of evidence.
  6. Explain how one pattern and one symbol contribute to the meaning.
  7. Write 300–450 words.
  8. Complete the archetype/pattern map.

Create four labeled boxes: Recurring Situation · Repeated Motif · Central Symbol · Cultural Function. In each box, include evidence and a one-sentence explanation. You may identify an archetypal pattern, but you must explain what it does in this specific story.
