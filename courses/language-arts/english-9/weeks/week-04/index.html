Day 16Myth & genreRL.9-10.2L.9-10.6

What Is Myth?

Today’s Question: If myth does not simply mean “a false story,” what does the word help us study?

Learning Target

I can explain myth as a traditional meaning-making narrative and distinguish it from related categories without treating cultural stories as false or primitive.

Launch · Two Meanings, One Word

Compare: “It is a myth that humans use only ten percent of the brain.” and “The class is studying creation myths.” In everyday speech, myth can mean a false belief. In literary and cultural study, myth often means a traditional narrative carrying important meanings about origins, powers, identities, duties, values, or the unknown.

I Do · Working Definitions

Myth: a traditional narrative often connected to origins, sacred order, cosmic relationships, exemplary figures, or foundational meaning.
Legend: often connected to remembered people, places, or events, though shaped through retelling.
Folktale: often circulated as communal storytelling and may emphasize humor, danger, cleverness, morality, or social behavior.

These boundaries overlap. Categories are tools, not rankings of truth or intelligence.

We Do · Function Sort

Sort six possible story functions: explain an origin; preserve memory; model a value; warn against a danger; connect people to place; explore a mystery. Discuss which functions can appear in several genres.

You Do

Write 150–200 words answering: Why is “myth = false story” an inadequate definition for literary study? Use at least three vocabulary terms accurately.

Respect Rule: When a narrative belongs to a living cultural or religious tradition, describe what the source and community say about it. Do not announce from outside the tradition what its spiritual claims “really” mean.

Day 17Oral traditionRL.9-10.2SL.9-10.1

Oral Tradition & Worldview

Today’s Question: What can a story preserve even when its exact wording changes?

Learning Target

I can explain how oral tradition can preserve patterns, values, relationships, and memory while allowing variation across tellings.

Launch · Memory Is Not a Photocopy

Retell yesterday’s definition without looking. Compare wording. Which ideas remained? Which changed? Skilled oral traditions can use repetition, rhythm, formula, performance, audience knowledge, and communal correction to carry meaning.

Text A · Stories Before Pages

Long before a story is printed, it can live in voices. A teller does not carry a page inside the mind. The teller carries relationships: who acts, what is repeated, where danger enters, which promise cannot be broken, which name the audience expects, and what the ending asks listeners to remember.

Stability and change can exist together. A repeated phrase may remain for generations while a local detail changes. A performance may answer the needs of a new audience without erasing the older structure beneath it. The story survives not because every word is frozen, but because a community recognizes what must remain meaningful.

For a reader, oral tradition creates an important question: when a pattern repeats, is it merely decorative, or is repetition carrying memory, value, warning, identity, or relationship?

I Do · Worldview

Worldview is a framework of assumptions and relationships through which people understand reality. In narrative, ask what is treated as powerful, dangerous, sacred, ordinary, human, nonhuman, just, shameful, possible, or impossible. Avoid statements like “this culture believes X” unless the evidence truly supports them.

We Do

Reread Text A and mark oppositions: stable/change, word/pattern, individual/community, performance/page. Explain how one opposition supports the central idea.

You Do

Create a four-row worldview lens for a story you know: power, duty, relationship, consequence. Each row must contain an evidence-based observation rather than a stereotype.

Day 18Pattern & symbolRL.9-10.2RL.9-10.4

Archetype, Motif & Symbol

Today’s Question: How can repeated patterns help interpretation without replacing interpretation?

Learning Target

I can distinguish archetype, motif, and symbol and use each term to build—not substitute for—an evidence-based interpretation.

I Do · Three Different Tools

Archetype: a recurring broad pattern in characters, situations, images, or narrative roles across many texts.
Motif: a repeated element inside a particular text or group of texts that gains meaning through recurrence.
Symbol: a concrete object, place, action, or image carrying meaning beyond its literal role in a specific context.

Calling something “the hero archetype” or “a symbol” is only the beginning. Ask: What does this pattern do here?

Text B · The Keeper of the Last Flame

Original course myth created for analytical practice; it does not represent a real cultural tradition.

When winter first learned to remain, every village fire went dark except one flame kept beneath a stone bowl at the edge of the valley. The keeper, Sela, was told never to carry it beyond the ridge. “The fire belongs where it was given,” the elders said.

Then travelers appeared on the northern road, carrying a child whose hands had turned blue with cold. Sela lifted the bowl. The flame bent toward the road as if it had heard the child breathe.

At the ridge, the wind struck hard. Sela could return and preserve the last fire exactly as she had received it, or cross the ridge and risk losing it. She stepped forward. The wind split the flame into a hundred sparks. For one terrible moment, the bowl was empty.

Then lights appeared across the dark valley—one in the travelers’ hands, three among the pines, dozens on distant hills. Sela returned carrying no flame at all. The elders looked at the empty bowl and understood that keeping was not always the same as preserving.

We Do · Pattern Before Label

List literal repetitions and contrasts: flame/darkness, keep/share, bowl/landscape, one/many, obedience/judgment. Only then decide whether an element functions as motif, symbol, or archetypal pattern.

Language Lab · Pronoun Reference & Agreement

Repair: “When Sela told the elder about the traveler, she was afraid.” Who is she? Rewrite so the referent is unmistakable. Every pronoun should point clearly to the intended noun.

You Do

Write one analytical paragraph explaining how one repeated pattern and one symbol work together to develop the story’s idea about preservation.

Day 19Responsible comparisonRL.9-10.2RL.9-10.9

Origins, Floods & Cultural Meaning

Today’s Question: How can we compare traditional narratives without flattening them into sameness?

Learning Target

I can compare traditional narrative contexts by identifying a meaningful similarity, an important difference, and what each source emphasizes within its own cultural setting.

Comparison Rule: Similar motifs do not prove that two traditions mean the same thing or came from the same source. Compare function, relationship, emphasis, and context—not just plot resemblance.

Source Snapshot A · K’iche’ Maya Creation

The Popol Vuh / Popol Wuj records K’iche’ Maya creation narratives, Hero Twins traditions, genealogies, and community history. Smithsonian NMAI’s educational material describes successful humans as formed from yellow and white maize and emphasizes maize, ancestry, life, and sacred time.

Read for: creators, humans, maize, ancestry, time, and relationship.

Smithsonian NMAI · Creation Story of the Maya

Source Snapshot B · Mesopotamian Flood Tradition

Tablet XI of the Epic of Gilgamesh, preserved on a Neo-Assyrian clay tablet in the British Museum, includes a flood account in which Ea warns Utnapishtim to build a boat and preserve life. Gilgamesh hears the story during his search for escape from death.

Read for: divine power, human vulnerability, survival, warning, mortality, and limits.

British Museum · The Flood Tablet
I Do · Compare in Two Directions

Weak: “Both stories are myths, so ancient people explained nature with gods.”

Stronger: Identify a real shared pattern, then explain how different relationships, purposes, histories, and cultural contexts change what the narratives mean.

We Do · Similarity / Difference / Significance

  2. Name one difference that matters.
  3. Explain why the difference changes interpretation.
  4. Identify one claim you should not make because evidence is too limited.

Cultural-Function Analysis

Today’s Question: Can I explain what work a myth performs using pattern, symbol, and evidence?

Learning Target

I can independently read an unfamiliar course-created myth, identify a defensible cultural function, and support my interpretation with precise evidence and reasoning.

Transfer Text · The River’s Promise

Original course myth created for assessment practice; it does not represent a real cultural tradition.

In the first years of the valley, the river changed its path whenever people argued about who owned its water. One season it ran beside the eastern fields. The next it bent toward the western orchards. Each village built walls to keep the water close, and each wall pushed the river farther away.

A young mason named Aro discovered a stone beneath the riverbed marked with a circle split into four open gates. His grandmother told him the mark was older than any village boundary. “The river remembers the whole valley,” she said. “We remember only the bank where we stand.”

Aro carried the stone to the four villages. Each council demanded that he place it on their side of the river as proof that the water belonged there. Instead, he set the stone in the dry center of the valley and broke every wall he had helped build.

That night the river flooded. It crossed fields, filled old channels, and ruined three storehouses. By morning, however, water stood in all four village wells for the first time in years.

The councils ordered Aro to rebuild the walls. He refused. “A wall can hold water for a season,” he said, “but it can also teach us to forget where the river must go.” The villages argued until winter. When spring came, they built gates instead of walls.

Independent Process
  1. Annotate repetition, contrast, choice, consequence, and symbol.
  2. Write one interpretive question.
  3. Choose one primary cultural function: explain, preserve, model, warn, connect, or question.
  4. Form a defensible claim.
  5. Select at least two precise pieces of evidence.
  6. Explain how one pattern and one symbol contribute to meaning.
  7. Write 300–450 words.
  8. Complete the pattern map.
