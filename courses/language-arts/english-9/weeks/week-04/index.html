Why Humans Make Myths

What work does myth perform in a culture?

Reading: Creation, flood, trickster, and transformation narratives from several traditions.
Writing: Explain how a myth encodes values through pattern and symbol without flattening cultural differences.

Weekly Targets

By the end of this week, the student can…

  • Distinguish myth as a literary/cultural form from the everyday meaning of “false story.”
  • Identify recurring motifs, symbols, archetypal roles, and cosmological questions while preserving cultural context.
  • Make a defensible claim about one function a myth performs and support it with precise textual evidence.
  • Compare patterns across traditions without claiming that similar patterns make cultures identical.
  • Apply pronoun reference and agreement in analytical writing.
  • Use accurately: archetype, cosmology, motif, symbol, oral tradition, worldview.

Five-Day Learning Cycle

Study myth as literature, memory, explanation, and cultural expression.

Day 1 · Form & Function

What Counts as Myth?

Read one creation or transformation narrative closely. Separate plot events from the larger questions the narrative addresses: origins, order, identity, duty, danger, nature, mortality, or the unknown.

  • Annotate repeated images, actions, oppositions, and consequences.
  • Write a neutral plot summary before interpreting cultural function.
  • Identify one possible motif and one symbol; support each with a detail.
  • Record one question that requires cultural context rather than assumption.
Day 2 · Context & Comparison

Pattern Without Flattening

Read a second narrative from a different tradition. Build a two-column comparison that distinguishes genuine textual similarity from superficial resemblance.

  • Use ARSHIF or teacher-approved sources for basic context.
  • Compare one shared question or motif and one meaningful difference.
  • Label what the texts actually show versus what you infer.
  • Write a caution statement: what would be inaccurate to conclude about these cultures?
Day 3 · Language & Vocabulary

Reference Clearly

Study pronoun reference and agreement using sentences about characters, groups, deities, narrators, and traditions. Ambiguous pronouns can distort analytical meaning.

  • Diagnose ten clear/unclear pronoun references.
  • Repair ambiguity by renaming the precise noun when necessary.
  • Revise five sentences from your myth notes.
  • Use all six weekly vocabulary terms in accurate context.
  • Required scored practice: 80% or higher; review and retry below 80%.
Day 4 · Cultural-Function Analysis

Claim → Pattern → Evidence → Meaning

Draft a paragraph explaining one function a selected myth performs. The claim should be narrow enough to prove and careful enough to avoid speaking for an entire culture without evidence.

  • Build an archetype/motif chart before drafting.
  • Use Evidence & Citation Studio if tracking multiple source/context notes.
  • Draft and revise in PROSE.
  • Include at least two precise textual details and explain each warrant.
  • Audit wording for overgeneralization such as “all people believed…”
Day 5 · Interpretation Defense

Test the Claim

Present the cultural-function paragraph and archetype chart. A partner, teacher, or Eiren conference should challenge one inference so the student must defend, qualify, or revise it.

  • Name the strongest evidence for the claim.
  • Name one reasonable alternative interpretation.
  • Revise one sentence after the challenge.
  • Complete the weekly check at 80% or higher where scored.
  • Preserve the paragraph, chart, and reflection as Unit 2 evidence.
