Unit 03 · Week 08

Point of View, Structure & Irony

How does the arrangement of information shape a reader's judgment?

Reading: Short fiction using first person, limited third person, frames, withheld information, foreshadowing, and irony.
Writing: Analyze how perspective controls knowledge, then transform a scene by changing viewpoint and information access.

Weekly Targets

By the end of this week, the student can…

  • Identify first-person, third-person limited, and framed narration and explain what each perspective permits or withholds.
  • Evaluate narrator reliability using contradictions, omissions, bias, limited knowledge, and textual evidence rather than intuition alone.
  • Analyze how sequencing, pacing, foreshadowing, and irony influence reader expectation and judgment.
  • Transform a scene into another viewpoint while deliberately changing access to information.
  • Maintain correct pronoun case and consistent narrative person unless a shift is purposeful and controlled.
  • Use accurately: narrator, reliability, irony, foreshadowing, pacing, frame.
  • Complete defined scored checks at 80% or higher.

Five-Day Learning Cycle

The reader can know only what the narrative allows.

Day 1 · Information Access Lab

Who Knows What—and When?

Read an assigned story and build a timeline of information. At each major moment, distinguish what the narrator knows, what a character knows, and what the reader knows.

  • Identify the narrative point of view using textual evidence.
  • Mark three moments where information is withheld, delayed, framed, or emphasized.
  • Predict how the judgment of a character would change if one fact appeared earlier.
  • Write a claim about how information access shapes reader response.
Day 2 · Reliability & Irony Lab

Can We Trust the Telling?

Test narrator reliability instead of treating “unreliable” as a label. Look for contradiction, omission, self-interest, limited knowledge, exaggeration, and gaps between what a narrator says and what the text allows the reader to infer.

  • Create a claim → evidence → reliability judgment chain.
  • Identify one place where dramatic, situational, or verbal irony may operate.
  • Explain what the reader understands that a narrator or character may not.
  • Record counterevidence that complicates the reliability judgment.
Day 3 · Structure & Language Lab

Sequence Changes Meaning

Study pacing, foreshadowing, framing, pronoun case, and shifts in narrative person. Then manipulate sequence to see what changes.

  • Reorder a short set of story events and predict the effect on suspense or irony.
  • Diagnose and repair ten pronoun-case or person-shift problems.
  • Revise five sentences from current writing for controlled perspective.
  • Use all six weekly vocabulary terms accurately.
  • Complete the scored language/vocabulary check at 80% or higher; review and retry below 80%.
Day 4 · Perspective Transformation Studio

Rewrite the Camera

Select a consequential scene and rewrite it from a different viewpoint. The transformation must change what can be known, noticed, misunderstood, concealed, or anticipated—not merely swap pronouns.

  • Create a before/after knowledge-access plan.
  • Decide which details disappear, appear, or gain new significance.
  • Draft in PROSE while preserving the original transformation plan.
  • Audit for accidental shifts in person and pronoun case.
  • Annotate three deliberate changes and explain their effects.
Day 5 · Reader-Judgment Defense

Prove the Perspective Effect

Submit the perspective analysis and rewritten scene. Defend how the original author and the student's transformation each control reader knowledge and judgment.

  • Identify the strongest evidence for the perspective analysis.
  • Compare one moment in the source scene with the transformed version.
  • Explain how irony, pacing, or reliability changed.
  • Use peer, teacher, or Eiren challenge to revise one unsupported claim or craft decision.
  • Preserve analysis, transformation, language evidence, and reflection as Unit 3 evidence.

Perspective Experiment

Turn narrative structure into a small game of controlled information.

Use this three-pass challenge with any approved scene. It requires no external service and can be played individually or with a partner.

Pass 1 · Detective

List five facts available to the reader. For each fact, mark who knows it: narrator, protagonist, another character, reader only, or unknown.

Pass 2 · Director

Move one fact earlier and one fact later. Predict how the change affects suspense, sympathy, surprise, or irony.

Pass 3 · Trickster

Retell one moment through a different observer while remaining faithful to observable events. A partner identifies what became newly visible and what became unknowable.
