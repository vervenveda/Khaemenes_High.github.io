Unit 03 · Short Fiction & Narrative Craft · Week 08

Point of View, Structure & Irony

How does the arrangement of information shape a reader’s judgment?

Stories do not simply contain information; authors decide who can tell it, when the reader receives it, and what the reader is allowed to know before a character does. This week, study point of view, reliability, chronology, flashback, foreshadowing, suspense, and irony as deliberate controls on interpretation.

Day 36Point of ViewRL.9-10.6

Point of View Controls Access

Today’s Question: What changes when the reader can know only what one narrator chooses or is able to tell?

Learning Target

I can identify narrative point of view and explain how it controls access to information, thoughts, and events.

Launch

Imagine the same hallway argument told by the student who started it, a classmate who heard only half of it, and an all-knowing narrator. The event may be the same, but the reader’s evidence is not.

I Do · Point of View as an Information System
  • First person: a narrator inside the story uses I/we. Access may be intimate but limited.
  • Third-person limited: the narrator remains outside the action but closely follows one character’s knowledge or consciousness.
  • Third-person omniscient: the narrator may move across multiple minds, places, or knowledge sets.

Do not stop at naming the viewpoint. Ask: What can this narrator know? What can this narrator not know? What can this narrator choose not to say?

Course Text · The Photograph in the Drawer, Part I

I noticed the leak before anyone else did. A thin brown line had appeared above the student-art display, directly over Mina’s photograph of the harbor. Everyone was busy arranging chairs for the open house, and Mina had gone downstairs to find tape.

I took the photograph out of its frame. That sounds worse than it was. The paper was already beginning to curl at one corner, and I knew the supply cabinet beside the sink stayed dry. I slid the photograph into the top drawer and closed it.

I meant to tell Mina as soon as she came back.

Then Principal Sayeed entered with three parents, Ms. Ortiz asked me to carry programs to the lobby, and someone shouted that the ceiling tile had started dripping. By the time Mina returned, six people were standing around the empty frame.

“Where is it?” she asked.

I had the cabinet key in my pocket. I could feel its square metal edge against my hand.

“I don’t know,” I said.

We Do

Create two columns: Sam can know and Sam cannot know. Then add a third: Sam may be choosing not to tell us yet. Which category creates the most interpretive tension?

Language Lab · Pronoun Case

Choose the correct form and explain why: “Mina and (I/me) arranged the display.” “Ms. Ortiz spoke to Mina and (I/me).” “The disagreement stayed between Mina and (I/me).” Then check that your own response does not accidentally shift from I to you or they.

You Do

Write 160–220 words: How does first-person narration shape your judgment of Sam so far? Use at least two details and identify one important fact the reader does not yet have.

Prove It

Underline the sentence where you explain an effect of point of view rather than merely naming the point of view.

Think About It

A limited narrator is not automatically unreliable. Limited knowledge and distorted reporting are different problems.

Day 37ReliabilityEvidence Cross-Check

Can We Trust the Narrator?

Today’s Question: What evidence allows a reader to question a narrator without simply calling that narrator a liar?

Learning Target

I can evaluate narrator reliability by comparing claims with actions, omissions, contradictions, motives, and other textual evidence.

I Do · Reliability Is a Spectrum

A narrator may be accurate about events but mistaken about motives; honest but limited; self-protective; biased; confused; exaggerating; or deliberately deceptive. The analytical task is not “trust/don’t trust.” It is: Which parts of the account deserve confidence, and which require verification?

The Photograph in the Drawer, Part II

I still wasn’t hiding the photograph. Hiding means you do not want something found. I wanted Mina to have it back; I just needed the right moment to explain why I had taken it.

When she asked whether anyone had seen me near the display, I remembered the cabinet key in my pocket and moved my hand away from it.

“Sam?” she said.

“I was carrying programs.” That was true.

The leak widened. Water struck the empty frame and ran down the wall in clear lines. Ms. Ortiz told everyone to move the remaining work. I could have opened the drawer then. Instead, I waited until the room had nearly emptied.

Mina was the last person left besides me.

I unlocked the cabinet and placed the photograph on the table.

She looked at the dry picture, then at the wet wall, then at me.

“You saved it,” she said.

I nodded too quickly.

“Then why did you say you didn’t know where it was?”

I had spent twenty minutes preparing an answer to a different question.

We Do · Reliability Audit
Narrator claimTextual evidenceEffect on reliability
“I still wasn’t hiding the photograph.”Key in pocket; waits until room emptiesClaim and behavior do not fully match.
“I was carrying programs.”The statement is factually true.A true statement can still be strategically incomplete.
You Do

Write 180–240 words answering: Is Sam unreliable about facts, motives, or both? Use at least three pieces of evidence and distinguish between what Sam says happened and how Sam interprets the behavior.

Prove It

Add one sentence beginning: “The strongest reason to trust Sam is…” and another beginning: “The strongest reason to question Sam is…”

Optional Reinforcement

Asset: ELA-UNRELIABLE-NARRATOR-002. Optional reinforcement; autoplay off. Written instruction remains required.

Important Distinction

Bias is not automatically dishonesty. A narrator can sincerely interpret events through a distorted or incomplete perspective.

Day 38StructureRL.9-10.5

Structure Changes Meaning

Today’s Question: How does changing the order of events change what the reader expects, suspects, or understands?

Learning Target

I can distinguish linear and non-linear structure and explain how flashback, foreshadowing, and pacing control information.

I Do · Structure Is Information Timing
  • Linear: events are primarily presented in chronological order.
  • Non-linear: the narration rearranges chronology through flashback, frames, jumps, parallel timelines, or delayed explanation.
  • Flashback: moves backward to reveal a past event relevant to the present.
  • Foreshadowing: places a clue or signal that gains meaning when a later event occurs.
  • Pacing: controls how much narrative time and attention an event receives.

Version A · Chronological

Sam sees the leak, removes Mina’s photograph, puts it in the drawer, lies about knowing where it is, waits for the room to empty, and finally returns it.

Version B · Non-Linear Opening

“Then why did you say you didn’t know where it was?” Mina asked.

Twenty minutes earlier, before the photograph disappeared, I had been the first person to notice the leak.

We Do

Compare the two openings. In Version A, when does suspicion begin? In Version B, what question does the reader carry into the flashback? Which version makes Sam’s earlier choices feel more consequential?

Foreshadowing Test

Return to Part I: “I had the cabinet key in my pocket. I could feel its square metal edge against my hand.” Explain how this detail functions differently before and after the reader learns Sam’s full behavior.

You Do

Reorder six key events from The Photograph in the Drawer into a purposeful non-linear sequence. Then write 160–220 words explaining why your first event should appear first and what expectation or question it creates.

Day 39SuspenseIrony

Expectation Is a Craft Tool

Today’s Question: How do authors use what readers expect—and what readers know—to create suspense, surprise, humor, or criticism?

Learning Target

I can distinguish suspense from irony, identify three major types of irony, and explain how information differences shape reader response.

I Do · Suspense vs. Irony

Suspense grows from uncertainty, anticipation, delayed information, risk, and controlled pacing. Irony grows from a meaningful contrast: between words and intended meaning, expectation and outcome, or character knowledge and audience knowledge.

  • Verbal irony: spoken/literal meaning contrasts with intended meaning.
  • Situational irony: actual outcome meaningfully contrasts with a reasonable expectation.
  • Dramatic irony: the audience knows something important that a character does not.

Three Micro-Scenes

A · Verbal: The lights failed one minute before the presentation. “Excellent,” Nia said, staring at the black projector screen. “Exactly how I rehearsed it.”

B · Situational: The school launched a campaign titled Never Lose Another Thing. By lunch, the bright yellow demonstration box containing the new tracking tags had disappeared.

C · Dramatic: Malik searched every drawer for the scholarship letter. The reader has already watched his little brother slide it beneath the keyboard to keep it safe from spilled juice. Malik has not.

We Do

For each scene, identify the two things being contrasted. Then answer: What does the irony make the reader notice that a literal reading would miss?

Suspense Lab

Take Scene C. Delay Malik’s discovery by 100 words without adding a new danger. Use pacing, interruption, narrowed attention, and controlled information. Suspense should come from waiting for a known or uncertain outcome, not from random threats.

You Do · Perspective Rewrite Plan

Choose one scene from this week and plan a 180–260 word rewrite from a different point of view. Record: original narrator → new narrator → information gained → information lost → expected change in sympathy or tension.

PROSE

Draft the rewrite independently first. Then move it into PROSE, preserve Draft 1, make one structural or perspective revision, and save a short revision note.

Irony Overview

Asset: ELA-IRONY-TYPES-001. The literary-examples asset ELA-IRONY-TYPES-002 is a useful alternate.

Suspense Extension

Do not confuse coincidence with irony. An unexpected event becomes situational irony only when the reversal carries a meaningful relationship to the expectation it overturns.
Day 40Independent Transfer80% Mastery

Information Design Demonstration

Today’s Question: Can I explain how point of view and structure work together to shape a reader’s judgment of an unfamiliar story?

Learning Target

I can independently analyze point of view, reliability, structure, suspense or irony, and then transform a scene by changing perspective.

Assessment Rule

No video is required during the assessment. Read the unfamiliar text first. Annotate independently. Build your claim and evidence plan before opening PROSE.

Unfamiliar Course Text · The Red Envelope

The red envelope was waiting on my desk when I arrived for second period.

I knew immediately what it meant.

Yesterday, after debate practice, I had seen Jordan switch two score sheets on Coach Ren’s table. I had not helped. I had not touched anything. But I had also not said anything, and by midnight that distinction had begun to feel much smaller than it had at four o’clock.

I turned the envelope over. My name was written across the front in Coach Ren’s careful block letters.

“You opening that?” Leila asked.

“Later.”

She raised an eyebrow. “You’ve been waiting for those results all week.”

I slid the envelope under my notebook.

The night before returned to me in pieces: Jordan laughing too loudly; the scrape of paper against paper; my own hand on the door; the thought Someone else will notice. I had replayed that sentence so many times that it no longer sounded like mine.

At 10:14, the intercom clicked on. Coach Ren’s voice filled the room.

“Would the student selected to represent North Hall at the regional forum please report to the debate room after third period?”

Leila looked at me. “That has to be you.”

I heard only the word report.

At the end of class, I carried the unopened envelope to the debate room. Jordan was already there, sitting beside Coach Ren.

My stomach dropped.

“Before you say anything,” I said, “there’s something I should have told you yesterday.”

Jordan stared at the floor.

Coach Ren did not interrupt.

I explained what I had seen and what I had failed to do. When I finished, Coach Ren folded his hands.

“Thank you for telling me,” he said. “We’ll deal with the score sheets separately.” Then he pointed to the envelope. “You should probably open that.”

Inside was a letter naming me the regional forum representative.

For several seconds I could not make the two facts occupy the same room.

Coach Ren said, “Being selected does not mean you never make a poor choice. It means what you do next still matters.”

I read the letter again. This time, I noticed it had been dated yesterday morning.

Part A · Analysis · 350–500 Words

Answer: How do first-person point of view and the story’s arrangement of information shape the reader’s judgment of the narrator? Your response must include:

  • a specific, arguable claim;
  • at least three precise pieces of textual evidence;
  • analysis of point of view and narrator reliability;
  • analysis of at least one structural choice: flashback, delayed information, pacing, or foreshadowing;
  • analysis of suspense or irony;
  • a concluding insight about why the information design matters.
Part B · Perspective Transformation · 180–260 Words

Rewrite one scene from Coach Ren’s or Leila’s point of view. Do not merely replace pronouns. Change the information available, observations noticed, and interpretation of the narrator’s behavior.

Part C · Craft Note · 100–140 Words

Explain one effect your perspective change creates. Name at least one piece of information gained and one piece lost.

Revision

After Draft 1 is complete, use PROSE to preserve it. Revise one reasoning paragraph in Part A and one information-design choice in Part B. Save the final versions and a brief revision note.
