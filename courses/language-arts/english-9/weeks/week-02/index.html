Day 6Claim formationRL.9-10.1W.9-10.9

From Topic to Claim

Today’s Question: What makes an interpretation defensible instead of merely personal?

Learning Target

I can transform observations and inferences into a specific literary claim that another careful reader could test against the text.

Launch · The Claim Ladder

Rank these statements from least analytical to most analytical:

  1. The story is about responsibility.
  2. I think Lena is responsible.
  3. Lena returns the key to the windowsill.
  4. Lena begins to understand responsibility when she chooses to repair a mistake that no one else knows she made.

The strongest statement does more than name a topic or preference. It makes a specific, arguable, evidence-testable interpretation.

I Do · Five Levels of Thinking
  1. Topic: a subject — responsibility.
  2. Observation: something directly visible — Lena returns the key.
  3. Opinion: a personal judgment — Lena is a good person.
  4. Inference: a reasoned idea — returning the key suggests regret.
  5. Claim: a defensible interpretation that answers a meaningful question — Lena’s private decision to return the key shows that responsibility begins when she stops measuring right action by whether anyone is watching.

A literary claim should pass three tests: specific, arguable, supportable.

Model · Strengthen the Claim

Weak: The key is important.
Better: The key represents responsibility.
Stronger: The key becomes a symbol of responsibility because Lena’s changing treatment of it mirrors her shift from avoiding consequences to accepting them.

The strongest version identifies both an idea and the textual pattern that makes the interpretation defensible.

We Do

Read Text A below. Generate one question beginning with Why or How. Then build a claim ladder: topic → observation → inference → claim.

You Do

Write two different defensible claims about Lena. Each claim must be supportable with at least two details from Text A. Underline the words that make each claim arguable rather than factual.

Text A · The Key on the Windowsill

Lena found the key in her coat pocket two days after the rain. It belonged to the old greenhouse behind the school, a narrow iron key with a square bow and a stripe of green paint near the teeth. She remembered borrowing it from Mr. Vale to bring in the seed trays before the storm. She also remembered telling him, with complete confidence, that she had returned it.

For two mornings she carried the key back to school and carried it home again. Each time she reached the greenhouse path, someone seemed to be nearby. On Monday, the groundskeeper was trimming the hedge. On Tuesday, two students were filling watering cans. Lena told herself she was waiting for privacy because an explanation would take too long. Yet she noticed that the key felt heavier when someone else could see it.

Wednesday arrived clear and cold. The greenhouse door stood chained because Mr. Vale had begun using the side entrance. Lena could have slipped the key under his office door. Instead, she walked to the greenhouse and placed it on the stone windowsill where he always left his gloves. The gesture felt absurdly small. No bell rang. No one stepped from behind the hedge. Nothing announced that she had corrected anything.

She had almost reached the building when she turned back. The key was still there, bright against the dark stone. Lena looked at it for a long moment, then kept walking. For the first time all week, she did not check whether anyone had seen her.

Day 7Analytical paragraphW.9-10.2L.9-10.1

Build a Paragraph of Analysis

Today’s Question: How does a paragraph prove rather than merely state an interpretation?

Learning Target

I can build an analytical paragraph in which every sentence contributes to a claim, evidence, explanation, or significance.

I Do · C-E-E-S
  1. Claim: answer the interpretive question.
  2. Evidence: provide precise textual support.
  3. Explanation: explain how the evidence supports the claim.
  4. Significance: explain why the interpretation matters to the text’s larger meaning.

This is not a rigid four-sentence formula. It is a reasoning sequence. Strong paragraphs may use multiple evidence-explanation pairs.

Model

Claim: Lena’s changing relationship to the key shows that responsibility becomes meaningful when she stops performing it for an audience.

Evidence: Early in the story, Lena notices that the key feels “heavier when someone else could see it.”

Explanation: The weight is not physical; it reflects her awareness of judgment. She is concerned with being caught rather than with repairing the mistake itself.

Significance: By the final paragraph, she no longer checks whether anyone is watching, showing that her motive has shifted from avoiding embarrassment to accepting responsibility.

We Do

Take your strongest Day 6 claim. Identify the best detail, not merely the first relevant one. Complete this reasoning chain: This detail shows ___ because ___. This matters because ___.

You Do

Write one 180–250 word analytical paragraph using at least two details from Text A. Highlight or label C, E, E, and S after drafting.

Language Lab · Subjects, Predicates & Clauses

In the sentence Although Lena could have hidden the key, she returns it because her understanding of responsibility changes, identify the subject and predicate of the independent clause and both dependent clauses. Then combine two short sentences from your paragraph using although, because, or while without creating a run-on.

Day 8Quotation integrationRL.9-10.1L.9-10.2

Integrate Quotations

Today’s Question: How can another writer’s exact words become part of my own reasoning?

Learning Target

I can select only the words I need from a text, introduce them grammatically, punctuate them correctly, and explain their significance.

I Do · The Quotation Sandwich Is Not Enough

A quotation needs context + integration + analysis. Avoid dropping a quotation as a sentence by itself.

Dropped: Lena is worried about judgment. “The key felt heavier when someone else could see it.”

Integrated: Lena’s concern with judgment appears when the narrator says the key feels “heavier when someone else could see it,” linking the object’s imagined weight to the pressure of being observed.

The integrated version makes the quotation grammatically part of the writer’s own sentence and immediately interprets its language.

Three Useful Methods
  1. Signal phrase: The narrator reveals Lena’s anxiety when she observes that “…”
  2. Embedded phrase: The key’s “…” suggests …
  3. Colon after a complete sentence: Lena’s motive is still external: “…”

Never use a colon unless the words before it form a complete sentence.

We Do

Shorten this evidence to the smallest useful quotation: For the first time all week, she did not check whether anyone had seen her. Decide which exact words carry the analytical weight and embed them in your own sentence.

You Do

Revise yesterday’s paragraph so that it contains two smoothly integrated quotations. After each quotation, explain a specific word, phrase, contrast, or implication instead of repeating the quotation in different words.

Language Lab · Phrases & Clauses

Identify the introductory phrase, independent clause, and quotation in one of your revised sentences. Check that the sentence would still be grammatically complete if the quotation marks disappeared.

Day 9Revision for precisionW.9-10.5L.9-10.1–2

Revise for Precision

Today’s Question: What changes make analysis more exact rather than merely longer?

Learning Target

I can identify vague, repetitive, summary-heavy, or structurally weak sentences and revise them so each sentence advances the interpretation.

I Do · Four Revision Targets
  1. Vague nouns: thing, stuff, something, aspect.
  2. Weak verbs: shows, says, is, has — when a more exact verb is available.
  3. Summary drift: retelling what happened without explaining why it matters.
  4. Reasoning repetition: saying the evidence again instead of interpreting it.
Model

Before: This shows that Lena is changing because she does a different thing with the key and this is important.

After: Lena’s decision to leave the key openly on the windowsill marks a shift from concealment to accountability; unlike her earlier attempts to avoid witnesses, this action no longer depends on anyone noticing it.

The revision is not better because it is longer. It is better because the nouns, verbs, comparison, and causal relationship are more precise.

Precision Audit

Underline every form of shows, says, is, important, thing, and because in your paragraph. Do not automatically delete them. For each one ask: Is this the most exact word or relationship?

You Do

Make at least three meaningful revisions: one to the claim or reasoning, one to evidence integration, and one to sentence precision. Then write a one-sentence revision note explaining which change most improved the analysis and why.

Language Lab · Sentence Architecture

Choose one sentence from your paragraph. Mark subject | predicate | phrases | independent/dependent clauses. Revise its structure to emphasize the most important idea. Compare the original and revised versions aloud.

Day 10First literary analysisRL.9-10.1–2W.9-10.4–5,9

First Literary Analysis

Today’s Question: Can I independently build and defend an interpretation of an unfamiliar literary text?

Learning Target

I can independently develop a defensible claim, select precise evidence, integrate quotations, explain reasoning, and revise a focused literary analysis.

Independent Sequence
  1. Read Text B once for situation and change.
  2. Reread and annotate repetition, contrast, shifts, significant language, and questions.
  3. Write one interpretive question.
  4. Draft two possible claims; choose the more defensible one.
  5. Select 2–3 pieces of evidence before drafting.
  6. Write 350–500 words of literary analysis.
  7. Complete a precision revision and self-score.
Prompt

What does Text B suggest about the relationship between certainty and understanding? Develop a focused interpretation of Mara’s change and support it with precise textual evidence.

Required Features
  • A specific, arguable claim.
  • At least two smoothly integrated quotations.
  • Reasoning that explains how the evidence supports the claim.
  • Attention to at least one literary feature such as contrast, repetition, imagery, or symbolic detail.
  • A concluding insight that explains the significance of the interpretation.
Mastery Rule

Score 8/10 or higher. If your score is below 8, identify the lowest rubric row, complete targeted corrective learning, revise that dimension of the same analysis, and resubmit. The standard does not change.

Text B · The Map in the Rain

Mara trusted maps because they admitted what they knew. Roads were drawn in firm lines. Rivers were named. Distances sat quietly in the corner, waiting to be measured. Even blank spaces seemed honest; they were simply places the map had not described.

On the morning of the hike, she folded the park map into a neat rectangle and placed it in the clear pocket of her jacket. Her brother Elias carried nothing but a bottle of water and a habit of stopping whenever he heard birds. “We’re going east,” Mara reminded him twice before the first mile marker. Elias nodded both times and then wandered ten steps off the trail to examine a feather.

By noon, rain had blurred the distant ridge. At a fork in the trail, Mara unfolded the map beneath a cedar tree. The printed path showed a bridge crossing the creek. The creek in front of them had no bridge. Mara checked the legend, the scale, and the trail marker. She checked them again. Elias walked downstream and called back that a fallen tree formed a narrow crossing.

“That’s not the route,” Mara said.

“It is a route,” he answered.

For several minutes she stood under the cedar holding the map open while rain collected in its folds. The blue line representing the creek began to bleed into the green around it. Mara felt an unreasonable anger at the paper. The map had not lied to her, exactly. It had simply remained correct about a world that was no longer there.

She folded it again, less carefully this time, and followed Elias downstream. At the fallen tree she stopped before crossing. On the far bank, the trail curved back toward the ridge just as he had guessed. Mara took out a pencil and drew a small broken line beside the printed bridge. Then, after a moment, she added the date.
