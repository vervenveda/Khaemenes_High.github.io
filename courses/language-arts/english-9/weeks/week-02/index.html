Close Reading, Claims & Evidence

How does a careful reader move from what a text says to what the evidence supports?

Reading: Short literary and informational passages with layered details.
Writing: Build claim–evidence–reasoning paragraphs with precise quotations and explicit warrants.

Weekly Targets

By Friday, show the reasoning—not just the answer.

  • Distinguish explicit detail from a reasonable inference and identify the evidence that makes the inference defensible.
  • Write a focused claim and connect evidence to it with a clear warrant rather than leaving a quotation unexplained.
  • Compare evidence for relevance, precision, and strength.
  • Recognize subjects, predicates, phrases, independent clauses, and dependent clauses; use that knowledge to improve sentence clarity.
  • Use accurately: claim, inference, context, warrant, connotation, precision.
  • Preserve two polished evidence paragraphs, an annotation sample, language-lab evidence, and a short reflection.

Five Distinct Daily Lessons

Observe → infer → test → explain → defend.

Each day produces evidence for the next. Do not mark a day finished merely because a page was opened.

Day 1 · Observation Before Inference

What is actually on the page?

Read one short literary passage and one short informational passage. First mark only observable details: diction, action, description, stated facts, repeated ideas, contrasts, and structural clues. On a second pass, write three inferences beside the details that support them.

  • Label each note D for detail or I for inference.
  • For every inference, point to at least one exact textual clue.
  • Reject one inference that goes beyond the available evidence and explain why.
  • Artifact: one annotated passage showing the detail→inference trail.
Day 2 · Evidence Laboratory

Which evidence earns its place?

Turn yesterday’s strongest inference into a claim. Enter the claim and at least three candidate pieces of evidence in Evidence & Citation Studio. Classify how each piece relates to the claim, then rank the evidence for relevance, precision, and explanatory power.

  • Keep the claim narrow enough to prove.
  • Record exact location/context for quotations.
  • Identify one piece of evidence that is interesting but insufficient.
  • Write a one-sentence warrant for the strongest evidence.
  • Artifact: exported or recorded claim/evidence relationship.
Day 3 · Language Laboratory

Build sentences that reveal reasoning.

Study subjects and predicates, then distinguish phrases from clauses and independent from dependent clauses. Use six sentences from this week’s notes: diagnose their structure, repair fragments or run-ons, and combine ideas where subordination or coordination makes the reasoning clearer.

  • Underline subjects once and predicates twice.
  • Bracket phrases; mark independent and dependent clauses.
  • Revise at least two sentences to make the evidence-to-warrant relationship clearer.
  • Use context, connotation, precision correctly in original sentences.
  • Artifact: before/after language-lab set with a reason for each revision.
Day 4 · Composition Studio

Make the quotation work.

Draft two claim–evidence–reasoning paragraphs in PROSE. Paragraph one should analyze the literary passage; paragraph two should analyze the informational passage. Integrate quotations grammatically, establish context, and follow every quotation with reasoning that explains how and why it supports the claim.

  • Claim: arguable and specific.
  • Evidence: precise, relevant, and introduced.
  • Warrant: explains the logical connection.
  • Revision: strengthen one weak verb, one vague reference, and one sentence transition.
  • Artifact: two paragraphs plus visible revision evidence.
Day 5 · Defense & Transfer

Can your reasoning survive a challenge?

Read both paragraphs aloud or review them slowly. For each, ask: “What might a careful reader question?” Add or revise reasoning where the evidence does not fully carry the claim. Then explain the detail→inference→claim→evidence→warrant chain without looking at the definitions.

  • Self-score claim, evidence, warrant, sentence clarity, and precision.
  • Ask Eiren one focused question only after identifying your own uncertainty.
  • Revise from the response using your judgment; do not substitute mentor text for your work.
  • Artifact: final paragraphs, annotation sample, and transfer reflection.
