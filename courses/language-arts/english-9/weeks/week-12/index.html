Unit 04 · Poetry, Voice & Form · Week 12

Comparison, Translation & Performance

What is gained, changed, or contested when poetry is translated and performed?

A translation is not a transparent window, and a performance is not a neutral delivery. Both involve choices. This week you will compare those choices responsibly, prepare a formal oral interpretation, complete the fourth poem in your Unit 4 portfolio, and defend your reading with evidence.

Weekly Targets

By the end of this week, I can…

  • distinguish source text, translation, paraphrase, adaptation, and performance interpretation;
  • compare two translations using specific criteria rather than declaring one simply “better”;
  • identify what a translation preserves, changes, clarifies, adds, or leaves ambiguous;
  • use punctuation, lineation, caesura, enjambment, cadence, pace, emphasis, and silence to plan an oral reading;
  • create and revise a fourth portfolio poem designed with the ear as well as the page in mind;
  • complete the Unit 4 poetry portfolio, comparative analysis, formal oral reading, and reflection at 80% mastery.

Source Discipline

When using a translated poem, identify the poet, source language when known, translator, edition or source, and access location. Do not treat one English translation as the original wording.

Day 56TranslationComparison

Translation Is Choice

When two versions differ, what evidence lets us compare them fairly?

Learning Target

I can distinguish supplied meaning from a translator’s interpretive choice and explain the effect of that choice.

Launch

Write one sentence describing rain without using the word rain. Compare two classmates’ versions. Which details came from the event, and which came from the writer?

I Do · Five Translation Questions
  1. What information or image appears to be preserved?
  2. What has been compressed, expanded, specified, or omitted?
  3. What happens to syntax, sound, line length, or ambiguity?
  4. Does the translator explain something the source leaves uncertain?
  5. What can we claim from the English versions—and what remains unknowable without the source language?
We Do · Course Translation Simulation

This is a course-created simulation, not a historical poem and not a real translation. The “source packet” supplies meaning only so we can isolate translation-like decisions.

Meaning Packet

Night market after rain. Paper lanterns reflected in street water. A distant bicycle bell. The observer cannot tell whether someone is arriving or leaving.

Version A

After rain, the market darkens. Lanterns tremble in the gutter. Far off, a bicycle bell— someone coming, or going.

Version B

Rain leaves the market shining. Red lamps swim beneath my feet. A bell turns once in the distance. I cannot tell which way you travel.

Questions

Which version adds specificity? Which changes perspective? Which preserves uncertainty differently? What does “red,” “beneath my feet,” or “you” add that the packet never supplied?

You Do

Write 180–250 words comparing Versions A and B. Make one claim about a tradeoff: precision vs. ambiguity, image vs. literalness, sound vs. directness, or distance vs. intimacy.

Prove It

Underline one sentence in your response that clearly separates evidence from inference.

Translation Vocabulary

  • source text: the text being translated
  • translation: a rendering into another language
  • paraphrase: restatement within the same language
  • adaptation: deliberate reshaping for a new context or purpose
  • ambiguity: language that permits more than one plausible meaning
Do not flatten uncertainty. If you cannot read the source language, you can compare English versions responsibly—but you cannot claim with certainty which one is “closest” to the original without stronger evidence.
Day 57Authentic SourcesCultural Context

Compare Without Erasing the Source

How can two translations illuminate a poem without pretending they are interchangeable?

Learning Target

I can compare two authentic translations using named criteria and accurate attribution.

I Do · Attribution Before Interpretation

Record: poet, source language when known, translator A, translator B, edition/source, date if available, and access location. Translation wording belongs to the translator as well as to the source tradition.

Think About It

“More literal,” “more beautiful,” and “more accurate” are not self-explanatory judgments. What criterion would make each defensible?

Day 58PunctuationLineationCadence

The Page Gives the Voice Instructions

When should a reader follow punctuation, line endings, syntax, or silence?

Learning Target

I can create a performance map that distinguishes punctuation, line break, enjambment, caesura, stanza break, pace, and emphasis.

I Do · Performance Is Not “Read Dramatically”

Use evidence. A comma may slow the phrase; a period closes a sentence; a caesura creates an internal interruption; enjambment can pull syntax across the line; a stanza break may create a larger conceptual shift. A line ending alone does not always require a pause.

We Do · Original Practice Poem

Before the Door Opens

The hallway keeps its borrowed light beneath the doors. I hear a chair move once—then nothing. Down the stair, a faucet counts what time forgets. I lift my hand. The knuckles wait. A bus exhales beyond the glass. The moment does not ask permission; it simply gathers at the latch.

Mark / for light pause, // for strong pause, underline emphasis, draw an arrow through enjambed syntax, and circle one place where silence matters.

You Do

Read the poem twice with different plausible performance maps. Write 160–220 words explaining how one change in pause, pace, or emphasis changes the listener’s interpretation.

Language Lab

Revise four lines of your own poetry using punctuation as a guide to voice and pace. Then test the revision aloud. Keep the version that better serves meaning—not the one with more punctuation.

Day 59Poem 4Rehearsal

Write for the Ear, Then Rehearse the Meaning

How can a poet design a page that supports a deliberate oral interpretation?

Learning Target

I can create the fourth Unit 4 poem and prepare an evidence-based performance plan.

Poem 4 · Voice & Cadence

Write an original 16–28 line poem designed to work both silently and aloud. It must contain a meaningful turn or shift. Your lineation, punctuation, sound pattern, repetition, pace, or silence should serve that turn.

No device quota. You do not need alliteration, rhyme, caesura, metaphor, refrain, and enjambment in one poem. Choose only the craft that earns its place.
Performance Map
Breath

Where is a pause required by syntax? Where is one optional?

Emphasis

Which words carry the conceptual or emotional load?

Pace

Where should time expand, tighten, or hesitate?

Turn

How will the listener hear the poem change direction?

Studio Choice

Draft by hand, type directly, or use one of the optional studio tools. Writer’s Grove may generate constraints only; it may not generate the poem. PROSE may organize and preserve versions after intellectual work exists.

Rehearse

Select one poem from the four-poem portfolio for formal reading. Rehearse at least three times, changing only one variable each time: pace, emphasis, or pause. Keep notes about what improved and what became distorted.

Accessible Performance

The intellectual standard is interpretation through voice, timing, emphasis, and phrasing. A student who uses AAC, text-to-speech, recorded delivery, or another approved accessible communication mode may demonstrate the same interpretive decisions. The student still authors the performance map.

Do not confuse volume with expression. A strong reading can be quiet, restrained, slow, conversational, or highly rhythmic if those choices are justified by the poem.

Part B · Comparative Translation Analysis

Write 500–700 words comparing two English translations of one poem. Use an authentic pair from the Poetry Hall when accessible. Identify translators and source information. Develop a defensible claim about at least two dimensions: image/diction, syntax/lineation, sound/rhythm, ambiguity, cultural reference, tone, or implied speaker.

Fallback: if an authentic lawful pair is not accessible, use the Day 56 course simulation and explicitly label it a simulation. Analyze versioning choices; do not claim historical or linguistic accuracy.

Part C · Formal Oral Reading

Perform one portfolio poem in approximately 2–3 minutes including a brief introduction. Submit the annotated performance map showing breath, emphasis, pace, and the poem’s turn. Your introduction should name the poem and one interpretive choice—not explain the whole poem in advance.

Part D · Portfolio Reflection

Write 200–300 words: Which revision across the four poems most changed meaning? Which performance choice most changed meaning? What do you now understand about the relationship among page, sound, reader, and interpretation?
