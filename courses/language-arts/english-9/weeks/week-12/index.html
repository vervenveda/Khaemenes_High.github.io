Weekly Targets

By the end of this week, the student can…

  • Compare two translations through specific choices in diction, image, syntax, lineation, sound, ambiguity, and tone.
  • Distinguish observable translation differences from unsupported claims about translator intent.
  • Use original-language evidence responsibly when the student has sufficient language support, while never treating machine output as authoritative.
  • Explain how performance choices—pace, pause, stress, volume, and tone—constitute an interpretation.
  • Use punctuation deliberately as one guide to voice and pace while recognizing that lineation and syntax also matter.
  • Use accurately: translation, speaker, persona, cadence, ambiguity, interpretation.
  • Complete the four-poem portfolio, comparative analysis, formal oral reading, and defined Unit 4 scored evidence at 80% or higher.

Five-Day Translation & Performance Laboratory

Compare → investigate → rehearse → curate → defend.

Day 1 · Translation Microscope

Difference Before Judgment

Read two approved translations of the same poem. Begin by recording observable differences before deciding which choices are more effective for a particular interpretation.

  • Align several corresponding lines or passages.
  • Mark differences in diction, syntax, image, punctuation, lineation, and ambiguity.
  • Identify one similarity that survives both translations.
  • Write a provisional claim about how one translation choice changes the reading experience.
Day 2 · Multilingual Inquiry

Translation Is a Series of Choices

Investigate one consequential word, phrase, image, or structural feature. Students with relevant language knowledge may contribute it; other students use teacher-approved notes or references without pretending expertise they do not have.

  • Use ARSHIF or approved reference material for contextual questions that materially affect interpretation.
  • When an approved translator tool is available, use it to generate hypotheses—not final authority—and verify important choices against trusted references.
  • Distinguish literal possibility, idiom, register, sound, and poetic effect.
  • Revise the Day 1 claim if new evidence complicates it.
Day 3 · Cadence & Performance Lab

A Reading Is an Interpretation

Prepare multiple oral versions of one poem or passage. Change pace, pause, stress, volume, and tone while remaining accountable to the language on the page.

  • Annotate punctuation, syntax, line breaks, and possible breath points separately.
  • Perform two contrasting readings and describe what each foregrounds.
  • Complete a punctuation/cadence correction and revision set.
  • Use all six weekly vocabulary terms accurately.
  • Complete the scored language/performance-knowledge check at 80% or higher; review and retry below 80%.
Day 4 · Portfolio Curation Studio

Revision Across Four Poems

Return to the Unit 4 poems and curate the four-poem portfolio. Use PROSE to make final substantive revisions before proofreading.

  • Preserve an earlier version of each poem.
  • Identify the strongest and weakest poem and explain why.
  • Make at least one substantive revision based on image, diction, form, sound, or structure—not surface correction alone.
  • Write the comparative translation analysis using traceable evidence.
  • Prepare a performance score for the selected oral-reading poem.
Day 5 · Poetry Colloquium & Unit Defense

Interpret, Perform, Defend

Present the formal oral reading and defend selected portfolio decisions. Then complete the defined Unit 4 assessment evidence.

  • Perform the selected poem with deliberate interpretive choices.
  • Explain one revision from an original poem using before/after evidence.
  • Defend one translation-comparison claim without declaring a universal “best” translation.
  • Respond to one peer, teacher, or Eiren challenge by defending or qualifying the reasoning.
  • Below 80%: diagnose the weak criterion, complete corrective learning, and reassess before Unit 5.

Sovereign Translation Game

Three Doors, One Line

This comparison game requires no external service. It demonstrates why translation cannot be reduced to word substitution.

Door 1 · Literal

Given a teacher-prepared source note and three plausible English renderings, choose the version that most closely preserves literal information. Name what it sacrifices.

Door 2 · Music

Choose the version that best preserves a sound, rhythm, repetition, or compactness feature. Name what changes in literal meaning or register.

Door 3 · Context

Choose the rendering that best fits the poem's surrounding imagery, speaker, or tone. Defend the choice with context. More than one answer may remain defensible.

Eiren · Language Arts Mentor

Eiren helps distinguish evidence from preference.

Bring aligned translation passages, a performance score, portfolio revision, or comparison claim first. Eiren can challenge unsupported assumptions and ask what specific language makes one interpretation defensible.
