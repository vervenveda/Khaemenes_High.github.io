Day 58PunctuationLineationCadence

The Page Gives the Voice Instructions

When should a reader follow punctuation, line endings, syntax, or silence?

Learning Target

I can create a performance map that distinguishes punctuation, line break, enjambment, caesura, stanza break, pace, and emphasis.

I Do · Performance Is Not “Read Dramatically”

Use evidence. A comma may slow the phrase; a period closes a sentence; a caesura creates an internal interruption; enjambment can pull syntax across the line; a stanza break may create a larger conceptual shift. A line ending alone does not always require a pause.

We Do · Original Practice Poem

Before the Door Opens

The hallway keeps its borrowed light beneath the doors. I hear a chair move once—then nothing. Down the stair, a faucet counts what time forgets. I lift my hand. The knuckles wait. A bus exhales beyond the glass. The moment does not ask permission; it simply gathers at the latch.

Mark / for light pause, // for strong pause, underline emphasis, draw an arrow through enjambed syntax, and circle one place where silence matters.

You Do

Read the poem twice with different plausible performance maps. Write 160–220 words explaining how one change in pause, pace, or emphasis changes the listener’s interpretation.

Language Lab

Revise four lines of your own poetry using punctuation as a guide to voice and pace. Then test the revision aloud. Keep the version that better serves meaning—not the one with more punctuation.
