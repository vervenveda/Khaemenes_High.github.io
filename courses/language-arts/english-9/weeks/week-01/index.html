Enter the Scholarly Conversation

Today’s Question: What makes reading scholarly rather than merely finished?

Learning Target

I can distinguish observation from interpretation and record an honest baseline of my current reading and writing habits.

Launch · 5 minutes

Read the statements below. Label each one Observation, Question, or Inference.

  1. The passage repeats the word again three times.
  2. Why does the writer return to the same image?
  3. The repetition suggests the character is beginning to reconsider an old belief.

Rule: an observation can be pointed to directly in the text. An inference must be reasoned from one or more observations.

I Do · Explicit Instruction

Scholarly reading is a cycle, not a hunt for the “right answer.” Use five moves:

  1. Observe: What is actually present?
  2. Question: What is uncertain, surprising, repeated, or important?
  3. Infer: What might the details mean together?
  4. Test: Does the rest of the text support, complicate, or contradict that idea?
  5. Explain: Can I state the idea and show another reader how the evidence supports it?

A claim is a defensible answer to a meaningful question. Evidence is the specific textual material used to test and support that answer.

Model

Suppose a passage says, “Mara closed the book, opened it again, and read the same sentence for a third time.”

Observation: Mara rereads one sentence three times.
Weak leap: Mara is confused.
Stronger inference: Mara treats the sentence as important or unresolved because she repeatedly returns to it instead of moving on.

The stronger inference stays close to the observable action and explains the reasoning.

We Do · Guided Practice

Read only the first paragraph of Text A below. Record 3 observations, 2 questions, and 1 possible pattern. Then choose one question that could lead to an interpretation rather than a one-word answer.

You Do · Baseline

Read the rest of Text A independently. Without opening PROSE or receiving sentence-level help, write 150–200 words answering: What does this passage suggest about the difference between reading and understanding?

This is a baseline, not a perfection test. Preserve it exactly as first written.

Text A · The Margin Between Reading and Knowing

On the first afternoon, Lena read the page as she had been trained to read bus schedules: from the upper left corner to the lower right, stopping only when the page ended. She understood every word. At least, she could have defined them. When her teacher asked what had changed between the first paragraph and the last, however, Lena looked down at the clean white margins and realized that she had no answer.

The next day she returned to the same page with a pencil. At first she underlined almost everything. The important sentence looked exactly like the unimportant one because both were trapped beneath identical gray lines. She erased until the paper grew soft. Then she tried something slower. Beside one sentence she wrote, “Why here?” Beside another, “This sounds like the opening, but colder.” A third note simply said, “I thought I knew what he wanted.”

Nothing in the printed passage had changed. Yet the page no longer seemed still. A repeated image that had looked accidental now connected two scenes. A word she had ignored began to sound deliberate. One question forced her back three paragraphs, where she found a detail that weakened her first idea. She crossed out the idea, not because it had been foolish, but because the text had given her a reason to change it.

By Friday the margins were crowded. Lena could not point to a single note and say, “This is understanding.” The understanding lived in the movement among the notes: noticing, asking, testing, revising. She had read the page four times. Only the first reading had been silent.

Day 2Close readingContextPattern

Read Closely: From Detail to Pattern

Today’s Question: When does a detail become evidence of a pattern?

Learning Target

I can move from noticing individual details to explaining a meaningful pattern in a text.

Launch

Return to Lena’s repeated actions: read, return, erase, ask, cross out, reread. Which actions simply describe what she does? Which actions suggest a change in how she thinks?

I Do · Three-Pass Close Reading
  1. Literal pass: What happens or is stated?
  2. Language pass: Which words, images, contrasts, or sentence choices stand out?
  3. Pattern pass: What repeats, changes, contrasts, accumulates, or causes something else?

Useful pattern categories include repetition, contrast, shift, cause/effect, and unusual word choice. A pattern is not just “something repeated.” It is a relationship among details that helps explain meaning.

Model

In Text A, the margins move from clean to crowded. That contrast is observable. The change can support the inference that Lena’s reading becomes more active because her thinking leaves visible traces on the page.

We Do

Find one repetition, one contrast, and one shift in Text A. For each, complete: The text shows ___ and ___. Together, these details suggest ___ because ___.

You Do

Create a three-row pattern log. Each row must include: detail 1 + detail 2 → named pattern → inference. Then write a 120–180 word paragraph explaining which pattern contributes most to the passage’s meaning.

Day 3Purposeful annotationAcademic questionsSentence control

Annotate With Purpose

Today’s Question: What makes an annotation useful rather than decorative?

Learning Target

I can annotate a text so my notes reveal a line of thinking and help me build an interpretation.

Launch

Which annotation is more useful beside “She crossed out the idea”?

A. Important!!!
B. REVISION: she changes the idea because new evidence weakens it.

Explain what B allows a reader to do that A does not.

I Do · Annotation Code

Use a small code consistently:

  • N — Notice: an exact detail, repetition, contrast, or shift.
  • ? — Question: a real uncertainty that could guide rereading.
  • I — Inference: a possible meaning connected to evidence.
  • L — Language: a word, phrase, image, or syntax choice worth examining.
  • P — Pattern: a connection among separated details.

No naked highlighting. Every mark should have a reason in the margin or notes.

Model

Beside “The understanding lived in the movement among the notes,” write: L: “movement” makes understanding sound like a process, not an object. P: connects to Lena repeatedly rereading and revising.

We Do

Annotate one paragraph together with at least three different code types. Compare two questions: one that asks for a fact already stated, and one that opens an interpretive problem. Revise the weaker question.

You Do

Re-annotate Text A with at least 8 purposeful annotations across 3 or more code types. Then write an annotation trail in five sentences: my first idea → a detail → a question → a revised idea → my current claim.

Language Mini-Lab · Sentence Boundaries

Margin notes may be fragments; analytical prose should usually be complete sentences. Turn three fragmentary notes such as “change in the margins” or “because she rereads” into complete analytical sentences with a clear subject and predicate.

Day 4EvidenceInferenceReasoning

Evidence, Inference & Explanation

Today’s Question: Why doesn’t a quotation prove a claim by itself?

Learning Target

I can select precise evidence and explain how it supports an inference or claim.

Launch

Claim: Lena becomes a more active reader. Which is stronger evidence?

A. “The margins were crowded.”
B. “She had read the page four times.”

There may be more than one defensible answer. The important work is explaining why the detail is relevant to this particular claim.

I Do · Evidence Quality Test

Strong evidence is:

  • Relevant: directly connected to the claim.
  • Specific: precise enough to analyze.
  • Sufficient: enough evidence to make the reasoning credible, not a single convenient fragment when the text is more complex.

Then build the reasoning bridge: Evidence → shows/suggests → because → significance.

Model

Claim: The passage presents understanding as revision rather than certainty.
Evidence: Lena crosses out an idea after finding a detail that weakens it.
Reasoning: Her willingness to replace an earlier interpretation shows that understanding develops by testing ideas against evidence, not by protecting the first answer she produces.
Significance: The passage therefore defines scholarly reading as an accountable process of changing one’s mind.

We Do

Rank three pieces of evidence from Text A as strong, usable, or weak for the claim “Lena’s reading becomes increasingly self-directed.” Defend your ranking. Then write one complete reasoning bridge for the strongest evidence.

You Do

Write a 180–250 word analytical paragraph answering: How does the writer use Lena’s changing behavior to define scholarly reading? Include a defensible claim, two pieces of specific evidence, and reasoning that explains each connection.

Language Mini-Lab · Paragraph Unity

Underline the controlling idea in your claim. For every other sentence, ask: How does this sentence advance that idea? Revise or remove one sentence that summarizes without contributing to the analysis.

Text B · The Unanswered Question

Niko liked questions with clean endings. In mathematics, an answer could be checked. In a map, a road either reached the river or it did not. But the note on the classroom board bothered him because it had been left there after everyone else had gone: What would change your mind?

He assumed it was unfinished business from another class. Still, while packing his bag, he began inventing answers. More information, he thought. Better evidence. Proof. Each answer sounded sensible until he noticed that all three meant nearly the same thing. He was imagining evidence that would change someone else’s mind, not his own.

On the walk home, Niko replayed an argument he had made that morning about a story the class was reading. He had insisted that the main character acted out of fear. When Amara pointed to a sentence that suggested pride, Niko had answered quickly: “That doesn’t count.” At the time, the reply felt like defense. Now it sounded like a locked door.

The next morning, the question was still on the board. Niko opened his notebook beneath it and copied Amara’s sentence. Below the quotation he wrote two headings: What supports my idea? and What makes my idea harder to defend? He did not cross out his original interpretation. He placed it between the two headings and waited to see what the text would make him do.
