Open the guided thinking sequence

1 · Retrieve

Before rereading, explain the difference between an observation and an inference in your own words.

2 · Study a worked reasoning model

Textual detail: Lena could define each word, but could not describe the change between the opening and ending. Observation: she knows the words but cannot explain the passage’s development. Possible inference: decoding words is not the same as interpreting relationships across a text.

3 · Challenge the easy answer

Tempting leap: Lena was not paying attention. The passage does not establish that motive. What other explanation fits the evidence?

4 · Apply and revise

In your existing observation field, distinguish one directly stated detail from one carefully qualified inference; then identify what new textual evidence could change your idea.

Self-check: My evidence is accurate; I explain rather than merely quote; I name an uncertainty; and I can show what changed in my reasoning.

Text A · The Margin Between Reading and Knowing

On the first afternoon, Lena read the page as she had been trained to read bus schedules: from the upper left corner to the lower right, stopping only when the page ended. She understood every word. At least, she could have defined them. When her teacher asked what had changed between the first paragraph and the last, however, Lena looked down at the clean white margins and realized that she had no answer.

The next day she returned to the same page with a pencil. At first she underlined almost everything. The important sentence looked exactly like the unimportant one because both were trapped beneath identical gray lines. She erased until the paper grew soft. Then she tried something slower. Beside one sentence she wrote, “Why here?” Beside another, “This sounds like the opening, but colder.” A third note simply said, “I thought I knew what he wanted.”

Nothing in the printed passage had changed. Yet the page no longer seemed still. A repeated image that had looked accidental now connected two scenes. A word she had ignored began to sound deliberate. One question forced her back three paragraphs, where she found a detail that weakened her first idea. She crossed out the idea, not because it had been foolish, but because the text had given her a reason to change it.

By Friday the margins were crowded. Lena could not point to a single note and say, “This is understanding.” The understanding lived in the movement among the notes: noticing, asking, testing, revising. She had read the page four times. Only the first reading had been silent.

An observation points to something present in the text. An inference explains what details might mean. A claim answers a meaningful question and can be supported with evidence.

Practice · Notice, question, infer

Read the first paragraph again. Record 3 observations, 2 questions and 1 possible pattern. Write what you actually notice before looking for a perfect answer.

See a worked example

Practice sentence: “Mara closed the book, opened it again, and read the same sentence for a third time.”

Observation: Mara rereads one sentence three times.

Weak leap: Mara is confused. The sentence does not establish this.

Supported inference: Mara treats the sentence as important or unresolved because she keeps returning to it.

Use “I notice…”, “I wonder…” and “This might suggest… because…”.
