Fate, Agency & Consequence

Today’s Question: What makes a hero responsible when not everything is under the hero’s control?

Learning Target

I can compare Gilgamesh and Odysseus by separating circumstance from choice and explaining how each tradition connects heroic action to consequence.

I Do · Four-Part Agency Lens
  1. Given condition: What situation, identity, prophecy, divine action, mortality, social role, or danger is not freely chosen?
  2. Available choice: What meaningful options remain?
  3. Decision: What does the hero choose?
  4. Consequence: Who bears the result, and what value does the story seem to test?

This prevents two weak readings: “everything is fate, so choices do not matter” and “everything is choice, so circumstance does not matter.”

You Do

Complete your own evidence matrix using at least two textual details for each hero from readings you have actually consulted. Then draft one comparative claim answering: Which hero’s greatest strength is more directly transformed into a lesson about responsibility? A strong answer may choose either hero if the reasoning is precise.

Language Lab · Verb Control

Audit the claim and evidence notes for literary present tense. Then circle passive constructions. Keep passive voice when the receiver of an action genuinely matters more; revise it when the actor is being hidden accidentally.
