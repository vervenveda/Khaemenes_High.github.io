Unit 02 · Week 05

Epic Heroes & Cultural Values

How does an epic hero embody—and sometimes challenge—communal ideals?

Reading: Episodes from Gilgamesh and The Odyssey with appropriate contextual notes.
Writing: Compare heroic traits, tests, consequences, relationships, and cultural expectations using traceable evidence.

Weekly Targets

By the end of this week, the student can…

  • Identify how an epic establishes a hero through action, reputation, relationships, tests, and consequences.
  • Distinguish a character trait from a cultural value and support each inference with textual evidence.
  • Compare heroic ideals across two texts without reducing either tradition to a stereotype.
  • Analyze how a hero can both embody and violate communal expectations.
  • Maintain verb-tense consistency and choose active or passive voice deliberately.
  • Use accurately: epic, invocation, epithet, hubris, quest, hospitality.
  • Produce a comparative evidence matrix and timed analysis with defined scored work at 80% or higher.

Five-Day Learning Cycle

Heroism becomes an argument when evidence enters the conversation.

Day 1 · Hero Under Observation

Actions Before Labels

Read an assigned epic episode and collect what the hero actually says, does, refuses, risks, loses, or learns before assigning traits such as courageous, proud, loyal, reckless, or wise.

  • Create an action → consequence evidence table.
  • Separate narrator description from the student's inference.
  • Identify one epithet or repeated description and ask what expectation it creates.
  • Write two competing trait claims and determine which has stronger evidence.
Day 2 · Values Laboratory

Hero and Community

Examine what the surrounding community rewards, condemns, fears, expects, or remembers. Then test whether the hero consistently represents those values.

  • Track one value such as hospitality, loyalty, courage, reputation, duty, restraint, or leadership.
  • Find evidence of the hero embodying that value.
  • Find evidence of tension, failure, excess, or contradiction.
  • Use ARSHIF or approved context only to clarify—not substitute for—the primary text.
Day 3 · Comparative & Language Lab

Compare Without Collapsing

Build a comparative evidence matrix for two epic heroes. Compare a shared analytical category while preserving differences in plot, context, consequence, and cultural expectation.

  • Record at least two evidence points per hero.
  • Use Evidence & Citation Studio when source tracking becomes complex.
  • Audit verb tense: literary analysis normally discusses textual action consistently in the literary present.
  • Rewrite five sentences to test active versus passive voice and explain which better serves the meaning.
  • Complete the scored language/vocabulary check at 80% or higher; review and retry below 80%.
Day 4 · Timed Analysis Studio

Plan Fast, Think Deeply

Practice a timed analytical response to a fresh prompt about heroic ideals. The goal is not speed alone; it is disciplined evidence selection under a reasonable time constraint.

  • Spend the opening minutes unpacking the prompt and planning a defensible claim.
  • Select evidence before drafting.
  • Write a focused comparison with warrants rather than plot summary.
  • After time expires, open PROSE and annotate where the response would benefit from revision.
  • Preserve both the timed version and post-task reflection.
Day 5 · Defense, Feedback & Transfer

Does the Claim Survive Challenge?

Present the comparative matrix and central claim. A peer, teacher, or Eiren conference challenges one assumption, evidence choice, or overgeneralization. Defend the interpretation when warranted and revise it when the challenge exposes weakness.

  • Name the strongest evidence for each hero.
  • Explain one meaningful similarity and one difference.
  • Identify where cultural context matters to the comparison.
  • Revise one claim or warrant after feedback.
  • Preserve the matrix, timed analysis, score, and reflection as Unit 2 evidence.
