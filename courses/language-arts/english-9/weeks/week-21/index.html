Synthesis, Critical Review & Defense

What can I responsibly conclude after my first judgment has been tested?

Research: Complete the 900–1,200-word research report through synthesis, citation, source boundaries, revision, and transparent provenance.
Reading Hall: Complete the three-week critical review of Guy de Maupassant's The Necklace and defend the reasoning behind the final judgment.

PreviousDaily LessonsCanonical Unit 7 Mastery

Weekly Targets

Synthesize without blurring the boundaries between text, source, inference, and judgment.

  • Synthesize research sources around relationships, tensions, gaps, and questions rather than source-by-source summaries.
  • Preserve accurate paraphrase, quotation, attribution, citation, and provenance.
  • Represent disagreement and uncertainty honestly rather than manufacturing consensus.
  • Write a critical literary review with an evaluative thesis, textual evidence, explicit warrants, and a credible counterreading.
  • Use verified historical/contextual evidence only for claims it can actually support.
  • Distinguish personal response from literary evaluation.
  • Calibrate certainty and identify what remains unresolved.
  • Explain how counterevidence, research, feedback, or rereading changed—or legitimately did not change—the final judgment.

Five-Day Synthesis & Review Laboratory

Map → draft → counterread → revise → defend.

Day 1 · Two Evidence Maps

Do Not Collapse Research and Literature into One Evidence Pile

Build a research synthesis matrix and a separate literary evidence map.

  • Research map: sources, provenance, agreements, tensions, limitations, gaps, and lineage.
  • Literary map: textual observations, interpretations, warrants, counterevidence, competing readings, and unresolved ambiguity.
  • Draw explicit bridges only where contextual evidence is genuinely relevant.
  • Identify one historical fact that informs the review and one fact that would be improper to use as proof of character motive.
Day 2 · Critical Review Architecture

A Review Is an Argument About the Work

Design the Necklace review around a defensible evaluative thesis rather than plot summary.

  • Opening: identify the work and frame the central evaluative question.
  • Judgment: state a thesis proportional to the evidence.
  • Evidence: select consequential textual details and explain their relationship to the judgment.
  • Context: use verified research selectively and with clear source boundaries.
  • Counterreading: present a credible competing interpretation in a form its defender could recognize.
  • Qualification: identify what the evidence does not permit the reviewer to claim.
Day 3 · Synthesis, Citation & Language

Trace Every Borrowed Idea; Own Every Judgment

Complete the research report's synthesis/citation work and audit the review's authorship boundaries.

  • Paraphrase from understanding, then verify meaning and scope against the source.
  • Quote only where exact language matters.
  • Distinguish “the source reports” from “the evidence establishes.”
  • Keep literary interpretation in the student's voice; do not outsource judgment to a critic, search result, or AI.
  • Complete the scored synthesis/citation/language check at 80% or higher.
Day 4 · Adversarial Revision

Revision Must Be Able to Change the Thinking

Put both the research report and critical review under challenge.

  • Identify the strongest sentence in each piece and ask what evidence it depends upon.
  • Identify the most vulnerable sentence and test its warrant.
  • Search for overstatement, hidden assumptions, false consensus, vague attribution, unsupported causation, and modern-context projection.
  • Compare the final review with the Week 19 first judgment.
  • Document one substantive revision caused by evidence—or explain why the original claim survived a serious challenge.
Day 5 · Reading Hall & Research Colloquium

Defend the Process, Not Just the Conclusion

Submit the research evidence set and the three-week Reading Hall review. Complete a short oral or approved accessible defense.

  • What did you initially believe about the story?
  • What evidence most challenged that belief?
  • What is the strongest counterreading?
  • Which contextual claim did you verify, and what can it legitimately establish?
  • What remains uncertain?
  • What would make you revise your conclusion again?
  • Trace one research-report sentence backward to its source and one literary-review judgment backward to the text.

Reading Hall Critical Review

The first three-week review becomes portfolio evidence.

The review should demonstrate intellectual movement, not merely polished prose. Students preserve Week 19 observations, Week 20 counterreading and source forensics, and Week 21 final judgment so later units can compare how their reasoning develops.

Required Core

Evaluative thesis · textual evidence · warrants · verified context where relevant · counterreading · qualification/uncertainty · substantive revision.

Forbidden Shortcut

Plot summary presented as analysis, predetermined “moral,” context presented as textual proof, critic/AI opinion substituted for student judgment, or confident language unsupported by evidence.

Defense

Student explains how the conclusion was reached, challenged, and revised. Different conclusions are valid when they meet the same evidentiary and reasoning standards.

Eiren · Defense Examiner

Eiren challenges the seams in the final reasoning.

Bring the student's own review/report first. Eiren may ask for textual support, challenge a warrant, test a contextual claim, steelman the counterreading, question certainty, or ask what would change the conclusion. Eiren cannot write the assessed pieces, invent citations, supply the student's thesis, decide the story's moral, assign verified scores, or override mastery.
