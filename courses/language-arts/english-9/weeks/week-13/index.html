Reading Drama as Literature and Performance

How does a play communicate through words, bodies, space, and silence?

Reading: Opening scenes, stage directions, entrances/exits, monologues, asides, and performance choices.
Writing & Staging: Build a director's notebook that connects conflict, blocking, subtext, audience knowledge, and staging decisions to textual evidence.

Weekly Targets

By the end of this week, the student can…

  • Read dramatic dialogue and stage directions as evidence while recognizing that performance adds interpretive choices not fully specified by the page.
  • Trace conflict through objectives, obstacles, tactics, entrances, exits, silence, proximity, and shifts in power.
  • Distinguish spoken text from subtext and support a subtext interpretation with language and situation.
  • Track differences among character knowledge, audience knowledge, and dramatic irony.
  • Write concise, playable stage directions using verbals and precise action rather than novelistic explanation.
  • Use accurately: scene, act, stage direction, soliloquy, aside, dramatic irony.
  • Complete the director's notebook, scene analysis, and defined scored checks at 80% or higher.

Five-Day Stagecraft Laboratory

Page → objective → space → interpretation → defense.

Day 1 · Page to Stage

What Is Written—and What Must Be Chosen?

Read an opening scene first as literature, then as a blueprint for performance. Separate explicit textual information from staging choices a director or actor must supply.

  • Mark dialogue, stage directions, entrances/exits, pauses, and structural turns.
  • Create two columns: text requires / production chooses.
  • Identify the central immediate conflict and each major character's objective.
  • Write one question the script leaves open for performers.
Day 2 · Blocking & Power Lab

Space Makes an Argument

Test how position, distance, movement, stillness, level, entrance, and exit can alter a scene's power relationships without changing a word.

  • Draw a simple stage map and assign initial positions.
  • Create two contrasting blocking plans for the same short exchange.
  • For every major movement, cite the textual clue or interpretive reason supporting it.
  • Explain how one blocking change alters audience judgment.
Day 3 · Subtext, Silence & Language Lab

Characters Do Not Always Say What They Mean

Analyze what characters say, what they want, what they avoid saying, and what the audience may understand. Practice concise stage directions built from playable actions.

  • Annotate spoken text → likely objective → possible subtext → evidence.
  • Track one aside, soliloquy, silence, or audience-knowledge gap where available.
  • Rewrite ten vague/novelistic stage directions as concise playable actions using verbals where appropriate.
  • Use all six weekly vocabulary terms accurately.
  • Complete the scored drama/language check at 80% or higher; review and retry below 80%.
Day 4 · Director's Notebook Studio

Build an Interpretation the Text Can Support

Select a consequential scene and develop a director's concept. The concept may be creative, but every major interpretive decision must remain accountable to the script.

  • State the central interpretive claim in one sentence.
  • Map objectives, obstacles, tactics, turning points, blocking, and audience knowledge.
  • Use Evidence Studio to connect three staging decisions to textual evidence.
  • Draft the scene analysis in PROSE and preserve one meaningful revision.
  • Use ARSHIF only if historical, theatrical, or cultural context materially informs the staging.
Day 5 · Table Read & Director's Defense

Test the Interpretation in Performance

Conduct a table read or low-resource staging. Then defend and revise the director's notebook based on what the performance revealed.

  • Read or stage the selected passage with the planned blocking/voice choices.
  • Ask observers what relationship, objective, or power shift they perceived before explaining the intent.
  • Compare intended effect with observed effect.
  • Use peer, teacher, or Eiren challenge to revise one weak staging warrant.
  • Preserve annotations, stage maps, language check, director's notebook, scene analysis, feedback, and reflection.

Sovereign Stagecraft Game

Same Words, Different Stage

No external application is required. Use one short exchange and test how performance variables alter interpretation.

Round 1 · Distance

Play the exchange with characters far apart, then close together. Observers record what changes in intimacy, threat, authority, or vulnerability.

Round 2 · Movement

Give one character movement while the other remains still, then reverse the pattern. Which character appears to control the scene, and why?

Round 3 · Silence

Place a defensible pause at two different moments. Observers explain how the pause changes subtext or audience expectation. The group selects the version best supported by the script.

Eiren · Language Arts Mentor

Eiren asks whether the stage choice is earned.

Bring a scene annotation, blocking plan, subtext claim, or director concept first. Eiren can challenge the reasoning, surface alternative interpretations, and ask what evidence would distinguish them.

Open Eiren
Try: “What in the script supports placing these characters far apart?” · “Ask me whether this is subtext or something I invented without evidence.” · “Give me an alternative staging question, not an answer.” Eiren cannot write the assessed analysis, create the director concept for the student, assign verified scores, or override mastery.

Beta Program

Audit whether drama is actually being treated as performance.

Report inaccessible scripts, unclear stage vocabulary, broken public tools, staging activities that lack textual accountability, performance barriers without alternatives, confusing mastery language, or exercises that reduce drama to silent reading alone.
