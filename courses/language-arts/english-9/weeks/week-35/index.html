Capstone source conversation · Public memory and community space

Source A · literary perspective. A course-created first-person vignette describes a teenager returning to a neighborhood square after several years and noticing that a familiar mural has been replaced. The narrator calls the change “cleaner, but harder to recognize as ours.”

Source B · public communication. A fictional redevelopment notice states that the square was renovated to improve lighting, accessibility, drainage, and event capacity. It reports project goals but does not document how residents evaluated the result.

Source C · community responses. Twelve fictional comment cards include praise for safer paths and criticism that local artwork was removed without enough consultation. The cards are examples of perspective, not a representative survey.

Worked synthesis. The sources do not simply agree or disagree. The notice measures planned improvements; the vignette explores identity and memory; the comment cards reveal competing experiences. A synthesis claim should explain the relationship among these forms of evidence and acknowledge what none of them can establish alone.

Inquiry boundary. A feasible Grade 9 capstone question might ask how public communication and personal narrative frame the meaning of a shared place. It should not claim to determine what an entire real community believes from this simulated packet.

Transfer challenge. Create a synthesis matrix with agreement, tension, source limits, and usable evidence. Draft a thesis that requires at least two source types and include a counterreading you can answer fairly.

All sources in this packet are explicitly fictional course-created material.

Evidence-Based English Architecture · Unit 12

Capstone Synthesis Project · Retrieval & Transfer

Grade 9 closes with visible evidence of growth: students curate work, revise major pieces, synthesize learning across the year, complete the cumulative assessment, and defend their choices orally.

Day 1 · Frame

Choose a capstone question that allows meaningful connection across at least two major Grade 9 English domains.

Day 2 · Gather

Select evidence from literature, research, writing, speaking, or portfolio artifacts that genuinely contributes to the synthesis.

Day 3 · Synthesize

Build a claim that connects evidence across domains rather than presenting separate summaries.

Day 4 · Test

Identify one contradiction, limitation, or piece of evidence that complicates the capstone claim and revise accordingly.

Day 5 · Present

Complete a capstone statement explaining claim, strongest evidence, synthesis move, limitation, and what the project demonstrates about readiness.

Mastery boundary: These prompts strengthen portfolio curation, capstone synthesis, cumulative transfer, corrections, oral defense, readiness planning, and reflection only. They do not change assessment answers, thresholds, learner-record authority, or completion rules.
