Passage A · Beyond the Frame

Original course informational passage.
A map seems to promise certainty. Lines mark roads, colors divide regions, and symbols announce rivers, schools, borders, and elevation. Yet every map is also an argument about attention. A transit map may distort physical distance so that routes are easier to follow. A historical map may use modern borders that did not exist in the period being described. A digital map may recommend the fastest route while hiding the neighborhoods, tolls, or environmental costs that the route crosses. Recognizing these choices does not make maps useless. It makes readers more capable. The first question is not merely, “Is this map accurate?” but “Accurate for what purpose?” A hiking map should represent terrain and trail difficulty; a voting map should represent districts and population; an emergency map should make hazards and exits immediately visible. Each purpose demands different information and different tolerances for simplification. The same principle applies to written explanations. A writer cannot include every fact. Selection is unavoidable. Responsibility begins when selection becomes visible: terms are defined, sources are named, limitations are acknowledged, and important alternatives are not quietly removed. A trustworthy explanation does not pretend to be the whole world. It gives readers enough evidence to understand both what it shows and what it leaves beyond the frame.

Passage B · The Last Lantern

Original course literary passage.
By the time Nila reached the harbor, the storm had already erased the horizon. The fishing boats were black shapes tugging against their ropes, and every window along the pier had gone dark except one. Her grandfather's lantern still burned in the watch house. “You said you were leaving,” Nila called when she stepped inside. “I am,” he answered, tightening the strap on an old canvas bag. “After the light is transferred.” On the table lay a newer electric beacon, sealed in plastic beside a handwritten logbook. Nila stared at both. “The new one turns itself on.” “That is what the box says.” She almost laughed. Instead, she opened the logbook. Page after page recorded fog, failed batteries, broken glass, unexpected currents, and the names of boats that had returned safely. None of the entries sounded heroic. Most ended with small instructions written in the margin. Outside, the wind struck the building hard enough to shake dust from the rafters. Her grandfather lifted the old lantern from its hook and placed it beside the new beacon. “A light is useful,” he said, “but a record teaches you what darkness does.” Nila looked again at the logbook. Then she began reading from the first page.
