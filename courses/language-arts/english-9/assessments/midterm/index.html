Reading Passage

This passage was written for this course and may be printed with the assessment.
A public speech is often judged by the strength of its conclusion, but conclusions earn their force earlier. A speaker first establishes the situation: who must decide, what is at stake, and why the moment requires attention. Next, the speaker selects evidence suited to the audience. A technical report may persuade engineers, while a concrete example may help a general audience understand the same problem. Responsible persuasion also makes room for resistance. A speaker who ignores a serious objection may sound confident, yet the argument remains fragile. By stating an opposing concern accurately, the speaker demonstrates understanding. By conceding what is true in that concern, the speaker gains credibility. Only then can a rebuttal show why the original claim still stands or must be qualified. Style matters, but style cannot repair broken reasoning. Repetition can make a sentence memorable; it cannot make weak evidence sufficient. Emotional language can make listeners care; it cannot establish that a claim is factual. The most durable public voice joins ethical purpose, tested evidence, logical movement, and language shaped for listeners.
