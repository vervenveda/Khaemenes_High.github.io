Foundations & Readiness · Week 5

Evidence, Sources & Critical Thinking

Responsible research means slowing the leap from “I saw a claim” to “I know this is true.” Identify what is being claimed, trace where it came from, inspect the evidence, seek independent confirmation, and change your conclusion when better evidence requires it.

Begin

Part 1 · Classify Before Judging

Not every sentence makes the same kind of statement.

Observation

A directly available detail: “The chart labels the value 42.”

Verifiable Fact Claim

A statement that can in principle be checked against evidence: “The meeting began at 4:00.”

Inference

A conclusion drawn from evidence: “The writer may be emphasizing urgency.”

Opinion / Value Judgment

An evaluation: “The mural is beautiful.” Reasons can support it, but it is not the same kind of claim as a date or measurement.

Prediction

A claim about what will happen: “Attendance will increase next month.”

Critical-thinking rule: Classifying a statement does not decide whether it is good or bad. It tells you what kind of support or verification it requires.

Part 2 · Source Investigation

Use the PACE check.

Provenance

Who created this? Where did it originate? Can you trace the claim to an original document, dataset, recording, study, or firsthand account?

Audience & Purpose

Who is meant to see it, and why was it created—to inform, persuade, sell, entertain, document, recruit, or advocate?

Corroboration

Do independent sources confirm the central facts? Ten sites repeating one original source are not ten independent confirmations.

Evidence

What support is actually supplied? Is it relevant, sufficient, current enough for the claim, and represented accurately?

PACE is a starting routine, not a magic score. A source can have a purpose or perspective and still contain accurate evidence. Conversely, a polished neutral-looking page can still make unsupported claims.

Part 3 · Guided Source Comparison

The Cafeteria Waste Claim

Claim circulating at school: “The new tray-return signs cut cafeteria food waste in half.”

Source A — Student Eco Club post: Celebrates the new signs and says waste “dropped dramatically.” It links to a photograph of two collection bins but gives no measurements.

Source B — Cafeteria manager's weekly log: Records discarded food by weight. Week before signs: 214 lb. First week with signs: 176 lb. The log notes that school attendance was lower on two days during the second week.

Source C — Student newspaper article: Repeats the Eco Club's phrase “dropped dramatically,” links back to Source A, and does not cite the cafeteria log.

Part 4 · Bias Without the Shortcut

Perspective is something to examine—not a substitute for examining evidence.

A company publishes a report saying its tutoring program improved scores. A competing company criticizes the report. A school using the program publishes its own results.

Weak shortcut

“The company benefits, therefore its report is false.”

Better reasoning

“The company has an incentive to present favorable results, so inspect the study design, underlying data, comparison group, definitions, and whether independent evidence reproduces the result.”

Apply equally

The competitor also has incentives. Do not use stricter standards only against sources whose conclusions you dislike.

Bias-management habit: Apply the same evidence standard to claims you agree with, disagree with, hope are true, and hope are false.

Part 5 · Lateral Verification

Do not stay trapped inside the page.

When a claim matters, pause and investigate outside the source:

  1. Identify the exact claim.
  2. Search for the original source behind quotations, statistics, images, or summaries.
  3. Check whether the original actually says what is claimed.
  4. Look for independent corroboration, especially from sources with direct access to the evidence.
  5. Check date and context.
  6. Search for credible disagreement or correction.
  7. Revise your confidence level.
Mini-case: A post says, “A 2021 study proves students learn 40% faster with Method X.” Four blogs repeat the sentence, but all four link to the same marketing page. The marketing page mentions a study but provides no title, authors, journal, data, or link.
