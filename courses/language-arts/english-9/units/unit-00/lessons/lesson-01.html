Foundations & Readiness · Week 1

Read, Observe & Understand

The habit beneath the entire English I course is simple to say and powerful to practice: notice first → infer second → support the inference → explain the connection → preserve uncertainty.

Begin

Part 1 · Learn

Five moves of responsible reading

1 · Observation

An observation is directly available in the text. Example: “The bicycle is beside the gate.”

2 · Inference

An inference combines textual evidence with reasoning. “Someone may have arrived by bicycle” is plausible, but it is not directly stated.

3 · Evidence

Evidence is the specific detail that supports or challenges a claim. Evidence must actually bear on the conclusion.

4 · Warrant

The warrant explains why the evidence supports the inference. It exposes the reasoning so another person can examine it.

5 · Calibration

Say only what the evidence permits. Use “suggests,” “probably,” or “one possibility is” when certainty is limited.

Critical-thinking rule: A reasonable inference is not automatically a fact. A confident voice does not make weak evidence stronger.

Part 2 · Worked Example

Watch the reasoning happen.

Micro-passage: At 7:10, Mara reached the bus stop with one shoe untied. She looked down the road twice, checked the time on her phone, and began walking quickly toward school.

Observation

Mara checks the road twice, checks the time, and walks quickly toward school.

Inference

She may believe the bus is late or that she has missed it.

Warrant

People who expect transportation often look in its direction and check time; choosing to walk quickly suggests concern about arriving.

Limit

We cannot prove why the bus is absent, whether Mara is actually late, or what she is feeling.

Part 3 · Guided Reading

The Garden Gate

When Elias stepped into the community garden on Saturday morning, the wooden gate was already open. A small watering can rested beside the tomato bed, and dark soil clung to its base. Three tomato plants near the center stood upright, their soil damp. The plants at the far end leaned slightly, and the ground around them was pale and dry.

Elias set down his backpack. He glanced toward the apartment building across the street, but no one was at the windows. On the worktable he found a folded note: “Started early. Had to leave before I finished.” There was no name.

Elias filled the watering can. Before watering anything, however, he walked slowly along both rows and touched the soil near each plant.

Guided Question A

What three details can you state as direct observations without adding an explanation?

Guided Question B

What is one reasonable inference about what happened before Elias arrived? Cite two details.

Guided Question C

Why does Elias touch the soil before watering? Give a plausible inference, then identify what remains uncertain.

Check the reasoning—not just the answer

Possible observations include the open gate, damp soil near three plants, dry soil at the far end, the unsigned note, and Elias touching soil. A reasonable inference is that another gardener watered part of the row and left before finishing. The note and uneven moisture support that inference. Elias may be checking which plants actually need water; the text does not directly state his motive.

Part 4 · Independent Reading

The Missing Poster

On Monday, the student council placed four posters along the hallway announcing Friday's food drive. By Tuesday afternoon, the poster beside the library was gone. A strip of blue tape remained on the wall.

“Someone tore it down,” Devon said.

Nia looked at the tape and then at a rolling cart parked nearby. Several cardboard boxes rose above the cart's top shelf. “Maybe,” she said. “But the hallway was crowded during the book delivery this morning.”

At lunch, the librarian found the missing poster behind the cart. One corner was bent, but the paper was not torn.

Observation → Inference → Warrant → Challenge

Choose an ordinary scene you can observe safely—a room, desk, yard, hallway, photograph supplied by an educator, or another neutral setting. Record four observations. Then make two inferences. For each inference, identify its evidence, write the warrant, and invent a credible alternative explanation.
