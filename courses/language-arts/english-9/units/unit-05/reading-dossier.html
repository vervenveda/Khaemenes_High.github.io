WEEK 13 · ORIGINAL DRAMA

The Unopened Meeting

Original complete instructional scene · local-first reading

[A public hall in heavy rain. One bucket catches a leak; three chairs face the audience. Mara, a student, tests the door. Councilor Vale writes figures at a table. Dev, the caretaker, carries the only accessible entrance key.]

MARA: The ceiling is worse. VALE: The budget will cover the roof or a permanent ramp. We cannot do both tonight. DEV: Two neighbors still need someone to carry them up the entrance steps. VALE: A roof protects the greatest number. [He moves his ledger away from the dripping bucket. Dev sets his key on an empty chair.]

MARA: Yesterday I agreed about the roof. Today I watched Mrs. Imani wait in the rain outside. I still want the roof repaired. I just cannot say her access matters less. VALE: We have asked for patience. DEV: Who gets to decide how long someone else must wait? [A wheel catches against a step outside. All three turn toward the sound.]

VALE: We must vote before the hall closes. MARA: Should we vote when people affected by the ramp cannot enter the meeting? DEV: The empty chair was supposed to be theirs. [The clock strikes six. The bucket fills. Vale leaves the approval line on the ledger blank.]

Explicit lesson

Stage directions, movement, objects, and sound provide evidence beyond dialogue. The empty chair makes exclusion visible while the bucket makes immediate danger visible. Readers must compare the competing responsibilities rather than declare one speaker a villain.

Worked reading model

Claim: The empty chair represents absent decision-makers. Evidence: Dev places a key on it after naming neighbors unable to enter. Reasoning: the staging makes a flaw in Vale's majority argument visible, while still acknowledging the dangerous roof.

Guided and independent application

  1. Highlight three stage directions and state how each changes the audience's understanding.
  2. Write a dialogue chart: speaker, duty, textual proof, and unresolved question.
  3. Explain how the scene creates suspense without an omniscient narrator.
Teacher guidance

Assign three reading roles. Pause after each stage direction and ask what the audience knows that the speakers do not fully acknowledge.

WEEK 14 · ORIGINAL DRAMA

The Ledger and the Uncast Vote

Original complete instructional scene · local-first reading

[The hall after the meeting. Mara studies a repair ledger while Dev illuminates it with a flashlight. Vale remains near the empty gallery.]

MARA: Emergency roof patch, two thousand; permanent roof, six thousand; access ramp, five thousand. The patch has a stated lifespan of one season. VALE: Then the patch is cheaper, but may cost more later. DEV: And a portable ramp kept inside a locked building is not access at all. [Vale reaches for Dev's key. Dev does not offer it.]

VALE: I must protect public funds. MARA: If we postpone every repair, someone could be hurt. I cannot prove an injury is certain. DEV: If we repair only the roof, the sign PUBLIC HALL still hangs above steps that exclude some of our neighbors. VALE: You demand the impossible. DEV: I ask that the people affected join the decision.

[Vale takes out a notice sheet.] VALE: We called the meeting public, yet did not arrange an accessible way to attend. MARA: Put barriers under the leak now. Publish the costs. Hold the next meeting where everyone can enter. DEV: That addresses immediate danger without pretending we have settled the long-term decision.

[Vale writes the safety action, new meeting site, and disclosure of the ledger on the sheet. He leaves the funding choice unsigned. Outside, the rain slows. The ledger remains open for others to inspect.]

Explicit lesson

Drama presents competing duties through language and action. Conditional phrases such as 'If we postpone' make consequences visible while avoiding certainty. Reading a whole play, not just an isolated speech, is necessary to understand how a character's decisions develop.

Worked reading model

Interpretation: Vale changes his approach when he recognizes the procedure excluded some neighbors. Evidence: his line about the meeting being called public and his written plan for another site. Limitation: the scene does not show that a repair has been funded.

Guided and independent application

  1. Trace one character's reasoning across two speeches and one action.
  2. Compare short-term safety and long-term fairness; explicitly qualify one prediction.
  3. Connect the conflict to a competing-duty moment in the assigned full-length drama, citing that separate work.
Teacher guidance

Contrast procedure with outcome. Invite students to compare this scene with Antigone or Julius Caesar as assigned; do not replace the larger reading.

WEEK 15 · ORIGINAL DRAMA

Two Stagings, Unchanged Words

Original complete instructional scene · local-first reading

[Director's notebook. The same three characters rehearse a passage from the first meeting. The dialogue is kept exactly the same. The director changes only blocking, lighting, pause, and sound.]

STAGING A. Vale sits at a raised desk and lifts the ledger on 'greatest number.' The bucket stands at the center of the stage. Dev remains near the door in shadow. When the sound of a wheel comes from outside, the lights fade before the audience can see the entrance.

STAGING B. The three chairs stand at floor level. Dev tries to pull the empty chair through the narrow doorway; it catches against the frame. The bucket rests at the back of the room. Vale speaks his identical line about the greatest number and pauses before the last word. The audience sees the blocked chair during the pause.

MARA: I heard the roof as the urgent problem in A. In B, I kept watching the doorway. DEV: We did not change the words. VALE: We changed what the words stood beside. DIRECTOR: That is an interpretation, not a new event in the plot. Can you defend it from the script?

[The performers make an evidence chart with three headings: unchanged dialogue, revised production choice, likely audience effect. A student suggests that every viewer will favor the ramp in B. The director crosses out 'every' and writes 'may' instead. They rehearse once more and preserve both versions.]

Explicit lesson

A production can alter the force of dialogue without rewriting the script. Interpretations must be justified through stage directions, character speech, and a carefully qualified audience effect.

Worked reading model

The visible blocked chair in Staging B foregrounds exclusion, while the central bucket in Staging A foregrounds roof danger. Either staging may be defensible if the director explains its relationship to the text; neither guarantees every viewer's response.

Guided and independent application

  1. Draw a third stage arrangement and label two interpretive choices.
  2. Draft a 150-word comparison citing unchanged words and different stage effects.
  3. Deliver a one-minute evidence-based oral defense and record a counterinterpretation.
Teacher guidance

Run the same excerpt twice. Assess textual accountability, staging rationale, and thoughtful responses to a different reading.
