Source & Edition Guide

A famous title is not a complete citation.

Use this guide to select lawful, attributable passages and to record what happened between an oral performance, manuscript, original language, edition, and the English wording in front of you.

Non-Negotiable Practice

Identify the text you actually read.

  1. Record the work and the tradition, attributed author, community, or performance lineage.
  2. Name the translator, editor, collector, adaptor, or compiler when one shaped the version.
  3. Record the publication or host and a stable location marker: book/tablet/chapter/line/page/section.
  4. Distinguish a complete text, excerpt, summary, retelling, and machine-generated draft.
  5. Check whether the version may be reproduced where the learner lives. Public-domain status can vary by jurisdiction.
  6. Use a summary for orientation only. Quote and analyze the assigned primary passage.

Translation warning

Translations make decisions about syntax, register, rhythm, names, cultural concepts, and ambiguity. Two English versions can support different observations without either reproducing every feature of the source language.

Machine translation: useful for tentative clarification; never the named edition for the major essay.

Free Source Pathways

Select two authentic traditions.

The course recommends one sustained passage from each source. Educators may substitute a more current lawful edition and should record that choice.

The Odyssey

Tradition: Ancient Greek epic traditionally attributed to Homer.

Open edition: Samuel Butler’s English prose translation, first published 1900; Project Gutenberg eBook 1727.

Suggested selections: Book I opening and hospitality; Book IX identity and consequence; Book XII counsel, restraint, and loss.

Limitation: Butler’s diction and interpretive assumptions are historically situated. Compare with a contemporary library translation when possible, without reproducing it unlawfully.

Open Gutenberg Record

The Epic of Gilgamesh

Tradition: Ancient Mesopotamian epic preserved across Sumerian and Akkadian textual traditions.

Open edition: R. Campbell Thompson, The Epic of Gilgamish (1928), Internet Archive scan.

Suggested selections: kingship and the city; Enkidu and friendship; the search after loss.

Limitation: The 1928 rendering predates important discoveries and modern scholarship. Treat gaps, tablet reconstruction, names, and wording as edition-specific.

Open Archive Record

Popol Vuh

Tradition: K’iche’ Maya sacred narrative with older oral foundations and a colonial-era written transmission history.

Open historical rendering: Lewis Spence, Popol Vuh: The Mythic and Heroic Sagas of the Kiches of Central America (1908), hosted by Sacred Texts.

Suggested selections: attempts at making beings who can remember; Hero Twins episodes; language, community, and responsibility.

Limitation: Spence’s title, framing, spelling, and interpretation are dated and mediated. Do not treat this version as a substitute for contemporary K’iche’ scholarship or community authority.

Open Historical Text

Additional tradition

Educators may select a lawful passage from the Mahabharata, Ramayana, Shahnameh, Sundiata, Mwindo, Kalevala, Enuma Elish, or a specifically identified oral tradition.

Requirement: A broad label such as “African myth” or “Indigenous story” is not sufficient. Name the people, language, teller/collector/translator where known, source, and permission boundary.

Living Traditions

Public access is not cultural permission.

Some stories, songs, names, images, performances, or knowledge may be seasonal, ceremonial, family-held, gender-specific, community-governed, restricted, or inappropriate to detach from their language and relationships. A classroom assignment does not create authority to reproduce, dramatize, remix, or publish them.

  • Prefer community-authored and community-approved public sources.
  • Identify the specific nation, people, language, and source.
  • Honor restrictions and remove a source when authority or permission is unclear.
  • Analyze the colonial or collecting context when a text was recorded by an outsider.
  • Do not reward “exotic” comparison or treat traditions as interchangeable examples of one universal pattern.
