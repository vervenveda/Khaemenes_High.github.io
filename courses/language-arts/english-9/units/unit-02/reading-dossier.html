Week 05 · Epic hero. Homer, The Odyssey, trans. Samuel Butler, Project Gutenberg eBook #1727. Assigned span: Book IX, with emphasis on Odysseus, the Cyclops episode, hospitality, cunning, consequence, and cultural values. Open stable source

Rights/provenance: the Gutenberg records identify the Spence and Butler editions as public domain in the USA; the Sacred Text Archive identifies the Thompson edition as public domain. Students should cite the exact edition and assigned section, not merely the work title.

Authentic-text custody record

For every assigned authentic work, record the exact title, author or tradition, editor/translator when relevant, edition or stable source, assigned chapter/passage, provenance or permission/public-domain basis, and the local/offline or approved access route. “A literary passage” is not a complete source record. WEEK 04 · UNIT 02

Why Humans Make Myths

Original practice myth · The River That Remembered

Before the valley had roads, the people crossed the river by listening. In spring the water spoke loudly against the stones; in winter it whispered beneath ice. A young potter named Sena began leaving clay marks on the bank so travelers could judge the safe crossing. When a flood erased the marks, the elders blamed the river for forgetting. Sena answered that the river had remembered exactly what water does: it changed. The people rebuilt their crossing higher and began teaching children both the old songs and the new marks.

Reading model

A mythic narrative can encode a worldview through repeated natural images, communal rules, tests and transformations. Do not reduce a tradition to a single moral. Ask what problem the story imagines, what behavior it rewards or questions, and whose perspective is centered.

Independent task

Compare this practice text with one approved authentic myth. Record exact title/tradition/source, identify one shared narrative pattern, one cultural difference, and one detail that prevents a simplistic “all myths are the same” conclusion.

WEEK 05 · UNIT 02

Epic Heroes & Cultural Values

Original practice epic episode · The Gate of Ash

Arun reached the city gate carrying the bronze key his father had guarded for thirty years. The watch captain ordered him to enter first because the smoke beyond the wall hid the road. Arun refused. “A key is not courage,” he said. He gave the key to the oldest porter, tied a rope around his waist, and crossed without it. When he returned, the crowd praised his bravery. The porter asked why he had disobeyed. Arun answered, “Because a hero who keeps every symbol of honor may leave everyone else carrying the danger.”

Epic-reading model

Epic heroes often embody cultural ideals while also exposing their limits. Track status, duty, hospitality, loyalty, pride, consequence and the community’s judgment. A comparison should use specific episodes rather than labels such as ‘brave’ or ‘strong.’

Independent task

Using an approved episode from Gilgamesh, The Odyssey, or another authentic epic, build an evidence matrix: test, choice, cultural value, consequence, and complication. Then write a qualified comparison with the practice episode.

WEEK 06 · UNIT 02

Comparative Myth Synthesis

Practice comparison

In one original classroom story, a community survives by preserving an old route. In another, survival depends on abandoning the route when the landscape changes. Their surface pattern is similar—danger, collective memory, a test—but their treatment of tradition differs.

Synthesis model

Comparative analysis should organize by a meaningful criterion—duty, knowledge, authority, transformation—not by summarizing Text A and then Text B. Context matters: a recurring flood, trickster, descent or hero pattern can perform different cultural work.

Independent task

Create a three-column synthesis using two approved authentic myths/epics plus one course-created practice text. Include context, pattern, difference, evidence locator, and one statement of interpretive limitation. Draft a thesis that preserves cultural difference.
