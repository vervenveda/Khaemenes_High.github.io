WEEK 16 · ORIGINAL FICTIONAL SOURCES

The Assembly and Its Absent Audience

Fictional classroom documents · locally accessible

In a fictional public assembly, Chair Vale began: “Every neighbor deserves a safe route to the library. The footbridge is not merely a structure. It is a promise to children who need a place to study and to workers returning home after dark.” Several listeners applauded. Vale asked them to support a trial crossing, additional lighting, and a survey of traffic near the library. She did not specify how long the trial would run or how residents would judge success.

One resident wrote: “I support better lighting, but I work nights and was unable to attend. A painted crossing might not change how drivers turn.” Another asked whether wheelchair users had been consulted about the location. The chair read both statements aloud. These comments did not reject the goal of safety; they challenged whether a proposed solution would work for all people. Vale invited further testimony instead of counting criticism as opposition.

The clerk recorded twenty-one attendees, fourteen signatures in favor of a trial, five requests for more information, and two attendees who left before signing. The record offered no survey of the full neighborhood. Reviewing the minutes, Vale revised a sentence in her published address: “Many residents present supported a trial, while important questions remain about design and accessibility.” The revision narrowed the factual claim without abandoning the original purpose of improving access.

Explicit instruction

Identify speaker, audience, occasion, purpose, constraints and the difference between ethical appeal and factual claim. The audience includes people absent from the room; applause is not a representative survey.

Worked reading model

The phrase 'every neighbor deserves' appeals to shared values, while 'fourteen signatures' reports limited evidence. A defensible précis says the chair urged a safety trial, appealed to community duty and then qualified her representation of community support.

Guided and independent application

  1. Annotate the chair's appeal and two audience-specific concerns.
  2. Write a rhetorical précis in 100–130 words with a qualified evidence claim.
  3. Describe two sources needed before claiming full neighborhood approval.

Misconception check: Attendee signatures do not automatically represent all residents.

Reading self-check · Week 16

Can a speaker sincerely defend safety for everyone without proving that everyone supports one particular design?

Reveal the model response

Yes. A value statement is distinct from an empirical claim about public opinion.

Teacher guidance

Model purpose versus evidence; have students label quotations separately from inferences. Preserve course speech and portfolio records.

WEEK 17 · ORIGINAL FICTIONAL SOURCES

The Evidence Audit and Two Editorials

Fictional classroom documents · locally accessible

A fictional transport-office memo stated that observers recorded thirty-eight turns by vehicles near the library during twelve short afternoon sessions. Four turns blocked the pedestrian waiting space. No collisions were observed while the observers were present. The memo cautioned that the sessions were too brief to estimate year-round collision risk and that observers did not consistently cover both directions of traffic. The counts referred to turning events, not unique drivers.

The first editorial asserted: “Either approve our crossing immediately or admit you do not care about children.” The second declared: “No one was injured during the observations, so the road is safe and any change is wasteful.” A student editor marked the first statement as a false dilemma; there could be several ways to improve safety. The second relied on limited observation to draw a much broader conclusion. The student did not call either author dishonest; the criticism concerned reasoning.

A council member proposed a further study that would compare crossing designs, document accessibility needs, and observe additional times of day. Four of thirty-eight recorded turns, approximately 10.5 percent of turns in the narrow sample, involved blocking the waiting space. The council member resisted describing the figure as a rate for all traffic or a percentage of dangerous drivers. The meeting ended without a construction decision; the next task was to gather evidence appropriate to the question.

Explicit instruction

Analyze claim, warrant, evidence and limitation. A fallacy label must explain the flawed inference: false choice, insufficient sample, or unjustified causal assumption.

Worked reading model

Four divided by thirty-eight equals about 10.5 percent of observed turns, not drivers or annual trips. The claim that zero observed collisions proves universal safety overreaches the sample.

Guided and independent application

  1. Explain the precise reasoning error in each editorial.
  2. Rewrite one overclaim as a testable, fair proposal.
  3. Identify the numerator, denominator and sampling limitation in the memo.

Misconception check: Absence of observed harm is not proof of universal safety.

Reading self-check · Week 17

Does observing no collisions in a brief sample establish zero future risk?

Reveal the model response

No. It supports only a statement about the observation period.

Teacher guidance

Ask students to state what each record can and cannot warrant. Keep the exercise a fictional analysis, not road-safety fieldwork.

WEEK 18 · ORIGINAL FICTIONAL SOURCES

A Public Address Revised for the Ear

Fictional classroom documents · locally accessible

Student speaker Jae drafted: “The road is dangerous. Anyone who disagrees with an immediate crossing values money more than children. We should build it tomorrow.” The first paragraph asked for agreement before defining the evidence or the particular change being requested. During rehearsal, a classmate asked whether a painted line, a signal or a different route would better serve pedestrians. Another listener asked who had counted the traffic and when.

Jae revised: “In twelve short sessions, observers saw four of thirty-eight turning movements block the waiting space. That is reason to investigate, not proof that a specific design will work. I ask the council to publish the design options, hear from people who use mobility aids and from workers traveling after dark, and state how a trial would be evaluated.” Jae kept a pause after the numbers and another before the request. The uncertainty was spoken clearly rather than apologized for.

An instructor's rehearsal note said: “Explain the denominator aloud. Avoid a list of numbers that listeners cannot follow. Use a slower pace when the argument moves from evidence to recommendation.” Jae practiced with an audience member who preferred to read the speech and another who needed the spoken version repeated. Their feedback led to a shorter introduction and a printed summary. The speech became more accessible and more persuasive because its practical action matched the narrow evidence.

Explicit instruction

A speech written for the ear needs a clear audience, organizational transitions, intelligible evidence, paced delivery and an actionable conclusion. A counterclaim should be represented fairly.

Worked reading model

Compare 'build it tomorrow' with 'publish and assess design options.' The latter request follows from the limited observation data and does not caricature opponents.

Guided and independent application

  1. Draft a four- to six-minute original evidence-based speech using the course rubric.
  2. Mark three pauses, two transitions and one place to define a number or technical term.
  3. Preserve a first and revised draft plus an audience-feedback note in the existing student portfolio.

Misconception check: Volume, emotional certainty and dramatic gestures do not replace reasoning.

Reading self-check · Week 18

Why is a narrower call to action sometimes more rhetorically credible?

Reveal the model response

It asks the audience to take an action justified by the available evidence rather than overstating what is known.

Teacher guidance

Use the existing teacher-scored speech rubric and accommodate recorded, oral or accessible delivery without altering mastery authority.
