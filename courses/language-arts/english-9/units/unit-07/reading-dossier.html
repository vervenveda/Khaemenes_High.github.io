WEEK 19 · ORIGINAL FICTIONAL RESEARCH RECORDS

An Undated Museum Claim

Classroom practice texts only · not genuine archive documents

Source A · Gallery label (invented)

A local museum's digital label says, “The Meridian Footbridge changed how nearly everyone crossed the river.” It names a bridge but does not list the author, a date, or the source of the claim. A photograph below the statement shows a crowded opening ceremony. The label is written in a confident voice, and visitors often repeat its words when describing the bridge. A student named Noor asks whether the phrase “nearly everyone” refers to residents, commuters, or only people photographed at the ceremony. She notices that the label discusses attendance rather than the routes people used after the opening. The museum website appears professionally designed, but the visual design does not supply the missing evidence.

Source B · Research desk memorandum (invented)

A fictional archive inventory lists three possible records: a council meeting agenda announcing a bridge trial, a traffic count made on one weekday after opening, and an interview with two residents. The inventory explains that its single traffic count was made between 8 and 9 a.m. on a Tuesday, not throughout an entire month. It also warns that the interview participants were selected because they volunteered at a neighborhood meeting. Noor decides the records might answer narrower questions about the trial, morning traffic, and specific people's experiences. They do not yet justify any townwide conclusion.

Explicit reading instruction

Begin with a question that can be answered by obtainable evidence, not with a conclusion you hope to prove. Divide a broad topic into a population, place, period, and measurable outcome. Search terms should include synonyms, dates, organizations, and types of primary record. Distinguish the apparent authority of a polished website from documented authorship and method.

Worked reasoning model

Weak question: “Did the bridge change everything?” Improved question: “What do the archived weekday pedestrian counts show about morning use of the Meridian Footbridge during its first month?” This specifies a measure and time period. Yet if the only count is one hour on one Tuesday, even that question remains unanswered; a researcher should request additional counts or narrow the question again.

Guided and independent application

Circle the undefined population in “nearly everyone.” Underline the only recorded counting interval. Write one question about morning use and a different question about accessibility that would require different evidence.

  1. Prepare a search log with a precise question, three keyword combinations, a source type, and a verification plan.
  2. Identify which invented document would support a historical claim, a numerical claim, and an individual experience claim.
  3. Write a 90–120-word research proposal that distinguishes what is known from what remains unavailable.

Research integrity check

The first search result, a professional design, or a memorable photograph does not establish a source's completeness or relevance.

Does one traffic count from 8–9 a.m. establish how the bridge was used by all residents over a month?

No. It documents only the people counted at that specified time under that count's method. The sample and interval do not cover the broader population or month.

Teacher guidance

Use the fictional texts to practice question design. For a genuine project require a real accessible archive and record the actual URL, publisher, date, and retrieval details. Keep the student's question and search log in the existing Week 19 workspace.

WEEK 20 · ORIGINAL FICTIONAL RESEARCH RECORDS

Two Articles, One Statistic

Classroom practice texts only · not genuine archive documents

Source A · Sponsored community article (invented)

A fictional website article titled “Bridge Trial Solves Town Traffic” states that sixty percent of people who answered a sidewalk survey favored keeping the new bridge. A small notice at the bottom says the article was paid for by an imaginary construction cooperative. The article does not explain how respondents were contacted, how many people answered, or whether pedestrians and drivers were both eligible. It quotes a shop owner who says foot traffic improved, but provides no receipts or independent analysis. The writer presents the survey as a community decision even though neither the sampling frame nor the total number of eligible residents is described.

Source B · Municipal summary note (invented)

A separate fictional clerk's note gives the missing counts: 18 of 30 people who returned a questionnaire supported the trial, eight opposed it, and four gave no preference. The form was distributed to visitors at a weekday public meeting. The note states that 30 is the respondent count, not the number of town residents or bridge users. It reports only opinions among respondents and does not measure accidents, travel times, accessibility, or business sales. When a student compares both texts, the sixty-percent figure is arithmetically correct for those thirty responses; the broader claim about solving traffic is not justified by the reported data.

Explicit reading instruction

Lateral reading means leaving a page to compare its claims with independent information about authorship, purpose, methods and evidence. A declared sponsor may affect incentives, but a source should be evaluated by traceable claims rather than rejected solely by association. Ask what was measured, by whom, when, and in which population. A statistic can be numerically accurate yet misleading when its denominator or sample is hidden.

Worked reasoning model

18 ÷ 30 = 0.60, or 60% of the thirty respondents—not 60% of residents and not evidence that traffic was solved. A qualified synthesis reads: “A small convenience sample at a weekday meeting recorded 18 supportive responses out of 30; the evidence does not establish wider support or improved travel conditions.” It identifies the numerator, denominator, sample, and limits.

Guided and independent application

Trace the 60% figure backward: what are its numerator, denominator, method, and sponsor? Which claims would require an accident log, transportation study, or independent business data?

  1. Create a four-part source audit for both invented texts: authorship, supporting evidence, potential incentive, and limit.
  2. Write two factual statements based on the note, then rewrite the sponsored article's headline so that it fits its evidence.
  3. List three real-world lateral-reading actions, including checking publisher information and finding independent documentation.

Research integrity check

Bias is not demonstrated solely by sponsorship, and a valid percentage does not automatically support the conclusion attached to it.

What does 18 supportive responses out of 30 questionnaires tell you?

Exactly 60% of those thirty responses supported the trial. Without a representative sampling design, it cannot be generalized to the whole town.

Teacher guidance

Model respectful but critical source scrutiny. Differentiate arithmetic validation from sampling validity. The fictional article and clerk's note are practice documents, not authentic sources to cite as local history.

WEEK 21 · ORIGINAL FICTIONAL RESEARCH RECORDS

The Footbridge Research Notebook

Classroom practice texts only · not genuine archive documents

Source A · Student evidence ledger (invented)

A student's research notebook contains a council agenda, the single Tuesday traffic count, a sponsored article, and the clerk's questionnaire totals. Beside every entry the student records the issuing body or writer, date if available, and the exact claim the source can support. The original museum label still has no confirmed publication date. Instead of inventing one, the student marks the date as unavailable and requests metadata from the museum. Her notes also distinguish the question “Was the bridge trial proposed?” from the question “Did the bridge reduce travel hazards?” The agenda is relevant to the first question but does not provide outcome data for the second.

Source B · Draft and revision conference (invented)

The student's first paragraph says, “Everyone agreed that the new bridge was safer and transformed the town.” The instructor asks for evidence, so the student revises: “In the fictional questionnaire, eighteen of thirty weekday meeting respondents favored the trial, while the supplied documents do not establish townwide opinion or changes in safety.” The student places the questionnaire figures beside that sentence, identifies the research note as the source, and lists the missing hazard records as a limitation. The revision is shorter but easier to verify. Her report ends by proposing a representative survey and repeated observations at different times before drawing a broader conclusion.

Explicit reading instruction

Synthesis is organized around the research question and claims, not a sequence of disconnected summaries. Attribute each claim to its source, represent disagreement honestly, and keep the denominator attached to every numerical statement. A citation must identify a real document when doing real research; invented classroom texts here must not become fabricated external references. End by separating supported findings, limitations, and responsible next inquiries.

Worked reasoning model

Model synthesis: “The council agenda documents the proposal of a footbridge trial, while the clerk's fictional questionnaire shows support among a limited group of meeting attendees. Neither establishes a reduction in accidents or represents every resident. The most responsible conclusion is that the trial drew recorded interest but needs wider evaluation.” In a real paper, cite the agenda and questionnaire by their verified author and date; do not supply invented bibliographic details.

Guided and independent application

Underline the claim supported by the agenda, one supported by the questionnaire, and one that lacks supporting measurement. Which should be revised or omitted?

  1. Compose a 180–240-word evidence-led synthesis using the two fictional records and a transparent limitations statement.
  2. Produce a source ledger with publication dates marked “not supplied” wherever the exercise does not provide them.
  3. Submit a revised thesis and a short correction note in your existing Week 21 workspace; explain how each citation supports its claim.

Research integrity check

Citing a source cannot make a claim credible when the source did not actually measure or describe that claim.

May an unverified museum page be given a made-up date to make a bibliography appear complete?

No. Record the date as unavailable according to the citation convention, or find reliable metadata. Never invent source information.

Teacher guidance

Use a claim-evidence-citation checklist and ask students to trace one sentence all the way to its document. The existing course assessment, bibliography and research report retain scoring authority.
