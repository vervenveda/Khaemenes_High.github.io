Essential Question

How can researchers decide what evidence justifies believing, doubting, qualifying, or revising a claim?

Learning Priorities

  • Develop focused research questions and mutate them when evidence exposes a better question.
  • Separate observation, source claim, inference, interpretation, and conclusion.
  • Trace provenance and source lineage rather than confusing repetition with corroboration.
  • Test expertise, evidence, incentives, context, independence, limitations, and missing information.
  • Identify assumptions, warrants, counterevidence, alternative explanations, causal overreach, and uncertainty.
  • Recognize framing, manipulated context, statistical presentation, and media-format effects.
  • Synthesize disagreement without manufacturing consensus or false balance.
  • State what evidence would change a conclusion and revise when stronger evidence warrants it.

Evidence Contract

Critical-Thinking Spine

Use the same disciplined questions throughout the rest of English 9.

1 · What is actually claimed?

Define terms, scope, population, timeframe, comparison, and degree of certainty before evaluating the claim.

2 · What supports it?

Locate evidence and provenance. Ask whether the evidence is relevant, sufficient, representative, independent, and accurately presented.

3 · What reasoning connects them?

Expose the warrant and assumptions. Test causation, generalization, analogy, classification, and other inferential moves.

4 · What challenges it?

Seek counterevidence, competing explanations, disconfirming cases, missing data, and credible uncertainty.

5 · What should change?

Calibrate the conclusion to the evidence and state what new evidence would strengthen, weaken, or reverse it.

Critical thinking is not automatic skepticism. Students receive credit for warranted revision—including changing their minds—when stronger evidence demands it.
