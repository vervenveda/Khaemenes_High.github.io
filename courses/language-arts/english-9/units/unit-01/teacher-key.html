Unit 1 Guidance & Key

Evaluate evidence, not compliance.

This key provides acceptable reasoning, not scripts students must reproduce. Protect assessment independence and review writing with the published rubric.

Coursebook

Week 1 sentence practice

  1. Fragment. Accept any revision that adds an independent clause, such as: “Although several residents questioned the cost, they listened to the revised plan.”
  2. Comma splice. Accept a period, semicolon, or comma plus a suitable coordinating conjunction.
  3. Complete sentence.

Week 2 guided reading

  1. The note supplies a research lead and reveals an otherwise absent community memory; it has evidentiary value as a question-generating artifact but not as independent proof.
  2. The conflict demonstrates that memory is situated and fallible. It can preserve experience, but claims should be compared with other evidence and qualified when accounts diverge.
  3. The contrast establishes the ethical limit of interpretation: researchers should listen for what incomplete records suggest without converting possibility into certainty.

Analytical paragraph: Score claim, evidence, reasoning, organization, and language control. Do not reward quotation volume without explanation.

Week 3 editing

  1. “Lena wanted a definite answer, but the sources required a qualified conclusion.” A semicolon is also valid.
  2. Add an independent clause: “Because the newspaper confirmed the flood but not the children’s crossing, Lena continued investigating.”
  3. Use periods, semicolons, or coordinated clauses: “The map raised a question; the interviews complicated it; the council minutes narrowed it.”
  4. Add an independent clause: “After comparing every source and reconsidering her original claim, Lena revised the paper.”

Mastery check

  1. An observation identifies a textual fact or feature; an inference is a conclusion reasonably drawn from observations and context.
  2. Accept any defensible claim grounded in the passage. Strong answers address intellectual honesty, uncertainty, source comparison, or revision.
  3. Evidence must be accurate and reasoning must explain the connection rather than repeat the quotation.
  4. Examples: “The note was intriguing, but it was not proof.” / “The note was intriguing; it was not proof.”
  5. Patchwriting preserves a source’s language or structure closely enough to present another writer’s expression as the student’s own; superficial substitutions do not create an independent paraphrase.
