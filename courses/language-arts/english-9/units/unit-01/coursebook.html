Week 1 · Diagnostic Reading & Writer Identity

Begin with honest evidence.

A diagnostic is a starting photograph, not a permanent label. Its purpose is to reveal what deserves practice.

Lesson 1.1 · Before the Diagnostic

What strong readers actually do

Reading closely is not the same as reading slowly. A close reader notices meaningful details, asks how those details work together, and tests an interpretation against the whole text. An observation names what the text says or does. An inference explains what the observation reasonably suggests. A claim is an interpretation that evidence and reasoning can defend.

Worked model

Observation: The students investigate ownership, safety, community need, and cost before asking for money.

Inference: Their proposal becomes credible because inquiry comes before enthusiasm.

Claim: The passage presents responsible change as a process of questioning, evidence gathering, and revision.

Literacy inventory

Answer in complete sentences. There are no “correct” answers.

Lessons 1.2–1.3 · Baseline Assessment

Complete the original diagnostic

  1. Open the diagnostic and read the passage once for meaning.
  2. Reread with a pencil or notes. Mark the central problem, the opposition, the revision, and the outcome.
  3. Answer the ten objective questions without outside help.
  4. Plan and write the 500–700 word analysis.
  5. Print or preserve the response before continuing.

Lesson 1.4 · Sentence and Paragraph Control

A sentence must carry a complete thought

An independent clause contains a subject, a complete verb, and a thought that can stand alone. A fragment is punctuated like a sentence but cannot stand alone. A run-on joins complete thoughts without correct punctuation or coordination.

Fragment: Because the students revised the plan.

Completed: Because the students revised the plan, residents took the proposal seriously.

Run-on: The station was neglected the students saw a possibility.

Corrected: The station was neglected, but the students saw a possibility.

Guided practice

Identify each as complete, fragment, or run-on. Then revise any error.

Growth goal

A useful goal describes a behavior, evidence, and review date. “Become a better writer” is a wish. “Before submitting each Unit 1 paragraph, I will underline every complete verb and repair sentence-boundary errors” is measurable.

Week 2 · Close Reading, Claims & Evidence

Move from detail to inference.

Evidence does not explain itself. The writer must show how a precise detail supports a reasonable conclusion.

Lesson 2.1 · Original Reading

The Map in the Margin

On the first morning of the neighborhood history project, Lena received a photocopy of an old street map. Its labels were faint, and a dark coffee ring covered part of the river. She nearly set it aside. Newer maps were clearer, and the city website could locate every current road in seconds.

Then she noticed handwriting in the margin: Footbridge washed out, April 1968. Children crossed at Bell Street until autumn. The note was unsigned. It did not prove that a bridge had existed, much less that children had used it. Still, it changed her question. Instead of asking, “Where were the streets?” Lena began asking, “How did people move when the official routes failed?”

She compared the map with newspaper archives, council minutes, and three recorded interviews. The newspaper confirmed a flood. Council minutes documented emergency repairs but did not mention children. Two interviewees remembered adults placing boards across the narrowest part of the stream; the third remembered being forbidden to cross there. Their memories disagreed about who built the crossing and how long it lasted.

Lena’s first draft declared that the handwritten note had uncovered a forgotten bridge. Her second draft was more cautious. She wrote that the note revealed a community memory worth investigating, while the surviving sources supported only a temporary crossing after the flood. The revision sounded less dramatic. It was also more truthful.

At the exhibition, a visitor asked whether Lena was disappointed that she could not prove the original note completely. She shook her head. “The uncertainty is part of the history,” she said. “It shows where the record becomes quiet—and where we have to listen carefully without pretending we heard more than we did.”

Lesson 2.2 · Annotation and Inference

Use a four-part evidence chain

  1. Context: identify where the evidence appears and what is happening.
  2. Evidence: quote only the words needed or paraphrase accurately.
  3. Inference: state what the evidence reasonably suggests.
  4. Warrant: explain why that inference supports the claim.
Model evidence chain

Claim: Lena becomes a more responsible researcher by accepting uncertainty.

Evidence: Her second draft says the note revealed “a community memory worth investigating,” but limits the supported conclusion to a temporary crossing.

Reasoning: The careful wording separates what attracted her attention from what the surviving records can verify. That distinction shows intellectual honesty rather than weakness.

Guided questions

Lesson 2.3 · Clauses and Sentence Purpose

Choose structure to show relationships

A simple sentence contains one independent clause. A compound sentence joins independent clauses. A complex sentence joins an independent clause with at least one dependent clause. Structure matters because it tells readers which ideas are equal and which ideas depend on others.

Simple: The interviews conflicted.

Compound: The interviews conflicted, so Lena qualified her conclusion.

Complex: Because the interviews conflicted, Lena qualified her conclusion.

Lessons 2.4–2.5 · Analytical Paragraph

Write a complete claim–evidence–reasoning paragraph

Prompt: How does Lena’s revision reveal a change in her understanding of responsible research?

Your paragraph must include a defensible claim, two precise details, reasoning after each detail, purposeful transitions, and a concluding insight. Recommended length: 250–350 words.

Week 3 · Revision, Style & Academic Integrity

Make writing clearer and more trustworthy.

Revision changes meaning, structure, evidence, or reasoning. Editing corrects the language that carries those decisions.

Lesson 3.1 · Revision Laboratory

Revise in layers

  1. Purpose: Does the draft make a defensible point?
  2. Evidence: Is every important statement supported precisely?
  3. Reasoning: Does the explanation connect evidence to the claim?
  4. Organization: Does each sentence advance the paragraph?
  5. Style and correctness: Are the words exact and the sentences controlled?
Before

Lena did good research because she found lots of sources. The interviews were different. This shows that history is hard. She changed her paper and made it better.

After

Lena’s research becomes more credible when she allows conflicting evidence to narrow her conclusion. Because the interviews disagree about the temporary crossing, she no longer presents the marginal note as proof of a forgotten bridge. Her revision replaces a dramatic certainty with a conclusion the available record can support.

Lesson 3.2 · Academic Integrity

Credit ideas and represent sources honestly

Academic integrity means more than avoiding copied sentences. It means distinguishing your ideas from another person’s, quoting accurately, paraphrasing genuinely, citing sources, and never making evidence appear stronger or more certain than it is.

Source: “The revision sounded less dramatic. It was also more truthful.”

Acceptable quotation: The narrator emphasizes that Lena’s less dramatic revision is “more truthful,” connecting caution with integrity.

Acceptable paraphrase: The narrator values the accuracy of Lena’s revised claim over the excitement of her first version.

Patchwriting—not acceptable: Lena’s revision was less dramatic, but it was more truthful. This follows the source’s wording and structure too closely without quotation.
