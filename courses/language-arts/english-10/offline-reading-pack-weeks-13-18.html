WEEK 13 · INTEGRATED ORIGINAL READING

The Garden Question — Two Competing Editorials

Complete fictional source set · offline readable · not a genuine municipal record

EDITORIAL A · The Garden Is Already a Classroom. The Maple Court Garden occupies a vacant public lot. According to a sign-in notebook kept by volunteers, 67 visits were recorded last month; some visitors came more than once. Neighbors grew beans and herbs, and two teachers brought small groups to observe insects and measure soil moisture. A council member now proposes keeping the garden open with a modest annual grant. Those who ask for more documentation are not wrong to ask questions. But a project should not be invisible simply because its work is quiet.

The garden association's volunteer chair argues that the site builds skills no classroom can fully imitate: patience, cooperation, attention to seasons, and care for shared property. She points to observations made by students and asks the council to consider the value of a public place not measured solely by ticket sales. Her conclusion, however, extends beyond her evidence when she says that every family benefits. The notebook records visits, not distinct households, and no systematic neighborhood survey is available.

EDITORIAL B · Accountability Is Not Opposition. A local budget reviewer agrees that the garden matters. Yet he asks how many unique people use it, what annual maintenance will cost, whether water access is secure, and how other public projects will be compared. A visit total can rise when the same few volunteers attend repeatedly. He does not claim the garden lacks value. He argues that funding decisions require information about opportunity cost and equitable access.

The reviewer proposes a three-month trial grant, a public expenditure ledger, and anonymous weekly visitor counts that distinguish total visits from returning visitors without collecting names. The volunteer chair welcomes a trial but worries that a temporary arrangement will discourage planting for the next season. Both writers claim to support community learning. They disagree about what evidence is sufficient before money is committed.

Explicit instruction

Distinguish each editorial's claim, reasons, evidence, warrant, and hidden uncertainty. Do not flatten a conditional funding proposal into a claim that the garden should close. Both essays are invented classroom sources and are not records of real local government.

Worked evidence analysis

The first writer's observation notebook supports the claim that the garden is being used. It does not prove universal benefit. The second writer's call for unique visitor counts is an evidentiary critique, not opposition to gardens. A fair comparison identifies common values before evaluating competing policies.

Independent reasoning and writing

  1. Construct a two-column argument matrix with a fair four-sentence summary of each editorial.
  2. Explain why '67 visits' cannot automatically be read as '67 distinct residents.'
  3. Write a tentative judgment specifying what further evidence would change it.

Reading self-check · Week 13

Do 67 garden visits demonstrate 67 different residents using the space?

Reveal the model response

No. A visit count may include repeat visitors, and there was no unique-visitor tracking. It supports activity but not the number of distinct residents.

Practice only. This does not record a grade or alter the weekly assessment.

Teacher guidance

Invite readers to steelman each view before choosing a side. Evaluate whether sources support the breadth of each claim, not merely whether the reader agrees.

WEEK 14 · INTEGRATED ORIGINAL READING

The Budget Ledger — Claims, Counterclaims and Warrants

Complete fictional source set · offline readable · not a genuine municipal record

DOCUMENT 1 · Working budget for Maple Court Garden, fictional. The garden seeks $4,000 in annual support. Proposed costs: water and repairs, $1,600; materials and accessibility improvements, $1,400; educational supplies, $1,000. Last year's volunteer sign-in recorded 67 visits in one month, without checking whether the same individuals visited more than once. An alternative proposal would provide $2,000 for a three-month evaluation period. Neither proposal includes a full comparison with other town projects.

DOCUMENT 2 · A letter from the garden association. 'If the council waits for perfect evidence, smaller projects will never receive help. The children who worked here last spring cannot put their learning on hold until a formal survey arrives. We ask for the full grant, transparent purchasing, and a student-designed impact report. The council can support a living classroom while still requesting better records.'

DOCUMENT 3 · The review committee's reply. 'We have not concluded that the garden lacks educational value. Our concern is that a full recurring grant obligates future budgets. The trial proposal would fund immediate maintenance and collect evidence about use and access. We will reconsider ongoing support when costs and benefits can be compared more carefully with other needs.'

ANALYST'S NOTE. The full request divided by a single month's visits is not a meaningful annual cost-per-person figure. Dividing $4,000 by 67 yields approximately $59.70, but the numerator covers a year while the denominator covers only one month and counts visits rather than people. Presenting the quotient without these warnings would be misleading. A defensible writer should use comparable time windows and clearly name assumptions before making a budget-efficiency claim.

Explicit instruction

An argument needs a warrant: the principle connecting data to a conclusion. A claim can be plausible while its statistical example is invalid. Examine the year-versus-month mismatch and contrast a fair concession with a straw-man rebuttal.

Worked evidence analysis

Claim: A trial grant could fund immediate needs while improving the evidence base. Evidence: the review committee offers $2,000 over three months and requests transparent records. Warrant: short-term funding and evaluation can inform a later decision. Counterclaim: seasonal planting schedules may suffer under temporary funding. A responsible rebuttal addresses that concern rather than accusing gardeners of rejecting accountability.

Independent reasoning and writing

  1. Write a claim-evidence-warrant paragraph with one accurate direct quotation from the provided documents.
  2. Identify the denominator problem in the tempting $4,000 ÷ 67 calculation.
  3. Add a concession and rebuttal that fairly represent the other side, without inventing facts.

Reading self-check · Week 14

Why is $4,000 divided by 67 an invalid annual cost-per-person estimate?

Reveal the model response

The numerator is an annual request, whereas 67 is one month's visits rather than an annual count of distinct people. The time periods and units are not comparable.

Practice only. This does not record a grade or alter the weekly assessment.

Teacher guidance

Teach units and sampling as reading-comprehension skills. Avoid grading whether students favor a specific public policy; score evidence use and logical fairness.

WEEK 15 · INTEGRATED ORIGINAL READING

Public Hearing — An Ethical Debate Transcript

Complete fictional source set · offline readable · not a genuine municipal record

[Fictional council hearing. Chairs are arranged in a semicircle so speakers face each other rather than the audience. The moderator reads a rule: Each person must restate the prior speaker's strongest point before disagreeing.]

MODERATOR: Our question is whether to fund Maple Court Garden for a year, run a shorter trial, or request another plan. Please use facts from the working ledger and name any uncertainty. CHAIR: The garden is open now. Our students have assembled a soil observation notebook. That is real educational activity, though it does not tell us the number of households reached.

REVIEWER: I hear the chair saying that waiting has costs too: lost learning time and uncertain planting plans. I agree those are relevant. I still favor a time-limited grant because the ongoing maintenance figure has not been compared with other public commitments. CHAIR: I hear the reviewer asking for transparent trade-offs, not the closure of the garden. But the grant period should cover a complete growing season, or the evaluation could misrepresent what the project does.

STUDENT REPRESENTATIVE: Our notebook shows tasks we learned, but we cannot claim those results for every learner in town. What if the evaluation included work samples, total visits, accessibility checks, and an anonymous survey? A report could explain both what was measured and what was missing. MODERATOR: That suggestion distinguishes a learning portfolio from a population estimate. It also asks the council to decide in advance which outcomes matter.

MODERATOR: Before closing, each speaker will identify one concern that survived the discussion. CHAIR: Uncertain long-term funding. REVIEWER: An evaluation window too short for the project's real season. STUDENT: The risk of treating a number as complete evidence. [The moderator writes the three concerns publicly; the hearing ends before the final vote.]

Explicit instruction

Persuasive speaking includes accurate listening, usable sources, a fair summary of opposing ideas, and disciplined acknowledgment of limits. Discuss why listening notes can be evidence of reasoning even when a debate has no winner.

Worked evidence analysis

The reviewer's statement restates the chair's concern about delay before advocating a trial. The chair likewise distinguishes a request for evidence from a demand for closure. This is a model of intellectual fairness: disagreement persists, but each claim remains recognizable to its author.

Independent reasoning and writing

  1. Prepare a 90-second opening argument using two dossier details with proper time frames.
  2. Record the best objection to your own position, followed by a serious reply.
  3. Evaluate the student representative's proposal: which indicators measure activity, which measure access, and which remain uncertain?

Reading self-check · Week 15

Does the reviewer's request for evidence mean the reviewer wants to close the garden?

Reveal the model response

No. The reviewer acknowledges the garden's value and proposes an evaluation period. A fair debate must address that actual position rather than invent a stronger opposition.

Practice only. This does not record a grade or alter the weekly assessment.

Teacher guidance

Use roles, partner rehearsals, and an optional written alternative for speech access. Keep official debate evidence in the existing Week 15 workspace and grading process.

WEEK 16 · ORIGINAL FICTIONAL SOURCES

Two Reports, One Library — Expository Synthesis

Fictional classroom source set · not a real library report

FICTIONAL LIBRARY MEMO: The invented Stone Harbor Library tested a quieter study room for four weeks. A volunteer counted 120 booked hours of use in the new room and 90 booked hours in the older open area. Bookings were recorded by hour rather than by unique student, so a student who stayed for three hours contributed three booked hours. The memo described the trial as popular but did not measure whether participants learned more or whether the room excluded anyone who could not reserve online.

FICTIONAL STUDENT INTERVIEW: Nia says the reserved room helped her concentrate during a mathematics project. She also reports that her brother, who works during reservation hours, could rarely find a suitable slot. Another student describes the open area as noisy but welcoming to those who arrive without an appointment. These individual reports reveal experiences that booking counts cannot capture. Neither interview proves how most students felt, and the students' circumstances differ from each other.

FICTIONAL EDITOR'S NOTE: A school newsletter writes, “The new room was a success because students booked it 120 hours.” A careful reviewer asks what success means. High reservation numbers may indicate demand, but they cannot alone show improved comprehension, equitable access, or better learning. A synthesis should combine the numerical trend with accounts of benefits and barriers, keep each source's limitations visible, and recommend what to measure next rather than convert different kinds of evidence into one simplistic verdict.

Explicit instruction

Synthesis combines sources around an idea; it is not a paragraph summarizing document A followed by a paragraph summarizing document B. Track common points, differences in viewpoint, method, denominator and scope. Distinguish booked hours from unique users.

Worked evidence model

A model synthesis states that recorded room use shows substantial demand while interviews suggest uneven access. The count documents hours reserved, not a proven improvement in academic results. A good transition says 'Although the memo indicates demand, the interviews complicate what success means.'

Guided and independent writing

  1. Build a three-column matrix: shared question, source detail, limitation.
  2. Write 140–180 words organized by claim rather than by document, citing both source types.
  3. Propose one measurable outcome and one fairness measure for a future evaluation.

Misconception check: Source integration does not require pretending that a booking total and an interview mean the same thing.

Reading self-check · Week 16

Can 120 booked hours establish how many individual students used the room?

Reveal the model response

No. A student may book more than one hour; the number counts hours, not unique people.

Teacher guidance

Ask learners to identify the unit of analysis before writing. Use the existing synthesis rubric for the official task.

WEEK 17 · ORIGINAL FICTIONAL SOURCES

The Portfolio Revision — Style, Grammar and Attribution

Fictional classroom source set · not a real library report

JORDAN'S ORIGINAL PARAGRAPH: “The room was great it solved the problem in the school and students loved it. The library memo counted 120 hours; the interview proves the room worked, for everyone. This demonstrates the idea that all strategies works when they are put in place by people who are motivated.” Jordan has combined a run-on opening, an overstatement about all students, subject-verb disagreement, and a loose connection between the reported evidence and a sweeping conclusion.

THE ANNOTATED REVIEW: “Separate two independent clauses or connect them correctly. The memo counted booked hours; it did not measure learning. The interview describes one student's experience and one access problem, so avoid calling it proof of a universal result. Replace the vague language 'great' and 'the problem' with precise descriptions. Use an attribution phrase before reporting the memo so readers can distinguish the fictional source's statement from your own inference.” The reviewer does not ask Jordan to erase the original draft.

JORDAN'S REVISION: “During the four-week trial, the Stone Harbor Library recorded 120 booked hours in its new study room. Nia's interview suggests that the space supported her concentration, but her brother's experience raises a question about reservations and access. Together, the records justify studying demand and fairness before claiming that the room improved learning for everyone.” Jordan keeps a revision log: split the run-on, corrected agreement, removed unverified universals, changed generic adjectives to evidence-specific language, and clarified source attribution.

Explicit instruction

Proofreading corrects mechanics; substantive revision repairs reasoning, audience fit and evidentiary scope. A revision memo should explain what was changed and why. Accurate attribution improves ethics, especially when the source is fictional or teacher-provided.

Worked evidence model

Original: 'students loved it' → Revised: 'Nia reported better concentration, while another student described access barriers.' The revision improves not only grammar but also the scope of the claim and source transparency.

Guided and independent writing

  1. Diagnose at least four problems across syntax, grammar, source scope and precision.
  2. Rewrite Jordan's original paragraph without erasing legitimate uncertainty.
  3. Create a five-row change log linking each revision to clarity, evidence or audience.

Misconception check: A grammatically correct claim can still be unfair, unsupported or misleading.

Reading self-check · Week 17

Why is removing 'everyone' a content revision rather than merely a style edit?

Reveal the model response

Because the supplied sources do not represent every student; the universal claim is unsupported.

Teacher guidance

Score student revision against the existing writing rubric; the fictional sample is for practice. Offer before-and-after reading aloud.

WEEK 18 · ORIGINAL FICTIONAL SOURCES

The Midyear Conference — Portfolio Evidence and Transfer

Fictional classroom source set · not a real library report

FICTIONAL PORTFOLIO RECORD: Jo selects a September paragraph that describes a source as “accurate because it has statistics,” then compares it with a later synthesis that identifies the population counted, the period observed, and the unanswered questions. Jo's reading log shows three completed short nonfiction selections and one abandoned text, with an honest note that its vocabulary proved too difficult without support. Jo initially plans to submit only the strongest draft and hide the unsuccessful attempt.

A TEACHER CONFERENCE NOTE responds: “A portfolio is evidence of growth, not a trophy shelf. You may preserve your earliest effort and explain why you changed it. But do not claim to have mastered a skill merely because you revised one paragraph. Show that you can use it again with unfamiliar material.” Jo chooses two passages for a transfer exercise: a fictional newsletter claim based on hours booked and a personal account based on one student's experience. Jo identifies which conclusions each supports.

IN THE MIDYEAR REFLECTION, Jo explains three improvements: naming the denominator in a numerical claim, making source attribution clear, and replacing unsupported universal statements with qualified conclusions. Jo also lists two unfinished goals: checking primary-source provenance before relying on a report, and writing stronger counterarguments. The midterm remains a separate supervised assessment; the conference and reading exercise are preparation and portfolio evidence, not exam answers or automatic mastery credit.

Explicit instruction

Midyear reflection has two purposes: make growth visible and reveal remaining needs. Choose artifacts demonstrating instruction-to-revision transfer, not simply completion. A diagnostic or midterm may reveal what to reteach; practice self-checks do not replace its grade authority.

Worked evidence model

An evidence-based reflection says: 'In an early draft I equated numerical data with reliability. My later synthesis identifies what was counted and notes the missing access measure. My next goal is to distinguish correlation from a claim about learning outcomes.'

Guided and independent writing

  1. Prepare an annotated three-artifact timeline showing initial claim, revision and new transfer attempt.
  2. Write a 150-word midyear reflection with two verified gains and one clearly named limitation.
  3. Choose an unfamiliar paragraph from an approved source and identify its claim, evidence and scope before the midterm.

Misconception check: A reading dossier or self-check is not the official midterm test.

Reading self-check · Week 18

Does one successful revision prove that a skill will transfer to unfamiliar reading?

Reveal the model response

Not necessarily. Independent work with a new text provides stronger evidence of transfer.

Teacher guidance

Retain the existing course midterm and its evaluation. Review accessibility, accommodations, source records and student authorship in the original portfolio.
