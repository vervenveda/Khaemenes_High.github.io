Day 1 · Original short-short story

The Public Draft

Nadia wrote that the community garden had united the neighborhood. She remembered a crowded opening day, cheerful music, and children planting seeds. During interviews she learned that two residents worried about the garden's maintenance. She rewrote her conclusion: the project brought many neighbors together but left practical disagreements unresolved. The revision made space for evidence she had not wanted to see.

Close-reading question: Compare the two conclusions and explain why one is stronger.

Read now · Source A: The First Draft

Original Khaemenes Academy instructional passage · available offline on this page

When Imani opened her notebook, the first page was covered with revisions. An arrow crossed the margin; three sentences had been moved to the bottom; the title was no longer the one she had written on Monday. Her classmate called it messy. Imani looked again and noticed that every mark answered a question she had learned to ask.

On Monday, she had written that the town library was important because everyone said so. On Wednesday, she counted who actually used its tables: two students practicing a speech, a grandfather studying a bus schedule, and a parent helping a child sound out a word. Her second draft replaced the grand claim with those observations. By Friday, she could explain why the examples mattered, and also what they could not prove about the town as a whole.

She kept the first draft behind the revised one. It reminded her that an early answer could be sincere without being sufficiently supported. The pages did not measure her worth; together they showed how her reasoning had changed.

Worked model · Observation, inference, limitation

Observation: Imani replaces “everyone said so” with examples of people using the library. Supported inference: She learns to make a narrower claim based on evidence. Limitation: Three observations cannot establish how every resident uses the library.

Your evidence practice

  1. Identify one sentence revealing Imani's original claim and one revealing her revised reasoning.
  2. Explain how the author contrasts a broad assertion with observable evidence. Cite words from the passage.
  3. Write a two-sentence claim about academic growth. Include a limitation or uncertainty rather than overstating your conclusion.

Misconception check: Revision is not simply correcting spelling. Here, Imani revises the scope and support of her claim.

Preserve: Write answers in your existing weekly notes or portfolio; this reading does not introduce a new storage key, tracking mechanism, or assessment gate.

Independent Work

Create a five-term vocabulary record with definition, context sentence, connotation or word-part note, and an original application. Write an initial 6–8 sentence response to: “How do mature readers and writers establish a truthful starting point for growth?”

Evidence to Preserve

Vocabulary record, source preview notes, and initial response

Support and access options

Use sentence frames, a vocabulary bank, audio reading, chunked text, or teacher-selected background resources as needed.

Extension

Locate a second text or real-world example that complicates the essential question and explain the connection.

Day 2 · Original short-short story

The Unfinished Map

A map of the river district showed a footpath that ended abruptly at the water. Jonas called it an error, but an archivist showed him an older map on which a narrow bridge crossed that spot. The later map had been printed after the bridge was dismantled. What seemed like a mistake was an accurate record of change. Jonas added the date of each map before deciding what either could prove.

Close-reading question: Analyze how historical context changes the interpretation of a source.

Tuesday · Day 2

Close Reading & Evidence Lab

Objective: Apply close-reading routines to establish a baseline, organize course materials, and explain evidence-based reasoning.

Mini-Lesson

Model a two-pass reading: first for comprehension and structure, then for evidence, author choices, patterns, contradictions, and significance. Review the difference between quotation, paraphrase, inference, and commentary.

Assigned selection for this lab: Source A · The First Draft (embedded in Monday’s lesson above). Read it again, marking the original claim, three observed examples, the revised claim, and the explicit limitation. All evidence for this task is on this page; no external resource is required.

Guided Practice

Read or reread the assigned selection. Annotate at least eight purposeful details and classify each annotation by skill or standard. Use the week’s language focus—sentence boundaries, paragraph unity, and complete thoughts—when writing notes.

Independent Work

Complete an evidence ladder: observation → quotation/detail → paraphrase → inference → reasoning → significance. Identify the strongest and weakest evidence and justify the ranking.

Evidence to Preserve

Annotated text, evidence ladder, and one defensible inference

Support and access options

Reduce the text span, provide guided annotation symbols, pre-highlight one model passage, or allow oral rehearsal before writing.

Extension

Develop a plausible alternative interpretation and identify the evidence that would support or weaken it.

Day 3 · Original short-short story

Two Voices at Dawn

At sunrise, Priya described the train station as a beginning. Her grandfather called it a place of farewells. They stood on the same platform and watched the same train depart. Each memory arranged the morning differently: she noticed light on the windows, while he counted how long the empty track stayed silent. Neither description was false, yet neither exhausted the meaning of the scene.

Close-reading question: Interpret how detail and perspective shape different tones.

Annotate two precise details and preserve a written response in your existing lesson workspace.

Day 4 · Original short-short story

The Headline Problem

A school newspaper announced that a new study proved longer reading periods always improved grades. The study actually described one classroom over six weeks. Its author revised the headline to match the sample and included what had not been measured. The editor worried that fewer students would click the story, but the writer preferred a smaller claim readers could trust.

Close-reading question: Explain the difference between correlation and a universal causal claim.

Annotate two precise details and preserve a written response in your existing lesson workspace.

Thursday · Day 4

Discussion, Feedback & Revision

Objective: Use collaborative reasoning and targeted revision to improve clarity, accuracy, audience fit, and intellectual fairness.

Mini-Lesson

Practice the feedback protocol: affirm a strength, ask a genuine question, identify one evidence or reasoning gap, and recommend one precise revision. Review active listening, paraphrasing a peer, and respectful disagreement.

Guided Practice

Discuss the essential question using at least two pieces of evidence. Compare interpretations or approaches, then test each against the text, source quality, or task criteria.

Independent Work

Revise the Wednesday product. Complete a revision memo naming at least three changes, the reason for each change, and the effect on the reader or listener.

Evidence to Preserve

Discussion record, peer/teacher feedback, revised product, and revision memo

Support and access options

Allow written discussion, partner rehearsal, teacher conference, checklist-based feedback, or one focused revision target.

Extension

Write a brief response to the strongest counterinterpretation or counterargument raised during discussion.

Day 5 · Original short-short story

The Margin Letter

Mila received feedback that her essay sounded certain where the story itself remained ambiguous. She returned to the final scene, where a character waited beneath an unlit streetlamp. She revised 'He regretted leaving' to 'His waiting may suggest regret, but the narrative never confirms his reason.' Her argument became more careful without surrendering its interpretation.

Close-reading question: Evaluate the revised claim and supply one alternative reading.

Friday · Day 5

Mastery Check, Corrections & Portfolio

Objective: Demonstrate weekly mastery and determine whether to advance, revise, or repeat targeted practice.

Mini-Lesson

Review the week’s vocabulary, standards language, and one common misconception. Model how to correct an error by naming the skill, explaining the mistake, and showing the corrected reasoning.

Guided Practice

Complete a short retrieval warm-up, compare answers with notes, and identify one skill that needs deliberate attention before the quiz.

Independent Work

Take the weekly quiz. Complete corrections for missed items. File the strongest artifact in the portfolio and write a 3–4 sentence reflection connecting evidence of growth to the essential question.

Evidence to Preserve

Quiz score, corrections, portfolio artifact, and reflection

Support and access options

Permit extended time, read-aloud where appropriate, reduced visual load, or a second attempt after corrections.

Extension

Create one original quiz question with four choices, a definitive answer, and an explanation.
