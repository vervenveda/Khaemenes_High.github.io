Essential question: How can readers preserve source meaning while writing independently and honestly?

Text A · Original extended narrative

The Reporter and the Record

The reporter's notebook lay open beside the town bulletin. A line from a volunteer interview had been copied into an early article: “We planted more than vegetables; we planted a place to meet.” The sentence sounded so complete that Mira hesitated to interrupt it. Her draft repeated the line almost exactly, changed two words, and left off the speaker's name. A classmate liked the image and asked whether Mira had written it. Mira realized that admiration for a sentence did not make it hers.

She returned to her notes. The volunteer had been speaking about eight households that helped build a community garden. The school newsletter's headline, however, had claimed that the project lowered food costs for every family in town. In the report's table, six households listed lower weekly produce spending, one reported no change, and one had not supplied comparable records. There was no survey of families outside the volunteer group. The headline might have been intended as encouragement, but its universal wording exceeded the observations.

At the next meeting, two sets of minutes gave different reasons for the vote. The chair's summary emphasized neighborhood pride; a secretary's note described a debate about fencing, maintenance, and access. The vote itself appeared in both records. Mira drew separate columns for facts on which the accounts agreed, interpretations they offered, and questions they could not settle. She refused to manufacture a single motive simply to make the article more fluent.

Mira's revised article preserved the volunteer's distinctive sentence inside quotation marks and attributed it to the interview. In her own prose she explained that several volunteers described benefits, while the available records did not demonstrate a townwide reduction in costs. She also noted that the photograph of a crowded opening day showed attendance rather than agreement. Her article became less sweeping and more useful: readers could see the project, the evidence, and the boundaries between them.

Text B · Fictional source card

**Fictional research card — Community Garden Pilot, supplied for classroom practice only.** Participants: eight volunteer households. Comparable before/after weekly produce-spending records: seven households. Reported decrease: six; reported no change: one; missing comparable values: one. Recruitment: volunteers, not a random town sample. These counts describe records in a fictional exercise and do not establish costs for all households or the cause of any change.

**Fictional editorial note — Revised headline.** Original: “Garden Cuts Every Family's Expenses.” Revised: “Six Participating Households Report Lower Produce Spending.” The revised line identifies a source population and measurement rather than speaking for residents who were never surveyed. A thorough report would also explain how spending was recorded, possible seasonal effects, and whether any costs were omitted.

Teacher-modeled paragraph

Although the school newsletter celebrated the garden, the fictional records support a narrower description of its financial impact. Among eight participating households, only seven provided comparable spending records, and six of those seven reported lower produce costs. Because participation was voluntary and no other residents were surveyed, the available evidence cannot establish a result for every household. The volunteer's gardening metaphor expresses a social benefit, but the financial claim requires a different kind of evidence. The revision therefore strengthens the article by distinguishing voice, observation, and inference.

Five days of reading and instruction

Read the full source set before forming a conclusion. Each task returns to the original student workspace; no duplicate grades or local records are created here.

Monday · Quotation, paraphrase, or analysis?

Read paragraphs 1 and 4 of Text A. Underline the volunteer's distinctive metaphor. Copy it accurately into a quotation with a source label, then explain the interviewee's idea in your own different sentence structure. Finally add one analytical sentence about the metaphor's effect. In the existing Monday workspace, label those three acts separately.

Worked response: Quotation preserves the original wording: The volunteer said, “We planted more than vegetables; we planted a place to meet” (fictional interview reproduced in Text A). Paraphrase restates the idea: A garden volunteer regarded the project as a way to create social connections. Analysis explains the effect: The planting metaphor makes a shared space seem as important as the produce. A paraphrase still needs attribution.

Check: Does replacing two words in an original sentence create an independent paraphrase?

Reveal reasoning

No. It usually retains the source's distinctive syntax or phrasing; rebuild the explanation in independent language and cite the idea.

Tuesday · Summary and the missing denominator

Reread Text A paragraph 2 and Text B. Calculate how many of the eight volunteers had comparable records and how many of those reported a decrease. Distinguish 'six of seven with records' from 'six of eight participants.' Write a three-sentence summary that does not suggest any conclusion about all town residents. Use Tuesday's original evidence field.

Worked response: A measured description is: Of eight volunteer households, seven had comparable records, and six of those seven reported lower spending. Because all participants volunteered and no outside households were surveyed, these observations cannot establish a townwide result. The before/after comparison alone also cannot establish that the garden caused the change.

Check: Can the fictional study establish what happened to every family?

Reveal reasoning

No. It observes a nonrandom, small group with incomplete records; residents outside the group are not represented.

Return to saved Tuesday work

Wednesday · Two documents, one documented vote

Compare the two sets of meeting minutes described in Text A paragraph 3. Build a four-column table: shared fact, claim unique to record A, claim unique to record B, and the additional record you would need. Draft an inference paragraph that does not erase the disagreement. Save the reasoning table and paragraph in the Wednesday workspace.

Worked response: Both reports record a vote. The chair's summary foregrounds neighborhood pride, while the secretary's notes record debate over practical conditions. An evidence-led inference is that the meeting combined civic enthusiasm with unresolved operational concerns. Without additional testimony or a full record, the evidence does not justify attributing one motive to every voter.

Check: If two sources agree that an event occurred, do they necessarily agree on its cause?

Reveal reasoning

No. Agreement about the occurrence is different from agreement about why participants acted.

Return to saved Wednesday work

Thursday · Revision for meaning and fairness

Rewrite the original headline using accurate scope, then prepare two paragraphs comparing your first and revised versions. State what became more accurate and whether you lost any important information. Include one plausible charitable explanation for the original wording without claiming private motives. Keep both versions in Thursday's saved work.

Worked response: Overclaim: “The garden lowered every family's costs.” Revised: “Six volunteer households with comparable records reported lower produce spending.” Fair interpretation: The original writer may have hoped to celebrate the project, but intent is not established by the evidence. Revision narrows the claim and makes the sample visible.

Check: Is a qualified statement weaker simply because it claims less?

Reveal reasoning

Not necessarily. A smaller claim can be more credible when the evidence supports it precisely.

Return to saved Thursday work

Friday · Ethical synthesis and portfolio check

Produce a 200–300-word article or analysis using the story and fictional research card. Include one attributed quotation, a true paraphrase, one numeric statement with its denominator, one conflicting-source comparison, and one explicit limit. Save your final version in Friday's existing field; then take the existing weekly quiz separately.

Worked response: A defensible synthesis can credit a participant's description of communal benefit while reporting that six of seven volunteers with comparable records recorded spending decreases. It must note the eighth volunteer's missing comparison, the volunteer sample, and the lack of causal evidence. It should distinguish the photograph's record of attendance from any claim of unanimous opinion.

Check: Does citing a source make an overgeneralized conclusion valid?

Reveal reasoning

No. Attribution is necessary but not sufficient; inference must also remain within the data's scope.

Return to saved Friday work

Vocabulary in use

Quotation: source wording repeated accurately and attributed. Paraphrase: original phrasing and sentence construction with source credit. Summary: essential meaning without unsupported additions. Sample: observed participants, not automatically a population. Inference: interpretation beyond direct observation that must be warranted.

Misconception to correct

Citing a copied sentence does not automatically make it an independent paraphrase; making a claim narrower does not remove an observation. Review both attribution and whether the conclusion exceeds the available sample.

Teacher notes and differentiation

Foundation: read paragraphs in chunks; use three-column quotation / paraphrase / interpretation frames; underline the denominator before calculating. Core: write the five evidence products as assigned. Extended: evaluate possible self-selection bias and why before/after results are not proof of causation. Compare revision drafts rather than grading verbal confidence.

Formative review: text fidelity, attribution, precise denominator, fair disagreement, and meaningful revision. The course's original quiz and teacher-scored products remain the only assessment authorities.
