Reading purpose and source integrity

Text A and Text B are original fictional instructional works created for close reading. They do not describe an actual school, travel route, survey, or safety finding. The shorter story already inside the Week 02 classroom, The Unfinished Map, remains the introductory model.

Core question: How does a reader distinguish what a document observes from what its language invites the public to infer?

Text A · Original short fiction

A Route Drawn in Blue

The corridor beside the river had two names. Students called it the Footpath; the transportation committee called it an unassessed connection. From her apartment window, Amara could see the bridge, its railings, and the people who crossed it to reach the school. The committee's blue map did not show it. At the bottom of the map, a caption announced that the illustrated routes were safe for everyone.

The Monday after the map appeared, Amara brought an annotated copy to the student council. She had circled the bridge's absence and underlined the word everyone. She did not write that the map was false. Instead, she asked which routes had been examined, when the examination happened, and what the words safe and everyone meant in the committee's method.

The council secretary found a list of surveyed streets. The survey had covered roads used by school buses during daylight hours. The list did not include a pedestrian bridge. The secretary said the bridge had been excluded because the team lacked a walking-route inspection, not because inspectors had found the bridge dangerous. Amara wrote that distinction beside her first annotation.

A second student, Malik, remembered the bridge closing briefly after a storm the previous year. He offered his memory as a question, not as a dated inspection report. Someone else claimed that a repaired railing proved the whole route safe. The council agreed that neither recollection nor repair alone answered the present safety question. They needed a current assessment by the appropriate people.

By Friday the revised map still contained only the surveyed roads. Its new caption read: 'The marked routes were examined by the bus team during the current survey period. Pedestrian connections not shown have not been assessed through this process.' Amara copied both captions into her journal. The second gave readers less reassurance, but more information about what the evidence could actually support.

She ended her journal entry with a question: 'When does a limited report become misleading—not because its data are wrong, but because its wording reaches farther than its method?'

Reading lens: plot perspective, qualifiers, source date, and claim scope.

Text B · Fictional public-information record

What the Survey Actually Covered

FIELD NOTE — SIMULATED DOCUMENT FOR CLOSE READING. Prepared by the fictional School Route Committee. This teaching document is not a real safety inspection and is not advice about where to travel.

Purpose: identify roads used by the morning school-bus route. Method: bus-team members recorded road names and visited the marked vehicle approaches in daylight. Dates: an illustrative two-day period. Exclusions: footpaths, pedestrian bridges, evening conditions, and routes beyond bus access were outside the survey scope.

Claim permitted by this note: the listed bus-access roads were visited under the specified conditions. Claim not permitted: every pedestrian route to school was assessed, or any excluded route was found safe or unsafe.

Revision required: replace the caption 'Safe routes for everyone' with wording that states the limited purpose and conditions. Follow-up recommended: ask an authorized pedestrian-route team to evaluate unassessed paths before making new safety statements.

Document limitation: this simulated note contains no hazard ratings, instrument records, or outcome measurements. Readers cannot infer actual safety from it.

Reading lens: audience, methodology, omitted populations, and uncertainty.

Model: observation → warranted inference

Observation: The survey included roads used by school buses in daylight and explicitly excluded pedestrian connections.

Supported inference: Its evidence cannot establish whether the footbridge was safe for pedestrians.

Unsupported inference: The missing bridge must be unsafe, or the committee intentionally hid a danger.

Reasoning: An omitted case falls outside the survey's stated method; exclusion is not a positive safety or danger finding.

Vocabulary in context

Quantifier: a word such as every that establishes a claim's breadth.

Scope: what a method or document actually covers.

Inference: a conclusion supported by evidence, not directly quoted as fact.

Qualification: wording that limits a claim to justified conditions.

Misconception and check

Mistake: assuming an omitted route is necessarily dangerous.

What is the strongest conclusion permitted by the fictional field note?

Show model answer

The note establishes only that the listed bus roads were visited under specified conditions. It does not establish the safety of excluded pedestrian routes.

Teacher's reasoning guide

Use paired annotation colors: one for factual method statements, one for interpretive claims. Require students to distinguish the fictional narrator's knowledge from the committee's record. For additional support, read Text A in three segments, provide a claim/evidence/limitation frame, and allow an oral first draft. For extension, ask how an authorized survey could responsibly broaden its scope.

Formative rubric (not an official score): Accurate text comprehension; precise citations to both sources; inference linked to scope; fair consideration of an alternative; explicitly stated limitation.
