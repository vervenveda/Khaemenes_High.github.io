WEEK 13 · INTEGRATED ORIGINAL READING

The Garden Question — Two Competing Editorials

Complete fictional source set · offline readable · not a genuine municipal record

EDITORIAL A · The Garden Is Already a Classroom. The Maple Court Garden occupies a vacant public lot. According to a sign-in notebook kept by volunteers, 67 visits were recorded last month; some visitors came more than once. Neighbors grew beans and herbs, and two teachers brought small groups to observe insects and measure soil moisture. A council member now proposes keeping the garden open with a modest annual grant. Those who ask for more documentation are not wrong to ask questions. But a project should not be invisible simply because its work is quiet.

The garden association's volunteer chair argues that the site builds skills no classroom can fully imitate: patience, cooperation, attention to seasons, and care for shared property. She points to observations made by students and asks the council to consider the value of a public place not measured solely by ticket sales. Her conclusion, however, extends beyond her evidence when she says that every family benefits. The notebook records visits, not distinct households, and no systematic neighborhood survey is available.

EDITORIAL B · Accountability Is Not Opposition. A local budget reviewer agrees that the garden matters. Yet he asks how many unique people use it, what annual maintenance will cost, whether water access is secure, and how other public projects will be compared. A visit total can rise when the same few volunteers attend repeatedly. He does not claim the garden lacks value. He argues that funding decisions require information about opportunity cost and equitable access.

The reviewer proposes a three-month trial grant, a public expenditure ledger, and anonymous weekly visitor counts that distinguish total visits from returning visitors without collecting names. The volunteer chair welcomes a trial but worries that a temporary arrangement will discourage planting for the next season. Both writers claim to support community learning. They disagree about what evidence is sufficient before money is committed.

Explicit instruction

Distinguish each editorial's claim, reasons, evidence, warrant, and hidden uncertainty. Do not flatten a conditional funding proposal into a claim that the garden should close. Both essays are invented classroom sources and are not records of real local government.

Worked evidence analysis

The first writer's observation notebook supports the claim that the garden is being used. It does not prove universal benefit. The second writer's call for unique visitor counts is an evidentiary critique, not opposition to gardens. A fair comparison identifies common values before evaluating competing policies.

Independent reasoning and writing

  1. Construct a two-column argument matrix with a fair four-sentence summary of each editorial.
  2. Explain why '67 visits' cannot automatically be read as '67 distinct residents.'
  3. Write a tentative judgment specifying what further evidence would change it.
Teacher guidance

Invite readers to steelman each view before choosing a side. Evaluate whether sources support the breadth of each claim, not merely whether the reader agrees.

WEEK 14 · INTEGRATED ORIGINAL READING

The Budget Ledger — Claims, Counterclaims and Warrants

Complete fictional source set · offline readable · not a genuine municipal record

DOCUMENT 1 · Working budget for Maple Court Garden, fictional. The garden seeks $4,000 in annual support. Proposed costs: water and repairs, $1,600; materials and accessibility improvements, $1,400; educational supplies, $1,000. Last year's volunteer sign-in recorded 67 visits in one month, without checking whether the same individuals visited more than once. An alternative proposal would provide $2,000 for a three-month evaluation period. Neither proposal includes a full comparison with other town projects.

DOCUMENT 2 · A letter from the garden association. 'If the council waits for perfect evidence, smaller projects will never receive help. The children who worked here last spring cannot put their learning on hold until a formal survey arrives. We ask for the full grant, transparent purchasing, and a student-designed impact report. The council can support a living classroom while still requesting better records.'

DOCUMENT 3 · The review committee's reply. 'We have not concluded that the garden lacks educational value. Our concern is that a full recurring grant obligates future budgets. The trial proposal would fund immediate maintenance and collect evidence about use and access. We will reconsider ongoing support when costs and benefits can be compared more carefully with other needs.'

ANALYST'S NOTE. The full request divided by a single month's visits is not a meaningful annual cost-per-person figure. Dividing $4,000 by 67 yields approximately $59.70, but the numerator covers a year while the denominator covers only one month and counts visits rather than people. Presenting the quotient without these warnings would be misleading. A defensible writer should use comparable time windows and clearly name assumptions before making a budget-efficiency claim.

Explicit instruction

An argument needs a warrant: the principle connecting data to a conclusion. A claim can be plausible while its statistical example is invalid. Examine the year-versus-month mismatch and contrast a fair concession with a straw-man rebuttal.

Worked evidence analysis

Claim: A trial grant could fund immediate needs while improving the evidence base. Evidence: the review committee offers $2,000 over three months and requests transparent records. Warrant: short-term funding and evaluation can inform a later decision. Counterclaim: seasonal planting schedules may suffer under temporary funding. A responsible rebuttal addresses that concern rather than accusing gardeners of rejecting accountability.

Independent reasoning and writing

  1. Write a claim-evidence-warrant paragraph with one accurate direct quotation from the provided documents.
  2. Identify the denominator problem in the tempting $4,000 ÷ 67 calculation.
  3. Add a concession and rebuttal that fairly represent the other side, without inventing facts.
Teacher guidance

Teach units and sampling as reading-comprehension skills. Avoid grading whether students favor a specific public policy; score evidence use and logical fairness.

WEEK 15 · INTEGRATED ORIGINAL READING

Public Hearing — An Ethical Debate Transcript

Complete fictional source set · offline readable · not a genuine municipal record

[Fictional council hearing. Chairs are arranged in a semicircle so speakers face each other rather than the audience. The moderator reads a rule: Each person must restate the prior speaker's strongest point before disagreeing.]

MODERATOR: Our question is whether to fund Maple Court Garden for a year, run a shorter trial, or request another plan. Please use facts from the working ledger and name any uncertainty. CHAIR: The garden is open now. Our students have assembled a soil observation notebook. That is real educational activity, though it does not tell us the number of households reached.

REVIEWER: I hear the chair saying that waiting has costs too: lost learning time and uncertain planting plans. I agree those are relevant. I still favor a time-limited grant because the ongoing maintenance figure has not been compared with other public commitments. CHAIR: I hear the reviewer asking for transparent trade-offs, not the closure of the garden. But the grant period should cover a complete growing season, or the evaluation could misrepresent what the project does.

STUDENT REPRESENTATIVE: Our notebook shows tasks we learned, but we cannot claim those results for every learner in town. What if the evaluation included work samples, total visits, accessibility checks, and an anonymous survey? A report could explain both what was measured and what was missing. MODERATOR: That suggestion distinguishes a learning portfolio from a population estimate. It also asks the council to decide in advance which outcomes matter.

MODERATOR: Before closing, each speaker will identify one concern that survived the discussion. CHAIR: Uncertain long-term funding. REVIEWER: An evaluation window too short for the project's real season. STUDENT: The risk of treating a number as complete evidence. [The moderator writes the three concerns publicly; the hearing ends before the final vote.]

Explicit instruction

Persuasive speaking includes accurate listening, usable sources, a fair summary of opposing ideas, and disciplined acknowledgment of limits. Discuss why listening notes can be evidence of reasoning even when a debate has no winner.

Worked evidence analysis

The reviewer's statement restates the chair's concern about delay before advocating a trial. The chair likewise distinguishes a request for evidence from a demand for closure. This is a model of intellectual fairness: disagreement persists, but each claim remains recognizable to its author.

Independent reasoning and writing

  1. Prepare a 90-second opening argument using two dossier details with proper time frames.
  2. Record the best objection to your own position, followed by a serious reply.
  3. Evaluate the student representative's proposal: which indicators measure activity, which measure access, and which remain uncertain?
Teacher guidance

Use roles, partner rehearsals, and an optional written alternative for speech access. Keep official debate evidence in the existing Week 15 workspace and grading process.
