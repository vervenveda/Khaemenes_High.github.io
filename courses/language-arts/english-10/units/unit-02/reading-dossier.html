WEEK 04 · UNIT 02

Literary Elements & Layers of Meaning

Original practice fiction · The Last Bus Map

Nia kept the old bus map folded behind her phone case even after the city changed every route. Her mother called it clutter. Nia called it proof that she had once known how to get anywhere alone. On the first day of the new schedule she missed her stop, walked three blocks in rain, and almost threw the map away. Instead she wrote the new route over the old lines in pencil.

Worked reading

Setting and object details can carry layered meaning without becoming fixed symbols. The obsolete map first represents competence, then inadequacy, then a record that can be revised. A strong interpretation traces that change rather than announcing ‘the map symbolizes growing up.’

Independent task

With one approved authentic literary text, identify setting, conflict, point of view and one recurring detail. Record exact text locator and explain how the detail changes meaning across at least two moments.

WEEK 05 · UNIT 02

Characterization, Conflict & Coming-of-Age

Original practice scene · The Receipt

After her first shift, Marisol counted the register twice and found the missing six dollars in a stack of coupons. Her supervisor had already written an incident note. She could quietly fix the drawer before he returned. Instead she placed the money beside the note and added one sentence: “I found it after I said I had checked everywhere.”

Character model

Coming-of-age is not simply ‘becoming mature.’ Track competing motives, pressure, self-knowledge, consequence and changed behavior. Indirect characterization often appears in what a character does when no audience would know.

Independent task

Create a character-growth chart for an approved authentic story or memoir excerpt. Use at least three evidence points, one internal/external conflict, and one detail that complicates a neat growth narrative.

WEEK 06 · UNIT 02

Conflicting Perspectives & Point of View

Paired practice voices

VOICE A: “The mural made the empty wall belong to everyone.” VOICE B: “The mural decided what the wall meant before anyone asked the shop owners who see it every day.” Both speakers describe the same neighborhood project but define ‘belonging’ differently.

Perspective model

Point of view shapes selection, emphasis, certainty and omission. Comparing perspectives is not choosing which speaker is ‘good.’ Ask what each can know, what each values, what evidence each supplies, and what remains outside both accounts.

Independent task

Pair the practice voices with an approved text containing conflicting values or viewpoints. Build a comparison table for knowledge, motive, diction, omission and reliability; then draft a paragraph that distinguishes perspective from factual accuracy.
