WEEK 16 · ORIGINAL FICTIONAL SOURCES

Two Reports, One Library — Expository Synthesis

Fictional classroom source set · not a real library report

FICTIONAL LIBRARY MEMO: The invented Stone Harbor Library tested a quieter study room for four weeks. A volunteer counted 120 booked hours of use in the new room and 90 booked hours in the older open area. Bookings were recorded by hour rather than by unique student, so a student who stayed for three hours contributed three booked hours. The memo described the trial as popular but did not measure whether participants learned more or whether the room excluded anyone who could not reserve online.

FICTIONAL STUDENT INTERVIEW: Nia says the reserved room helped her concentrate during a mathematics project. She also reports that her brother, who works during reservation hours, could rarely find a suitable slot. Another student describes the open area as noisy but welcoming to those who arrive without an appointment. These individual reports reveal experiences that booking counts cannot capture. Neither interview proves how most students felt, and the students' circumstances differ from each other.

FICTIONAL EDITOR'S NOTE: A school newsletter writes, “The new room was a success because students booked it 120 hours.” A careful reviewer asks what success means. High reservation numbers may indicate demand, but they cannot alone show improved comprehension, equitable access, or better learning. A synthesis should combine the numerical trend with accounts of benefits and barriers, keep each source's limitations visible, and recommend what to measure next rather than convert different kinds of evidence into one simplistic verdict.

Explicit instruction

Synthesis combines sources around an idea; it is not a paragraph summarizing document A followed by a paragraph summarizing document B. Track common points, differences in viewpoint, method, denominator and scope. Distinguish booked hours from unique users.

Worked evidence model

A model synthesis states that recorded room use shows substantial demand while interviews suggest uneven access. The count documents hours reserved, not a proven improvement in academic results. A good transition says 'Although the memo indicates demand, the interviews complicate what success means.'

Guided and independent writing

  1. Build a three-column matrix: shared question, source detail, limitation.
  2. Write 140–180 words organized by claim rather than by document, citing both source types.
  3. Propose one measurable outcome and one fairness measure for a future evaluation.

Misconception check: Source integration does not require pretending that a booking total and an interview mean the same thing.

Reading self-check · Week 16

Can 120 booked hours establish how many individual students used the room?

Reveal the model response

No. A student may book more than one hour; the number counts hours, not unique people.

Teacher guidance

Ask learners to identify the unit of analysis before writing. Use the existing synthesis rubric for the official task.

WEEK 17 · ORIGINAL FICTIONAL SOURCES

The Portfolio Revision — Style, Grammar and Attribution

Fictional classroom source set · not a real library report

JORDAN'S ORIGINAL PARAGRAPH: “The room was great it solved the problem in the school and students loved it. The library memo counted 120 hours; the interview proves the room worked, for everyone. This demonstrates the idea that all strategies works when they are put in place by people who are motivated.” Jordan has combined a run-on opening, an overstatement about all students, subject-verb disagreement, and a loose connection between the reported evidence and a sweeping conclusion.

THE ANNOTATED REVIEW: “Separate two independent clauses or connect them correctly. The memo counted booked hours; it did not measure learning. The interview describes one student's experience and one access problem, so avoid calling it proof of a universal result. Replace the vague language 'great' and 'the problem' with precise descriptions. Use an attribution phrase before reporting the memo so readers can distinguish the fictional source's statement from your own inference.” The reviewer does not ask Jordan to erase the original draft.

JORDAN'S REVISION: “During the four-week trial, the Stone Harbor Library recorded 120 booked hours in its new study room. Nia's interview suggests that the space supported her concentration, but her brother's experience raises a question about reservations and access. Together, the records justify studying demand and fairness before claiming that the room improved learning for everyone.” Jordan keeps a revision log: split the run-on, corrected agreement, removed unverified universals, changed generic adjectives to evidence-specific language, and clarified source attribution.

Explicit instruction

Proofreading corrects mechanics; substantive revision repairs reasoning, audience fit and evidentiary scope. A revision memo should explain what was changed and why. Accurate attribution improves ethics, especially when the source is fictional or teacher-provided.

Worked evidence model

Original: 'students loved it' → Revised: 'Nia reported better concentration, while another student described access barriers.' The revision improves not only grammar but also the scope of the claim and source transparency.

Guided and independent writing

  1. Diagnose at least four problems across syntax, grammar, source scope and precision.
  2. Rewrite Jordan's original paragraph without erasing legitimate uncertainty.
  3. Create a five-row change log linking each revision to clarity, evidence or audience.

Misconception check: A grammatically correct claim can still be unfair, unsupported or misleading.

Reading self-check · Week 17

Why is removing 'everyone' a content revision rather than merely a style edit?

Reveal the model response

Because the supplied sources do not represent every student; the universal claim is unsupported.

Teacher guidance

Score student revision against the existing writing rubric; the fictional sample is for practice. Offer before-and-after reading aloud.

WEEK 18 · ORIGINAL FICTIONAL SOURCES

The Midyear Conference — Portfolio Evidence and Transfer

Fictional classroom source set · not a real library report

FICTIONAL PORTFOLIO RECORD: Jo selects a September paragraph that describes a source as “accurate because it has statistics,” then compares it with a later synthesis that identifies the population counted, the period observed, and the unanswered questions. Jo's reading log shows three completed short nonfiction selections and one abandoned text, with an honest note that its vocabulary proved too difficult without support. Jo initially plans to submit only the strongest draft and hide the unsuccessful attempt.

A TEACHER CONFERENCE NOTE responds: “A portfolio is evidence of growth, not a trophy shelf. You may preserve your earliest effort and explain why you changed it. But do not claim to have mastered a skill merely because you revised one paragraph. Show that you can use it again with unfamiliar material.” Jo chooses two passages for a transfer exercise: a fictional newsletter claim based on hours booked and a personal account based on one student's experience. Jo identifies which conclusions each supports.

IN THE MIDYEAR REFLECTION, Jo explains three improvements: naming the denominator in a numerical claim, making source attribution clear, and replacing unsupported universal statements with qualified conclusions. Jo also lists two unfinished goals: checking primary-source provenance before relying on a report, and writing stronger counterarguments. The midterm remains a separate supervised assessment; the conference and reading exercise are preparation and portfolio evidence, not exam answers or automatic mastery credit.

Explicit instruction

Midyear reflection has two purposes: make growth visible and reveal remaining needs. Choose artifacts demonstrating instruction-to-revision transfer, not simply completion. A diagnostic or midterm may reveal what to reteach; practice self-checks do not replace its grade authority.

Worked evidence model

An evidence-based reflection says: 'In an early draft I equated numerical data with reliability. My later synthesis identifies what was counted and notes the missing access measure. My next goal is to distinguish correlation from a claim about learning outcomes.'

Guided and independent writing

  1. Prepare an annotated three-artifact timeline showing initial claim, revision and new transfer attempt.
  2. Write a 150-word midyear reflection with two verified gains and one clearly named limitation.
  3. Choose an unfamiliar paragraph from an approved source and identify its claim, evidence and scope before the midterm.

Misconception check: A reading dossier or self-check is not the official midterm test.

Reading self-check · Week 18

Does one successful revision prove that a skill will transfer to unfamiliar reading?

Reveal the model response

Not necessarily. Independent work with a new text provides stronger evidence of transfer.

Teacher guidance

Retain the existing course midterm and its evaluation. Review accessibility, accommodations, source records and student authorship in the original portfolio.
