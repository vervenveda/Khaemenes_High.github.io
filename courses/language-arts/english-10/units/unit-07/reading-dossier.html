WEEK 19 · ORIGINAL ACADEMY POETRY

Whose Rain Is This?

Original instructional poems, not attributed to historical poets

Poem A · The Weather Ledger

At six, I counted windows lit, Not all the homes the rain had found. A shopkeeper dragged his sign indoors; The bus kept making borrowed sound. I wrote down every passing coat, The faces hidden in their hoods. At noon I saw my own reflection In puddles where the pavement stood. The page said twelve; the street said more. I closed the book against the rain. What numbers hold, a number holds; What passes through, comes back as stain.

Poem B · After the Last Bus

The stop has kept a silver stripe Where one wet wheel ran past the curb. A folded map lies in the grate; The wind turns pages without words. I almost call the road a promise, Then watch the signal change to red. Some doors are bright across the block, Some names are only in my head. If I say “home,” does that mean where The lights stay on beyond the square? The rain cannot decide for me. It leaves the question in the air.

Explicit poetry instruction

A lyric speaker is the voice constructed by the poem; it should not be equated automatically with the author. Ambiguity occurs when multiple interpretations remain defensible. In “The Weather Ledger,” the speaker catalogs visible details but recognizes limits in counting. In “After the Last Bus,” uncertainty turns on the word “home” and the promise implied by a road. Identify what the speaker perceives, what they infer, and what remains unsaid. A sound pattern or image supports an interpretation only when the quoted words and their placement actually produce that effect.

Worked comparative reading

Read “The page said twelve; the street said more” as a contrast between recorded observation and unmeasured experience. This does not prove the speaker is a professional statistician or describe a particular real town. In the second poem, “If I say ‘home,’ does that mean where / The lights stay on” places a question across a line break, leaving belonging unresolved. A defensible claim is that both speakers doubt whether visible evidence can settle a personal question, although one uses counting and the other uses an unanswered question.

Guided and independent writing

Mark lines where the speaker speaks about evidence versus feeling. Identify one image that admits more than one credible reading and explain the textual clues supporting each interpretation.

  1. Annotate two line references from each poem and separate observation, inference, and uncertainty.
  2. Write a comparative 140–180-word paragraph with a precise claim and one counterreading.
  3. Compose four original lines from a speaker whose knowledge is intentionally limited; explain how readers know what remains uncertain.

Misconception and close-reading self-check

A poem's “I” is not automatically its historical author, and ambiguity is not permission for interpretations unsupported by textual detail.

What makes the word “home” genuinely ambiguous in Poem B?

The speaker asks whether home is a particular place or a relationship of security and belonging. The poem names lights, names, and unanswered questions without choosing one meaning.

Teacher guidance

Model speaker/author distinction first. Ask students to cite exact lines, not presumed biography. Additional authentic poetry remains part of the existing unit reading.

WEEK 20 · ORIGINAL ACADEMY POETRY

The Light That Changes Its Meaning

Original instructional poems, not attributed to historical poets

Poem A · The Glass River

Above the shops the windows burn, Small lanterns in a patient row. Below, the gutter takes their fire And folds it into water's flow. A bicycle releases dusk; A bell forgets its proper hour. One window goes from gold to gray As clouds lean close above the tower. The street is not a river, yet Its moving lights persuade my eye. I call the evening generous; Then hear the doors close one by one.

Poem B · Night Window

The yellow square across the lane Was summer when I first looked out. It held a plant, a book, a chair, A lamp no wind could blow about. But autumn thickened every pane; The same small square became a sign That someone else could sit inside While I stood waiting for a line. No glass had moved. No color changed. The distance did, because I learned A shining room can welcome one And leave another on the curb.

Explicit poetry instruction

Figurative language creates relationships between unlike things. A metaphor can make reflections look like fire or water without asserting literal transformation. Mood is the emotional atmosphere suggested by diction, imagery, sound, syntax, and pacing; connotation concerns associations carried by words. The first poem changes from generous light to closing doors. The second explicitly examines how the same image is differently experienced over time. A reader should trace the shift rather than assign one permanent mood label to every stanza.

Worked comparative reading

In Poem A, “the gutter takes their fire / And folds it into water's flow” recasts reflected light as fire; the delayed verb “folds” lends a gentle movement. But “hear the doors close one by one” introduces possible exclusion or ending. Poem B's “The distance did” answers its claim that neither glass nor color changed: the altered relationship to the room changes the image's meaning. Compare how both poems turn bright windows from comfort toward isolation, while their speakers reach that shift by different routes.

Guided and independent writing

Underline two positive connotations and two details that disturb them. What has literally changed in each poem, and what has changed in the speaker's interpretation?

  1. Make a four-column table: figurative phrase, literal referent, connotation, effect on tone.
  2. Explain one shift in mood in 120–160 words, quoting before and after the shift.
  3. Rewrite three lines with neutral diction, then explain which emotional nuances are weakened or lost.

Misconception and close-reading self-check

Mood is not simply the reader's personal feeling; justify a mood claim with diction, image, sound, and structure.

Does “the windows burn” mean that the buildings are literally on fire?

No. In context it describes illuminated windows metaphorically; later reflection imagery confirms the emphasis is on light and perception.

Teacher guidance

Ask learners to track image changes in two colors. Require literal versus figurative readings to be distinguished before interpreting effect.

WEEK 21 · ORIGINAL ACADEMY POETRY

Two Ways to Measure a Day

Original instructional poems, not attributed to historical poets

Poem A · Tide Chart

First light: the pier is almost bare. By nine, three boats lean into air. At twelve, the ropes grow taut with rain; At three, the gulls return again. I mark the hours in measured ink And leave a blank where shadows sink. At dusk, the water takes the stairs. No ruled blue line can say who cares. I fold the chart at closing time; The last two waves refuse to rhyme.

Poem B · Clock Tower

The tower holds its breath. A minute crosses the square. Another waits beside a shoe, untied; its owner runs with no time left to spare. Somewhere a bell arrives too late for the person who needed it.

Explicit poetry instruction

Structure includes lineation, stanza grouping, pacing, repetition, sound and visible shape. Poem A initially pairs end-rhymes and repeated time markers, suggesting an orderly ledger; its final couplet breaks that expectation by explicitly resisting rhyme. Poem B uses short, uneven lines and enjambment to slow and accelerate the reader. Rather than calling one form “better,” ask which formal choices enact each poem's ideas about time and human experience. In comparing poems, organize by a shared problem and distinct technique rather than summarizing one whole poem after another.

Worked comparative reading

A model thesis: “Both poems question whether measured time captures lived experience, but ‘Tide Chart’ uses regular time stamps and a disrupted closing rhyme to reveal the limits of order, whereas ‘Clock Tower’ uses broken lines and delayed syntax to make a minute feel unstable.” Evidence for the first technique includes “First light,” “By nine,” and “At twelve.” Evidence for the second includes the separation of “A minute / crosses the square” and the interruption “untied,” which creates a pause inside the motion.

Guided and independent writing

Tap out where the poem invites a pause. Which pauses follow punctuation, and which are caused by the end of a line? Identify one pattern and one departure from that pattern.

  1. Annotate at least two examples of line breaks, one of rhyme or near rhyme, and one example of delayed syntax.
  2. Create a comparative paragraph with a shared theme, two formal contrasts, and quoted line-level evidence.
  3. Perform each poem aloud twice—one reading honoring line endings, one honoring syntax—and explain changes in meaning or emphasis.

Misconception and close-reading self-check

Short lines are not automatically free verse and regular rhyme is not automatically simple; describe the actual pattern before interpreting it.

What is the effect of placing “untied” on its own line in Poem B?

It interrupts the running action, briefly foregrounding a small detail that changes the rhythm and suggests time pressure and vulnerability.

Teacher guidance

Invite accessible alternatives to oral performance, such as a marked reading script. Keep teacher assessment of comparative analysis within the existing weekly classroom.
