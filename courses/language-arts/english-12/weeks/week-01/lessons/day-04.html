Attrition, Research Claims, and Statistical Honesty

Revise an overgeneralized research claim after identifying missing outcome data and the risk of selection bias.

Weekly Classroom & Saved WorkCourse HomePrevious DayNext Day

Read the original passage

A research summary claimed a new program improved literacy for every participant. A footnote showed that several students had not completed the final assessment. The author changed the abstract to describe outcomes only for those with recorded results and added an attrition limitation. The claim became narrower, but the research became more useful to readers deciding what to investigate next.

Annotate at least two phrases. Distinguish facts within the story from unavailable source documents.

Explicit instruction

Attrition occurs when some participants do not contribute final measurements. “Everyone improved” requires outcome evidence from every participant, not merely those who completed a test. Even among completers, an improvement claim depends on baseline and follow-up measurements and their comparability. Missing results may be systematically different from observed results. Clearly report the observed sample size, who is excluded from the analysis, and plausible effects on the conclusion.

Vocabulary: attrition; denominator; sample; causal inference.

Worked reasoning model

Illustrative numbers: 20 participants enrolled; 16 completed the final assessment; 12 of those 16 improved. The supported description is “12 of 16 assessed participants improved.” It is not “60% of all participants benefited,” because four final outcomes are unknown and improvement is not equivalent to program-caused benefit. The numbers are teaching examples, not results from the fictional study.

Guided practice

If twelve of sixteen completers improved, what fraction of completers improved? Why is twelve of twenty a different denominator with a different interpretation?

Independent evidence

  1. Rewrite the original absolute abstract in a way that states who was measured and what evidence exists.
  2. Draw a participation flow: enrolled, followed up, missing, analyzed, using hypothetical sample numbers.
  3. Propose two ways missing follow-up data could change a conclusion, without guessing why people left.

Misconception check

Replacing an unsupported universal claim with a precise denominator is increased rigor, not failure.

Low-stakes concept check

Does an improvement rate among completers prove the intervention caused improvement?

Reveal model response

No. Causality requires appropriate comparison and control of confounding as well as a clearly specified outcome.

Unscored support; does not modify any assessment or completion records.

Teacher notes

Model the denominator explicitly. Foundation: practice part/whole language. Extended: compare complete-case results with best-case and worst-case bounds.
