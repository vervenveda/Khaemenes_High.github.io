Source status and central inquiry

Keep the Week 02 short stories and the Unit 01 senior reading as parallel foundational resources; these longer passages develop high-complexity reading and evaluation.

Inquiry: How can an official source be authentic and precise within its scope, yet inadequate for making claims about broad public experience or consent?

Text A · Original extended literary nonfiction-style narrative

A Label Revised in Pencil

At the end of the winter term, the city archive displayed a memorandum beneath a glass case. Its printed caption called the page 'the definitive record of the waterfront agreement.' The memorandum set out the boundaries of a public square and the hours when stalls could be installed there. It carried the signatures of six officials and one merchants' representative. On its face, the page was precise, dated, and formally approved. It was also incomplete as an account of everyone who used the square.

Sana, a senior student building a documentary essay, was asked to write the exhibit's introductory paragraph. She began with a sentence she soon distrusted: 'The agreement settled the conflict.' A signed text might record an institutional settlement; it could not establish that every conflict had ended. She marked the difference between a legal action and an experienced consequence in the margin.

She interviewed Mara, whose family had sold fruit on the square for generations. Mara remembered the agreement as a relief because the new hours made trading predictable. Sana also spoke with Idris, who recalled that some deliveries had become more expensive after vehicle access was restricted. Both spoke from memory, years after the decision. Their accounts were not transcripts from the original meeting, and neither could represent everyone affected. Still, each asked a question the memorandum alone had not answered.

The archive contained a typed abstract prepared two decades later. It described the agreement as 'widely welcomed.' A footnote attributed that judgment to a survey, but the survey pages were missing from the folder. Sana searched the accession list and found a separate record indicating that questionnaires had been distributed to licensed stallholders. That record did not show whether street performers, customers, residents, or people who had left the market were contacted. Even a genuine survey, she realized, would require a denominator and sampling method before its finding could be generalized.

Her mentor warned against building a dramatic narrative out of every absence. The omission of a group from a file did not prove its exclusion from every conversation. Silence might reflect a filing decision, lost paperwork, a narrow survey frame, or exclusion from the process. The interpretation mattered precisely because the evidence could not resolve those possibilities.

Sana revised the museum label to read: 'The signed memorandum established official rules for the waterfront square. Later accounts describe both benefits and difficulties, but surviving records do not establish how broadly residents supported the terms.' It was a less triumphant sentence than the original. It was also more accountable to a reader who might return tomorrow with an additional document.

Before submitting the exhibit, she preserved her discarded draft beside the revision. The act of saving both versions mattered to her. It gave future readers a record not just of what she claimed, but of what had caused her to change her mind.

Read for distinctions between official record, retrospective testimony, source silence, and responsible revision.

Text B · Fictional institutional source packet

Four Records, Four Evidentiary Limits

DOCUMENTARY PRACTICE PACKET — ALL MATERIAL BELOW IS FICTIONAL AND CREATED FOR ENGLISH IV. No statement here represents actual civic history or real archival data.

Source 1: Memorandum excerpt, fictional official text. 'The square shall be open to licensed merchants from six in the morning until six in the evening. Freight vehicles may enter only within the posted delivery period. The signatories agree that these terms take effect on the first day of the coming season.' Strong evidence for: the written rules and who signed. Weak evidence for: widespread consent or individual experience.

Source 2: Later editorial excerpt, fictional secondary text. 'The waterfront compromise brought peace to the market, as a survey from the period demonstrates.' The editorial cites a survey but supplies neither a response count nor a sampling description. Without the survey, the scope of this statement cannot be independently assessed.

Source 3: Fictional registry note attached to the archive box. 'Questionnaires distributed to licensed stalls; count returned not entered on this index card. No separate distribution record found for other user groups.' This is evidence about the known distribution list and missing return count, not a finding about what all the market's users believed.

Source 4: Fictional late-life testimony excerpts. Mara: 'The schedule helped me know when I could sell.' Idris: 'My deliveries cost more after the change.' Each statement identifies a lived effect for one speaker. Memory, elapsed time, and the speakers' positions must be considered; neither excerpt establishes the experience of an entire population.

Analytical constraint: a credible research paragraph may infer that sources differ in perspective and evidentiary scope. It may not state that the survey proved universal support, that all groups were consulted, or that particular officials consciously suppressed testimony.

Evaluate document provenance and scope; do not confuse the text's simulated survey with empirical research.

Worked critical synthesis

Initial overclaim: 'The agreement brought peace to everyone who used the square.'

Relevant evidence: Source 1 records the official rules; Source 3 specifies a survey distribution frame but not how many responses were returned; Source 4 gives two individual experiences that differ.

Defensible revision: 'The memorandum set new market rules, and surviving later accounts describe mixed practical effects; the available record does not establish the breadth of public approval.'

Reasoning: An agreement's official terms are observable within the document. Broad opinion cannot be derived from an unknown response count or testimonies selected from only two speakers.

Limitation: The fictional archive's omissions do not show whether other records once existed or whether groups were consulted through other channels.

Vocabulary with intellectual precision

Authenticity: whether a document is what it purports to be.

Scope: which people, events, or claims the evidence covers.

Sampling frame: the population or list from which participants were selected.

Attrition or nonresponse: missing outcomes or responses that may change an interpretation.

Triangulation: relating claims to independent sources and identifying disagreement.

Misconception and formative check

Misconception: the existence of a referenced survey proves a public consensus.

What additional information would be needed to justify the editorial's claim that the compromise was widely welcomed?

Reveal model answer

The survey's exact question, eligible population, sampling frame, response count and rate, distribution of responses, date, and reasonable evidence of representativeness. Even then, the conclusion must be restricted to what was measured.

Teacher guidance and evidence criteria

Model three high-complexity reading passes: first identify the narrative's core claims and timeline; next separate embedded voices and kinds of documents; finally test the generalizations and uncertainties. Provide a graphic organizer or audio reading for access while keeping the same analytical demand. Extension: analyze whether the museum label should communicate its uncertainty directly to public visitors.

Formative criteria: precise attribution, at least three textual anchors, valid argument structure, an accurately expressed alternative reading, and a research limitation. This is not an automatically graded assessment or authoritative source check.
