Text A · Original senior narrative

The Claim That Outran Its Evidence

The seminar began with a paragraph that looked finished. A digital summary told Iris that the speaker in her chosen story had achieved a clear victory. She had not reread the final page, where the speaker returned to the same doorway without knowing whether anyone would answer. The summary's certainty was attractive: it offered a thesis before she had done the difficult work of reading. When Iris compared the summary with the actual final lines, she could find evidence of persistence but not of triumph. She annotated the difference rather than silently treating the generated account as a primary source.

The next source was a research abstract claiming complete improvement among twenty program participants. A small appendix supplied a different picture: twenty had enrolled, ten had completed the final measurement, and nine of those ten showed improvement on the reported scale. Iris drew a participation flow diagram and noticed that ten outcomes were not reported at all. She could calculate nine of ten completers, and she could say that nine enrolled participants had documented improvements. She could not say that nineteen or twenty improved, nor could she conclude that the program itself caused the changes. The original abstract had omitted the very denominator needed to interpret the result.

In the archive, a public address contained a familiar promise: the square would be reopened “when resources permit.” A headline reproduced the first half of the sentence and called reopening immediate and guaranteed. Iris drew brackets around the condition and wrote a new summary that kept it. Removing the dependent clause had not simply shortened the language; it had altered the scope of the commitment. She began to ask the same question of every convenient summary: what important qualification might have disappeared?

She then examined a translated essay in which one key word appeared as “duty.” An editorial note offered “obligation” and “care” as alternative renderings. In English, each could direct attention toward a different part of the argument: formal requirement, binding responsibility, or relational concern. Iris did not know the original language well enough to pronounce one version definitive. She compared the English effects and listed the linguistic evidence she would need, including contextual usage and the source-language passage. Her ignorance was a boundary to report, not a gap to fill with speculation.

At the end of the week, Iris kept two drafts. The first had accepted the summary's confident ending, repeated the abstract's broad success claim, and treated the headline's promise as unconditional. The revised draft described uncertainty in the story, retained the ten-completer denominator, and restored the speech's resource condition. She noted when digital tools had been used, checked each claim against primary material, and kept her own reasoning trail. Her conclusion had become less dramatic and much more responsible.

Text B · Three fictional evidence records

**Fictional study card — program evaluation.** Enrolled: 20. Final measurement available: 10. Improved among those measured: 9. Not improved among those measured: 1. Final result missing: 10. The exercise does not supply a comparison group or details about baseline comparability. Improvement among measured completers = 9/10 = 90%; proportion of all enrollees with observed improvement = 9/20 = 45%. Neither number establishes treatment effect or the outcomes of the ten missing cases.

**Fictional source passage — public address.** “The council intends to reopen the square when resources permit, following an independent safety review.” A shortened fictional headline reads “Council Guarantees Immediate Reopening.” A faithful summary must preserve intention, resource condition, and safety review. This exercise is about language, not actual municipal policy.

**Fictional literary evidence — final line.** “She waited at the closed door, listening for a reply she could not know would come.” The line can suggest unresolved hope or persistence, but it does not explicitly report a victory. Neither digital summarization nor the reader's preference can add that missing outcome.

Worked critical synthesis

A careful analyst does not confuse fluent presentation with evidence. In the fictional story, a generated summary described triumph while the final literary line ended in uncertainty. The research abstract spoke of complete improvement, though results were observed for only ten of twenty enrollees. The public headline promised an immediate result even though the primary speech included prerequisites. These three errors share a pattern: each removes a limitation that determines what the source can actually establish. A responsible revision restores the uncertainty, denominator, or condition and names what remains unknown.

Five-day close-reading and revision workshop

Every daily task connects to the existing course workspace and preserves the original grade and mastery authorities.

Monday · Verify research language and a missing denominator

Reread narrative paragraph 2 and the fictional study card. Separate the enrolled group from participants with final results. Write three defensible statements using 9/10, 9/20, and the ten missing outcomes. Explain why a causal claim is not established. Record the source and a correction in Monday's original workspace.

Modeled reasoning: Nine of ten completers (90%) had recorded improvement. Nine of twenty enrolled (45%) are known, from the given records, to have improved. Ten enrolled participants lack reported final outcomes. No control group is provided, so these figures do not establish that the program caused the improvements. The numbers refer to different denominators.

Understanding check: Can a writer claim 'nineteen of twenty improved' from these data?

Reveal evidence-based answer

No. Ten final outcomes are missing and cannot be invented or assumed.

Return to Monday saved work

Tuesday · Verify a digital summary against the primary text

Read paragraph 1 of the narrative and the fictional literary line. Build an audit table with 'digital claim,' 'actual textual evidence,' 'interpretation,' and 'unresolved uncertainty.' Rewrite the generated thesis so it does not overstate the ending. Keep the actual excerpt in your notes, not only the summary.

Modeled reasoning: Unsupported summary: 'The narrator wins.' Source-grounded interpretation: 'The final scene emphasizes waiting and uncertainty; the narrator's continued presence may suggest determination, but no response or victory is narrated.' The corrected interpretation distinguishes observable action from an inferred emotional effect.

Understanding check: Is a fluent machine-produced summary evidence of what a primary text says?

Reveal evidence-based answer

No. It is a claim to verify against the text; unsupported details should be corrected or omitted.

Return to Tuesday saved work

Wednesday · Maintain conditional scope when revising

Compare the full fictional public address with its headline. Mark every condition and the modal verb. Rewrite the headline in an accurate sentence, then explain what was lost by the shorter version. Place your revision and a two-column syntax map in Wednesday's writing record.

Modeled reasoning: Faithful: 'The council intends to reopen the square if resources permit and an independent safety review is completed.' The source does not guarantee immediate reopening. The change from 'intends' to 'guarantees' and the deletion of conditions both enlarge the claim beyond its support.

Understanding check: Does cutting a conditional clause preserve a promise's meaning?

Reveal evidence-based answer

Not when the condition limits whether the promised event may occur; its removal changes the scope.

Return to Wednesday saved work

Thursday · Compare translations without fabricating original-language facts

Compare 'duty,' 'obligation,' and 'care' as English readings of a fictional source word. Discuss which dimensions each emphasizes. Write an evidence-limit paragraph explaining why the given English alternatives are insufficient to select a uniquely correct translation. Record any external source you would need to assess more.

Modeled reasoning: 'Duty' may foreground a role or requirement; 'obligation' may emphasize being bound; 'care' may highlight relational concern. Those connotations affect interpretation in English, but the original phrase and linguistic context are absent. The reader can compare effects without asserting what the writer originally intended.

Understanding check: Can the most emotionally appealing English version be declared the original meaning without seeing the source language?

Reveal evidence-based answer

No. A judgment about fidelity requires the original wording, context, and appropriate linguistic evidence.

Return to Thursday saved work

Friday · Document authorship and defend a revised synthesis

Write a 300–400-word analysis with a precise thesis, a primary-text quotation, the numerical denominator, the restored condition, a translation caveat, and a plausible counterreading. Attach an authorship statement indicating if you used writing or summarization tools, what they helped with, and how you checked them. Preserve both drafts in Friday's existing portfolio.

Modeled reasoning: A qualified thesis can argue that misleading certainty arises when a summary erases uncertainty, drops sample boundaries, or removes grammatical conditions. The student supports it with actual fictional examples, explicitly distinguishes simulated data from research findings, and documents independent verification. Revision notes show why each claim was narrowed.

Understanding check: Does a responsible authorship statement replace textual verification?

Reveal evidence-based answer

No. It describes the process; the claims still require direct evidence, accurate attribution, and defensible reasoning.

Return to Friday saved work

Concept vocabulary

Attrition: loss of participants from later measurement. Denominator: the reference group in a proportion. Qualification: condition or limit on a claim. Textual fidelity: correspondence between interpretation and source wording. Authorship record: transparent account of the student's drafting and verification process.

Misconceptions to correct

Unknown outcomes are not failures or successes until measured; a generated summary is not a primary source; removing a conditional phrase alters a promise; and comparing translation effects is not the same as verifying the original language.

Teacher guidance and access

Foundation: give a claim/source/limit table, visible denominators, and chunked excerpts. Core: complete all five evidence-linked tasks and preserve both drafts. Extended: explore whether missing participants could bias a conclusion, distinguish association from causation, and present a formal oral defense with two plausible objections. Text-to-speech or written seminar alternatives should preserve the reasoning target.

Assess formatively: authentic source quotation, complete context, correct denominator, syntactic precision, caveats, and documented independent revision. This resource does not award assessment or completion credit; use the existing weekly classroom and teacher-scored work.
