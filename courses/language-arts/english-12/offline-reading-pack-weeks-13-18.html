WEEK 13 · SENIOR SOURCE DOSSIER

The Transit Report — Claims, Warrants and Scope

Original fictional instructional records · not real municipal data or real online posts

FICTIONAL POLICY BRIEF · An imaginary town offers an evening shuttle for people traveling from the public library to the transit station. After a four-week pilot, organizers post a headline: 'The shuttle cuts every rider's commute by twenty minutes.' The supporting document tells a narrower story. Of 40 volunteers who signed up, 32 completed a before-and-after travel log. Twenty-four of those 32 reported a shorter trip. The average decrease among the 24 reporting improvement was 20 minutes. Eight other completers reported no decrease or a longer trip; eight volunteers did not return the second log.

RESEARCHER'S MEMO. A result for riders who improved cannot be generalized to every rider. The 20-minute mean describes only the 24 whose reported time decreased, not the mean for the complete group of 32 or the original 40. We also lack a matched control group. Changes in traffic, weather, and departure times could contribute to some measured differences. The pilot's reports justify investigating whether the shuttle helps some travelers, but they do not demonstrate a guaranteed 20-minute reduction.

ADVOCATE'S RESPONSE. The initial headline overstated what we measured. Still, riders who saved time may have received meaningful help. Replacing the headline is compatible with continuing the pilot under a better study design. Publishing the missing data is not an admission that the program has no benefit. It is a way to identify whom it helps and whose needs remain unsolved.

BUDGET REVIEWER'S RESPONSE. A larger trial should state its comparison method and cost before collecting data. We should ask about trip length, reliability, accessibility, total ridership, and the frequency of use rather than celebrating an improvement among a selected subset. The correct question is not whether the shuttle is good in the abstract; it is whether its benefits and costs are documented well enough for a particular decision.

Explicit analysis and source literacy

Map conclusion, evidence, sampling population, warrant, and scope. The difference between 24/32 and 24/40 matters: 75% of completers improved, while 60% of enrollees are confirmed to have improved and the other outcomes are incomplete. Neither percentage proves causal impact.

Worked advanced reasoning

A responsible claim is: 'Twenty-four of 32 people completing logs reported shorter trips, and those 24 averaged a 20-minute reduction.' This is descriptive. It does not claim a 20-minute benefit for everyone or prove that the shuttle alone caused the decrease.

Independent synthesis and defense

  1. Draw an argument map with four components: claim, evidence, warrant, and limitation.
  2. Identify and correct two overclaims in the original headline.
  3. Draft a formal policy paragraph that defends a next step while respecting the missing data.

Reading self-check · Week 13

What does 24 out of 32 establish, and what does it not establish?

Reveal the model response

It establishes that 75% of log completers reported shorter trips. It does not prove every rider benefited, determine outcomes for eight noncompleters, or establish that the shuttle caused the change.

Practice only. This does not record a grade or alter the weekly assessment.

Teacher notes

Distinguish conditional averages, missing observations, and causal inference. All figures are invented for teaching, not a municipal service report.

WEEK 14 · SENIOR SOURCE DOSSIER

Two Public Arguments — Steelmanning and Fair Rebuttal

Original fictional instructional records · not real municipal data or real online posts

POSITION ONE · Continuing the shuttle. The local access committee argues that the pilot offered a valuable first glimpse into evening travel needs. Those who reported improvements were not imaginary beneficiaries; their experiences are worth investigating. If the town ends the service before refining the records, it may overlook people whose work shifts or disability-related access constraints make ordinary connections difficult. The committee recommends a six-week extension with standardized log entries and voluntary, privacy-respecting rider interviews.

POSITION TWO · Reworking the pilot. The fiscal review group agrees that travel access matters but questions whether the shuttle route is the best use of limited funds. The trip logs are voluntary, lack a comparison group, and omit eight follow-ups. The group recommends testing a schedule change on the existing bus line, comparing costs and access outcomes over the same weeks, and avoiding any permanent promise until both approaches can be assessed.

SEMINAR EXCHANGE. COMMITTEE REPRESENTATIVE: 'Your request for comparison is not a demand to abandon riders. But a schedule-only alternative may fail people who cannot reach the current stop.' REVIEWER: 'Your concern for access is not proof that a particular shuttle works for everyone. But you are right that an option inaccessible to some travelers cannot count as an equally effective substitute.' MODERATOR: 'Which factual questions could distinguish these proposals? Which value judgments will remain even after the measurements improve?'

ANALYST'S COMMENT. A straw-man reply would say the fiscal group 'does not care about riders.' That distorts the actual proposal. Another weak reply would call the committee 'anti-data' although it requested improved data. A fair rebuttal identifies a real weakness: for example, schedule changes might leave distance-to-stop barriers untouched, while an extended shuttle trial might cost more than alternative services. Both arguments still need comparable outcomes and transparent cost accounting.

Explicit analysis and source literacy

Separate validity from persuasion and steelman each side. A strong concession preserves the opposing position's best reasoning; a rebuttal responds to an actual premise with relevant evidence or an explicit evaluative standard.

Worked advanced reasoning

The strongest version of the reviewer argues that limited resources require comparing effective alternatives, not that transit access lacks value. A fair response would question whether the bus-line alternative actually reaches people the shuttle helps, not accuse the reviewer of opposing accessibility.

Independent synthesis and defense

  1. Write two charitable 100-word summaries and allow a partner to check whether each would be recognized by its original speaker.
  2. Construct a claim–warrant–objection–concession–rebuttal outline using the fictional evidence.
  3. Identify one possible false dilemma and one missing comparison measure in the debate.

Reading self-check · Week 14

What makes a rebuttal fair rather than a straw man?

Reveal the model response

A fair rebuttal accurately states the strongest relevant point of the opposing argument, then challenges its premise, evidence, or implications. It does not replace the argument with an easier accusation.

Practice only. This does not record a grade or alter the weekly assessment.

Teacher notes

Do not grade agreement with either position. Score representation of opposing views, evidence quality, and willingness to revise overclaims.

WEEK 15 · SENIOR SOURCE DOSSIER

A Viral Chart — Media Provenance and Quantitative Integrity

Original fictional instructional records · not real municipal data or real online posts

SOCIAL POST · A fictional account shares a cropped bar chart entitled 'Shuttle success: 75% happy passengers.' The bars show 24 positive reports and 8 other completed reports, but the accompanying caption says, 'Three out of four town residents approve.' The post leaves out the eight pilot volunteers with no follow-up data. It also replaces 'reported shorter trips' with 'happy,' which was never measured in the travel log.

ORIGINAL REPORT EXCERPT · 'Among the 32 volunteers who returned both travel logs, 24 reported shorter trips. This self-selected pilot did not sample town residents at random. The program did not administer a satisfaction survey. The findings should not be generalized to all riders or used alone to infer a cause.' A footnote indicates that no comparison group was used and that the numbers represent participant reports rather than independently verified travel times.

VERIFICATION NOTEBOOK. The first task is not to decide whether the social account is malicious; it is to trace the claim. A reader searches for the original report, checks the denominator, distinguishes a travel-time outcome from a satisfaction outcome, and compares the screenshot with the fuller display. If the original source cannot be located, the correct label is 'unverified,' not necessarily 'false.' The reader also asks whether the sharing account gave a timestamp, whether another site reproduced the report independently, and whether cropping removed a warning.

CORRECTION DRAFT. 'The screenshot does not support the claim that 75% of town residents approved the shuttle. It displays 24 of 32 returning pilot volunteers who reported shorter travel times. Eight original volunteers had missing follow-up logs, and no approval question was asked. These results may motivate more study, but they do not justify the social caption.' The correction names the evidence and preserves the distinction between error and unproven intent.

Explicit analysis and source literacy

Media verification requires tracing provenance, separating indicators, checking denominator and population, identifying omitted notes, and naming what remains unknown. Use lateral reading and source comparison, not the popularity of a post, as evidence.

Worked advanced reasoning

24/32 = 75% of completers reporting improvement. It is not 75% of all enrolled volunteers, nor 75% of residents, nor any measured approval rating. Each change in denominator or outcome creates a materially different assertion.

Independent synthesis and defense

  1. Build a four-row verification table: screenshot claim, actual source, numerical meaning, and justified correction.
  2. Write a 200-word public explainer that avoids speculating about the poster's motivation.
  3. Design an accessible alternative caption for the chart stating who was measured, who was missing, and what the chart does not prove.

Reading self-check · Week 15

Why is the caption '75% of town residents approve' unsupported?

Reveal the model response

The source measured reported shorter travel times among 32 returning pilot volunteers. It did not randomly sample residents or ask about approval. The caption changes both population and outcome.

Practice only. This does not record a grade or alter the weekly assessment.

Teacher notes

Use a mock social post, not an actual person's account. Distinguish fabrication, decontextualization, and uncertainty; require accessible numerical explanation.

Return to Week 15 existing records

WEEK 16 · ORIGINAL FICTIONAL SOURCE SET

The Incomplete Survey — Evidence Without Forced Agreement

Constructed senior inquiry scenario · not empirical findings

FICTIONAL CIVIC SURVEY BRIEF: In the invented township of Vale Harbor, a library committee surveyed fifty visitors who attended a weekday program. Thirty-four respondents said longer hours would help them. The committee's summary called this a mandate from “most residents.” Yet the sampling frame covered current weekday visitors, not every resident, and offered little access to people who worked when the program took place. The briefing included no estimate of the number of eligible residents or of potential nonresponse among people unable to visit.

FICTIONAL PERSONAL TESTIMONY: A resident named Nura described difficulty reaching the library after work and argued for evening hours. Another witness, Tomas, praised the present afternoon schedule because it allowed his children to attend before dinner. Neither witness could supply a complete townwide trend. Their experiences are meaningful but not interchangeable with the visitor survey. One speaks about a barrier; the other about an existing benefit. A careful writer must represent both without presenting their disagreement as a contest to determine who is telling the truth.

FICTIONAL BUDGET NOTE: The library director estimated that extending Thursday hours would require changing one staff shift, and the note asked for a review of transportation access, wage agreements and operating costs. It did not give an approved budget or predict how many new users would attend. An editorial draft announced, “The survey proves that extended hours serve everyone.” A research reviewer rewrote that sentence: “Most respondents in a limited survey favored extended hours, while other sources indicate that scheduling changes could benefit some groups and inconvenience others.” The narrower statement preserved useful evidence and made its limits explicit.

Explicit instruction

Source-based synthesis compares what different methods reveal. The survey's denominator is fifty sampled visitors, not all residents. Analyze agreement, conflict, missing perspectives, relevance, source provenance and the epistemic scope of testimony. Synthesis can establish a nuanced conclusion without forcing uniformity or averaging incomparable evidence.

Worked senior analysis

Thirty-four of fifty surveyed weekday visitors equals 68% of that survey sample; it is not a population estimate without representative sampling. A defensible synthesis joins the measured interest with Nura's access issue, Tomas's concern and the incomplete budget note, then recommends targeted outreach and transparent constraints.

Guided and independent research

  1. Develop an evidence matrix with source, population, claim, denominator and limitation.
  2. Write a 200-word synthesis organized around access and constraints, citing all three sources responsibly.
  3. Explain why neither visitor percentage nor two testimonies establishes a community mandate.

Misconception check: A synthesis does not turn a convenience sample into a representative population estimate.

Reading self-check · Week 16

Does 34 out of 50 prove 68% of all residents prefer longer hours?

Reveal the model response

No. It is 68% of the fifty surveyed visitors, with no demonstrated representativeness of the entire township.

Teacher guidance

Require attention to sampling frame, testimonial voice and cost uncertainty; do not use fictional numbers as actual library statistics.

WEEK 17 · ORIGINAL FICTIONAL SOURCE SET

The Research Prospectus — Scope, Sources and Ethical Questions

Constructed senior inquiry scenario · not empirical findings

FICTIONAL STUDENT PROPOSAL, FIRST DRAFT: “My research will prove that every community should keep public libraries open all night.” The learner, Sera, lists a fictional local news story, one opinion column and the Vale Harbor survey but has not checked the original survey method. The question already assumes the conclusion and spans budgets, worker safety, transit, disability access and public demand in every community. Sera's mentor responds that a source list without publication dates, authorship and methodological notes is not yet an annotated bibliography.

SERA'S SECOND DRAFT asks: “How might one additional evening library session each week affect access for employed adult learners in Vale Harbor during a twelve-week pilot?” The question names a population, one operational change, a location, a timespan and an outcome that can be described. It still requires defining “access,” specifying what data may ethically be gathered, and recognizing that employed learners are not the only relevant group. The project can begin as a research design without claiming that the pilot has taken place.

A RESEARCHER'S SOURCE PLAN proposes reviewing the library's actual, authorized schedule, interviewing participants voluntarily with informed consent, comparing possible cost scenarios from verified budget documents, and searching scholarly studies on library access in similar settings. The proposal records the source's author, date, method, population, relevant claim and limitations. One annotation explains why the weekday visitor survey can help formulate hypotheses but is inadequate as a townwide estimate. A second note explains that student-made mock documents must never be included as authentic primary research.

Explicit instruction

A feasible research question is focused, researchable, contestable and ethically appropriate. An annotation is more than a summary; identify provenance, method, credibility, relevance and limitation. Avoid reverse-engineering sources to prove an answer chosen in advance. Maintain a difference between a proposed method, verified evidence and speculative prediction.

Worked senior analysis

Broad thesis-first topic: 'All libraries should open all night.' Researchable question: 'What barriers and potential benefits should a twelve-week evening-session pilot measure among employed adult learners in one community?' The latter can guide data collection without presupposing an outcome.

Guided and independent research

  1. Revise the first research claim into a narrow question naming population, timeframe, variable and evidence needed.
  2. Create three **provisional** annotated bibliography records as source-search plans; do not invent published authors or URLs.
  3. Write a brief ethics note covering consent, privacy, accessibility and risk of excluding underrepresented participants.

Misconception check: An impressive list of source titles is not a credible bibliography if sources were never verified.

Reading self-check · Week 17

Why isn't a fictional practice survey an acceptable authentic citation in a serious research bibliography?

Reveal the model response

Because it is invented instructional material and cannot substantiate an empirical claim about real people.

Teacher guidance

Assess annotations for source verification and intellectual fairness. Use actual library/catalog databases if available, but keep a no-login local alternative plan.

WEEK 18 · ORIGINAL FICTIONAL SOURCE SET

Midyear Oral Defense — Revising a Claim Without Erasing Uncertainty

Constructed senior inquiry scenario · not empirical findings

FICTIONAL SENIOR PORTFOLIO EXCERPT: Sera's first essay claimed that extending library hours would unquestionably improve education for the whole township. A subsequent draft distinguished the observed interest of weekday visitors from uncounted residents, discussed two contrasting testimonials and revised the thesis into a conditional recommendation for a limited pilot. Sera preserved the old version and annotated why the change mattered: the initial conclusion had used an unrepresentative sample as though it described everyone.

DURING A MIDYEAR CONFERENCE, the teacher asks what Sera learned that would transfer to a new research topic. Sera replies: “I now ask who produced each source, who could participate, what the denominator means and which interpretation the evidence can support. I also ask which people were absent and whether my question silently assumes a favorable outcome.” A peer challenges Sera's confidence in the pilot design. She acknowledges that a proposed study has no empirical results yet; even a good method cannot guarantee its prediction.

SERA'S REVISION PLAN names three artifacts: an initial annotated reading, a midterm-source synthesis and a research prospectus. For each she identifies a concrete intellectual change rather than a cosmetic improvement. She lists future work on literature-review synthesis, proper citation, oral reasoning and accessible presentation. The course's official midterm examination is administered and evaluated separately under its existing authority. The fictional conference rehearses the process of explaining growth and gaps; it must not supply exam solutions or replace teacher review.

Explicit instruction

Senior readiness is evidenced by transferable methods, responsible source handling, independent composition and a defensible research question. A midyear conference should allow challenge and revision without converting every uncertainty into an untestable opinion. Separate formative practice, authentic portfolio evidence and the controlled midterm.

Worked senior analysis

Weak defense: 'My new thesis is right because I found studies.' Improved defense: 'My revised conclusion is proportionate to observed data and named limitations; I would need a representative design and verified source methods to make a broader claim.' A qualified response can still recommend a precise next step.

Guided and independent research

  1. Prepare a three-artifact portfolio defense identifying initial reasoning, revision and independent transfer.
  2. Record two methodological strengths, two unresolved questions and one concrete research follow-up.
  3. Practice responding to a fair objection in a two-minute oral or accessible equivalent presentation; preserve feedback in the original course records.

Misconception check: Formative reading checks and rehearsal conferences are not alternative routes to a secured midterm score.

Reading self-check · Week 18

Does admitting that a proposed study has no results undermine the validity of the research plan?

Reveal the model response

No. It accurately separates the quality of a research design from evidence about outcomes that have not yet been observed.

Teacher guidance

Evaluate senior synthesis, ethical source use and oral argument against official rubrics; preserve student ownership and existing midterm security.
