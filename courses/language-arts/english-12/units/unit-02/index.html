Senior required reading · A Speech to Two Audiences

Original Academy close-reading source, embedded for offline use.

The mayor promised efficiency to merchants and fairness to residents in the same speech. The terms sounded compatible, but each audience understood the costs differently. The critic tracked how a single address concealed competing obligations.

Senior analysis: Develop a nuanced interpretation with at least two textual details, address a competing reading and a limitation in your claim, and outline how additional primary or scholarly sources could test it. Revisit during Weeks 4–6. Preserve work in the existing portfolio; this microtext complements longer advanced literature.

Two Audiences, One Address — extended rhetorical reading

Original Khaemenes Academy extended reading, available offline.

At the opening of a public market, the mayor announced a plan to reduce traffic. To residents whose windows faced the square, she promised quiet streets. To merchants who depended on deliveries, she promised efficient access. Both groups applauded the same sentence: 'The new plan will give this square back to its people.'

A reporter asked which people would gain access, and who might lose it. The mayor described a trial period but did not name the hours during which delivery vehicles would be restricted. A merchants' representative later praised the plan's ambition while asking for a published schedule. A resident replied that a schedule alone would not measure noise.

In a commentary, a student initially called the speech deceptive because its wording appealed to competing audiences. The teacher asked for a narrower claim. The student revised: the speech unites its listeners through inclusive language while leaving the costs of implementation undefined. That observation could be supported without assuming the mayor's intentions.

Close-reading and composition assignment

Write a 300–400-word rhetorical critique: analyze audience, inclusive diction, implied trade-offs, and what evidence would be required to evaluate the policy. Present an alternate reading fairly.

Preserve the first draft and a revision in your existing student portfolio. This reading supplements, rather than replaces, longer assigned works.
