WEEK 13 · SENIOR SOURCE DOSSIER

The Transit Report — Claims, Warrants and Scope

Original fictional instructional records · not real municipal data or real online posts

FICTIONAL POLICY BRIEF · An imaginary town offers an evening shuttle for people traveling from the public library to the transit station. After a four-week pilot, organizers post a headline: 'The shuttle cuts every rider's commute by twenty minutes.' The supporting document tells a narrower story. Of 40 volunteers who signed up, 32 completed a before-and-after travel log. Twenty-four of those 32 reported a shorter trip. The average decrease among the 24 reporting improvement was 20 minutes. Eight other completers reported no decrease or a longer trip; eight volunteers did not return the second log.

RESEARCHER'S MEMO. A result for riders who improved cannot be generalized to every rider. The 20-minute mean describes only the 24 whose reported time decreased, not the mean for the complete group of 32 or the original 40. We also lack a matched control group. Changes in traffic, weather, and departure times could contribute to some measured differences. The pilot's reports justify investigating whether the shuttle helps some travelers, but they do not demonstrate a guaranteed 20-minute reduction.

ADVOCATE'S RESPONSE. The initial headline overstated what we measured. Still, riders who saved time may have received meaningful help. Replacing the headline is compatible with continuing the pilot under a better study design. Publishing the missing data is not an admission that the program has no benefit. It is a way to identify whom it helps and whose needs remain unsolved.

BUDGET REVIEWER'S RESPONSE. A larger trial should state its comparison method and cost before collecting data. We should ask about trip length, reliability, accessibility, total ridership, and the frequency of use rather than celebrating an improvement among a selected subset. The correct question is not whether the shuttle is good in the abstract; it is whether its benefits and costs are documented well enough for a particular decision.

Explicit analysis and source literacy

Map conclusion, evidence, sampling population, warrant, and scope. The difference between 24/32 and 24/40 matters: 75% of completers improved, while 60% of enrollees are confirmed to have improved and the other outcomes are incomplete. Neither percentage proves causal impact.

Worked advanced reasoning

A responsible claim is: 'Twenty-four of 32 people completing logs reported shorter trips, and those 24 averaged a 20-minute reduction.' This is descriptive. It does not claim a 20-minute benefit for everyone or prove that the shuttle alone caused the decrease.

Independent synthesis and defense

  1. Draw an argument map with four components: claim, evidence, warrant, and limitation.
  2. Identify and correct two overclaims in the original headline.
  3. Draft a formal policy paragraph that defends a next step while respecting the missing data.
Teacher notes

Distinguish conditional averages, missing observations, and causal inference. All figures are invented for teaching, not a municipal service report.

WEEK 14 · SENIOR SOURCE DOSSIER

Two Public Arguments — Steelmanning and Fair Rebuttal

Original fictional instructional records · not real municipal data or real online posts

POSITION ONE · Continuing the shuttle. The local access committee argues that the pilot offered a valuable first glimpse into evening travel needs. Those who reported improvements were not imaginary beneficiaries; their experiences are worth investigating. If the town ends the service before refining the records, it may overlook people whose work shifts or disability-related access constraints make ordinary connections difficult. The committee recommends a six-week extension with standardized log entries and voluntary, privacy-respecting rider interviews.

POSITION TWO · Reworking the pilot. The fiscal review group agrees that travel access matters but questions whether the shuttle route is the best use of limited funds. The trip logs are voluntary, lack a comparison group, and omit eight follow-ups. The group recommends testing a schedule change on the existing bus line, comparing costs and access outcomes over the same weeks, and avoiding any permanent promise until both approaches can be assessed.

SEMINAR EXCHANGE. COMMITTEE REPRESENTATIVE: 'Your request for comparison is not a demand to abandon riders. But a schedule-only alternative may fail people who cannot reach the current stop.' REVIEWER: 'Your concern for access is not proof that a particular shuttle works for everyone. But you are right that an option inaccessible to some travelers cannot count as an equally effective substitute.' MODERATOR: 'Which factual questions could distinguish these proposals? Which value judgments will remain even after the measurements improve?'

ANALYST'S COMMENT. A straw-man reply would say the fiscal group 'does not care about riders.' That distorts the actual proposal. Another weak reply would call the committee 'anti-data' although it requested improved data. A fair rebuttal identifies a real weakness: for example, schedule changes might leave distance-to-stop barriers untouched, while an extended shuttle trial might cost more than alternative services. Both arguments still need comparable outcomes and transparent cost accounting.

Explicit analysis and source literacy

Separate validity from persuasion and steelman each side. A strong concession preserves the opposing position's best reasoning; a rebuttal responds to an actual premise with relevant evidence or an explicit evaluative standard.

Worked advanced reasoning

The strongest version of the reviewer argues that limited resources require comparing effective alternatives, not that transit access lacks value. A fair response would question whether the bus-line alternative actually reaches people the shuttle helps, not accuse the reviewer of opposing accessibility.

Independent synthesis and defense

  1. Write two charitable 100-word summaries and allow a partner to check whether each would be recognized by its original speaker.
  2. Construct a claim–warrant–objection–concession–rebuttal outline using the fictional evidence.
  3. Identify one possible false dilemma and one missing comparison measure in the debate.
Teacher notes

Do not grade agreement with either position. Score representation of opposing views, evidence quality, and willingness to revise overclaims.

WEEK 15 · SENIOR SOURCE DOSSIER

A Viral Chart — Media Provenance and Quantitative Integrity

Original fictional instructional records · not real municipal data or real online posts

SOCIAL POST · A fictional account shares a cropped bar chart entitled 'Shuttle success: 75% happy passengers.' The bars show 24 positive reports and 8 other completed reports, but the accompanying caption says, 'Three out of four town residents approve.' The post leaves out the eight pilot volunteers with no follow-up data. It also replaces 'reported shorter trips' with 'happy,' which was never measured in the travel log.

ORIGINAL REPORT EXCERPT · 'Among the 32 volunteers who returned both travel logs, 24 reported shorter trips. This self-selected pilot did not sample town residents at random. The program did not administer a satisfaction survey. The findings should not be generalized to all riders or used alone to infer a cause.' A footnote indicates that no comparison group was used and that the numbers represent participant reports rather than independently verified travel times.

VERIFICATION NOTEBOOK. The first task is not to decide whether the social account is malicious; it is to trace the claim. A reader searches for the original report, checks the denominator, distinguishes a travel-time outcome from a satisfaction outcome, and compares the screenshot with the fuller display. If the original source cannot be located, the correct label is 'unverified,' not necessarily 'false.' The reader also asks whether the sharing account gave a timestamp, whether another site reproduced the report independently, and whether cropping removed a warning.

CORRECTION DRAFT. 'The screenshot does not support the claim that 75% of town residents approved the shuttle. It displays 24 of 32 returning pilot volunteers who reported shorter travel times. Eight original volunteers had missing follow-up logs, and no approval question was asked. These results may motivate more study, but they do not justify the social caption.' The correction names the evidence and preserves the distinction between error and unproven intent.

Explicit analysis and source literacy

Media verification requires tracing provenance, separating indicators, checking denominator and population, identifying omitted notes, and naming what remains unknown. Use lateral reading and source comparison, not the popularity of a post, as evidence.

Worked advanced reasoning

24/32 = 75% of completers reporting improvement. It is not 75% of all enrolled volunteers, nor 75% of residents, nor any measured approval rating. Each change in denominator or outcome creates a materially different assertion.

Independent synthesis and defense

  1. Build a four-row verification table: screenshot claim, actual source, numerical meaning, and justified correction.
  2. Write a 200-word public explainer that avoids speculating about the poster's motivation.
  3. Design an accessible alternative caption for the chart stating who was measured, who was missing, and what the chart does not prove.
Teacher notes

Use a mock social post, not an actual person's account. Distinguish fabrication, decontextualization, and uncertainty; require accessible numerical explanation.
