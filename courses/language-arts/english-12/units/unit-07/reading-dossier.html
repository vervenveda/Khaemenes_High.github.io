WEEK 19 · ORIGINAL FICTIONAL LITERATURE

The Photograph at the Station

Two original classroom narratives · fictional practice, not real testimony or historical evidence

Reading A

Mira discovered a photograph tucked inside her father's old cookbook. It showed a railway platform with four people standing by a clock. She recognized his coat, but not the other faces. The station had since been rebuilt, and Mira had always assumed he wished to forget the town where he grew up. Yet he had preserved this photograph beside recipes he still made each winter. Mira wrote in her notebook, “He remembers differently from me.” Then she crossed out “differently.” She realized the comparison assumed she knew what the picture meant to him. The photograph documented a scene; it did not explain his motives or what happened outside the frame.

Reading B

Ivo had watched the photograph being taken. The photographer asked him to step behind a bench so the new station clock would be visible. It was raining; the people in the final picture were waiting for a delayed service, not preparing for an important departure. When Mira described the image as a record of leaving, Ivo called it a record of waiting. Neither knew how the other felt at the time. Mira accepted Ivo's account of the photographic arrangement without treating it as the last word on her father's attachment to the image. The two perspectives changed the image's possible meanings without changing a single visible pixel.

Explicit senior literature instruction

Modern fiction frequently withholds information by selecting a narrator or focal consciousness. Distinguish what a character actually observes from what that character infers about another's motives. Material artifacts such as photographs may recur as motifs, yet a photograph is a framed record rather than a full account of the past. The reader must also notice how a narrator revises an earlier assumption. Modern world fiction is not one literary tradition; these invented scenes are close-reading exercises, not representative accounts of a particular culture.

Worked comparative analysis

A defensible interpretation states: Mira's crossing-out of “differently” reveals self-correction because her earlier sentence assumed access to her father's memories. Ivo's knowledge about the staged clock resolves part of the photograph's provenance but does not settle its emotional meaning for the father. The two narrators therefore complicate different forms of certainty. One concerns personal motive; the other concerns the circumstances of representation.

Guided and independent analysis

Find one observation, one unsupported inference, and one moment of revision in each scene. What information is unavailable to both characters?

  1. Create a perspective matrix identifying each narrator's observations, interpretations and unknowns.
  2. Write a 180–220-word comparative claim about visual evidence, memory and focalization, supported by textual phrases.
  3. Retell four sentences from a third witness's limited viewpoint and explain what that witness cannot know.

Misconception and evidence self-check

Contradictory interpretations do not automatically make every factual claim equally reliable.

Does Ivo's knowledge of how the photograph was staged prove what it meant to Mira's father?

No. Ivo can clarify circumstances of the photograph, but not another person's private interpretation.

Teacher guidance

Model factual evidence versus narrative implication and ask students to mark who knows each detail. Preserve the original Week 19 interpretation and reading record.

WEEK 20 · ORIGINAL FICTIONAL LITERATURE

Letters from Two Doorways

Two original classroom narratives · fictional practice, not real testimony or historical evidence

Reading A

Lina catalogued letters her grandmother wrote after moving to the invented coastal city of Alisport. The letters described crowded rooms, a borrowed sewing machine and unfamiliar bus routes. Lina wanted to call the archive “The Story of Arrival” because a new address seemed to mark a beginning. But one letter read, “I arrived every day and was still on the way.” Some pages described neighbors offering help; others recorded debts and the people left behind. Lina noticed how her proposed title compressed years of uncertain belonging into one triumphant moment. The archive contained no letters from people who could not write, whose papers were lost or who had chosen not to share them.

Reading B

At a public hearing in the same invented city, officials proposed numbering neighborhood streets to simplify emergency navigation. Residents who supported the plan described delivery drivers getting lost. Others valued the names as records of local history. Dania cared deeply about the old street names but also wanted signs emergency workers could read at night. A planner proposed dual signs while admitting maintenance costs had not been calculated. Lina saw a resemblance to her archive: official systems needed clarity, but clarity could erase layered experiences if their designers asked too few questions. She did not assume the public hearing settled whose history mattered, and she did not mistake one person's view for a townwide consensus.

Explicit senior literature instruction

Diaspora, displacement and postcolonial writing involve different histories of movement, power, language, sovereignty and belonging; they are not interchangeable categories. The stories here are invented and do not document real colonial relationships or migration experiences. For authentic study, locate works by identifiable authors and verify contextual information. Within these fictional texts, examine how archive labels, street names and narrative frames can include some histories while excluding others.

Worked comparative analysis

The grandmother's paradox, “I arrived every day and was still on the way,” challenges a simple chronological reading of arrival. In the hearing, Dania values both historical memory and practical wayfinding. A careful synthesis says that naming practices shape public understanding but the text supplies no evidence of a final policy decision or a representative survey. The scenes show different tensions without asking readers to choose an uncomplicated side.

Guided and independent analysis

Which words in Lina's proposed title imply closure? Which questions about the street proposal remain unresolved?

  1. Annotate how each text treats the difference between a documented event and a lasting sense of belonging.
  2. Compose a claim and counterclaim chart for the dual-sign proposal, including unanswered cost and access questions.
  3. Select an authentic, teacher-approved text about displacement and research its author and context without assuming it represents every member of a group.

Misconception and evidence self-check

A fictional character's migration does not by itself establish a postcolonial history, and no one voice can represent an entire diaspora.

Must Dania oppose safer emergency navigation in order to value historic street names?

No. The account records her simultaneous concern for usable signage and preservation of local memory.

Teacher guidance

Avoid exercises that require students to impersonate real displaced populations. Pair these invented readings with authentic, contextualized voices and ethical source evaluation.

WEEK 21 · ORIGINAL FICTIONAL LITERATURE

Three Versions of a Farewell

Two original classroom narratives · fictional practice, not real testimony or historical evidence

Reading A

At a workshop, Sorin compared two English versions of the last line of an invented letter. One read, “Keep a light for me.” The other read, “Leave the window bright.” The first sounded like a request to another person; the second placed attention on a physical place. No participant could consult the fictional source language, and the handout supplied no original grammar. A classmate preferred the first because it felt more moving. Sorin agreed its effect was different, but refused to call it the more accurate translation. He wrote two notes: “What each English version suggests” and “What we would need to know before claiming fidelity.”

Reading B

A student director adapted the imagined letter for the stage. An actor asked for a lamp and left before it appeared. Only afterward did another performer raise a curtain to reveal a bright window. Mira thought the gesture suggested hope arriving too late; Ivo interpreted it as a response that reached someone after all. The rehearsal notes recorded several deliberate changes: a written request became spoken dialogue, the image was delayed, and the audience saw something the departing actor did not. The director had created a new distribution of knowledge rather than merely repeating a line. Neither the director nor the audience could determine what an unavailable original-language letter had said.

Explicit senior literature instruction

Translation aims to represent a source-language work responsibly; analysis of accuracy requires access to the source and knowledge of its linguistic context. Adaptation changes medium, structure and who knows what. Intertextuality concerns relationships among texts, which can include quotation, reference, retelling and transformation. Here the source language is deliberately unavailable; evaluate the supplied English effects and staging choices without inventing claims about grammar or cultural authenticity.

Worked comparative analysis

“Keep a light for me” emphasizes a relationship; “Leave the window bright” emphasizes a continuing state. The stage adaptation delays illumination so that the audience knows more than the departing actor, a form of dramatic irony. A defensible comparison distinguishes the versions' observable effects from their untestable fidelity to an unseen source. Interpretive appeal is not evidence of translation correctness.

Guided and independent analysis

Compare what readers of the letter, an actor onstage and members of the audience can know. Which effect depends on staging rather than word choice?

  1. Build a three-column comparison of translated wording, staged adaptation and audience knowledge.
  2. Write a 200-word analysis using two precise textual references and one acknowledged uncertainty.
  3. Draft six stage directions that change pacing or focalization and annotate three deliberate adaptation decisions.

Misconception and evidence self-check

Adaptation is not word-for-word translation, and aesthetic preference does not verify a translation's accuracy.

Why might the final illuminated window produce dramatic irony?

The audience sees the light after the actor leaves, creating a knowledge difference between viewers and the departing character.

Teacher guidance

Keep source-language claims explicitly provisional. For an authentic text, require edition, translator credit and contextual notes; preserve existing Week 21 assessment authority.
