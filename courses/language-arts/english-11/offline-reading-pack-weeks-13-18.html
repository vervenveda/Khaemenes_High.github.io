Unit 05 · Weeks 13–15

VERIFIED SOURCE CUSTODY · UNIT 05

Authenticated comparative civic-argument set

Primary source family. Abraham Lincoln and Stephen A. Douglas, Political Debates ... in the Celebrated Campaign of 1858 in Illinois, 1860 published collection, Library of Congress item 12007439. The Library of Congress identifies this digitized book as public domain and free to use/reuse. Open Library of Congress source

Week 13 · Rhetorical devices and figurative force. Assigned span: one Lincoln opening/response and one Douglas opening/response from the same joint debate. Mark antithesis, rhetorical question, repetition, analogy, concession, and audience positioning.

Week 14 · Multiple arguments on the same topic. Use matched sections from a single 1858 debate so both speakers respond to the same occasion. Separate historical claims, constitutional reasoning, moral claims, and attacks on the opponent.

Week 15 · Contemporaneous authors and shared topics. Compare Lincoln and Douglas as contemporaneous political speakers while preserving the historical reality that the debates centered heavily on slavery and Black citizenship. Students must not flatten the disagreement into a generic “two sides” exercise; evaluate claims in historical and ethical context.

Provenance caution: the Library of Congress notes that newspaper reporting of the debates could be partisan and that Lincoln himself preserved clippings from party-aligned papers. Use the published collection as the course baseline while discussing transmission and reporting conditions.

WEEK 13 · ORIGINAL RHETORICAL READING

A Bridge Is More Than Stone — Rhetorical Address

Invented period-style instructional texts · not authenticated archival documents

FICTIONAL ADDRESS · Mara Venn, 1924 · Invented period-style source for classroom analysis. Fellow residents: A bridge is more than stone. It is a decision about whose journey matters. At the western bank, a worker waits for the first morning ferry and misses an hour of wages when the river is high. At the eastern bank, a pupil waits for an evening crossing and reaches home after dark. We measure the river in yards; we ought also to measure the time the river takes from its neighbors.

I have heard that a bridge will be costly. I agree. We should publish the estimate and name its uncertainty. I have heard that the ferries have served us for years. They have. But a service that works on quiet mornings may fail when floodwater rises. Shall we call an old arrangement fair simply because it is familiar? Shall we call an expense unnecessary simply because the people who pay it do not all endure the delay?

We need a bridge not for one trade but for several; not for one neighborhood but for every bank; not as a monument to our generosity but as a practical route open to examination. Let the engineer show the calculations. Let the treasurer show the accounts. Let the school show who arrives late and how that number was counted. If the evidence weakens our proposal, revise the proposal. If it supports the need, do not hide behind a further year of convenient postponement.

I do not promise that a bridge will solve every hardship in this district. Houses will remain far from workplaces, and some travelers will still need transport to the crossing. I ask for a public study with clear costs, accessible routes, and an honest vote. A town cannot cross a river on eloquence; it can only decide whether eloquence will be followed by work.

Explicit instructional focus

Identify anaphora in 'Let the...', antithesis in 'not for one...but...', rhetorical questions, and the extended metaphor of a bridge as civic connection. Analyze what each device does rather than merely naming it. This is an invented address, not a historical source.

Worked rhetorical analysis

The repetition of 'Let the' distributes responsibility among engineer, treasurer, and school. That syntax makes the speaker's appeal seem accountable to multiple forms of evidence rather than resting on emotion alone. Its limit is that none of the documents is actually supplied within this fictional speech.

Guided and independent writing

  1. Select three rhetorical devices, quote each exactly, explain their effects, and evaluate whether they support the claim.
  2. Analyze how the speaker concedes cost and imperfect outcomes without abandoning advocacy.
  3. Revise a short paragraph by removing one rhetorical device and explaining the change in persuasive force.

Reading self-check · Week 13

What is the effect of repeating 'Let the' in the invented address?

Reveal the model response

The parallel clauses distribute demands for evidence across the engineer, treasurer, and school. They connect persuasion to public accountability, though no documents have yet been supplied.

Practice only. This does not record a grade or alter the weekly assessment.

Teacher guidance and source ethics

Ask students to distinguish a rhetorical device from a logical warrant. The 1924 label is fictional; do not treat it as authentic archival evidence or use it for real historical sourcing.

WEEK 14 · ORIGINAL RHETORICAL READING

A Bridge and Its Alternatives — Competing Civic Arguments

Invented period-style instructional texts · not authenticated archival documents

TEXT A · Short editorial attributed to fictional Mara Venn. Delaying a formal feasibility study imposes costs even when the town writes no check. A ferry passenger who loses working hours bears a cost outside the public ledger. A family whose child arrives home late bears another. These burdens do not settle the bridge question, but they make it irresponsible to consider construction costs alone. Commission a comparative study and publish the assumptions behind its recommendations.

TEXT B · Response attributed to fictional accountant Jonas Reed. The delays described by Venn deserve attention, but a bridge is not the only possible remedy. A scheduled ferry with safer evening service, better landing ramps, and published reliability figures might help travelers sooner and for less money. No credible cost comparison has been produced for either plan. When the advocate calls another year 'convenient postponement,' she risks presenting a demand for alternatives as indifference to people who wait.

TEXT C · Joint fact sheet drafted for the fictional public meeting. Last quarter, travelers logged 140 late ferry arrivals on a voluntary report card. The organizers do not know how many distinct passengers submitted reports, how often the same incident was recorded, or how many trips occurred overall. The river flooded on five recorded days; reports often, but not always, coincided with flooding. The data indicate problems worth investigating; they cannot establish a delay rate, a representative sample, or the least costly remedy.

EDITOR'S NOTE. Venn's argument uses opportunity costs and distributed burdens. Reed's argument compares policy options and demands a common baseline. Each makes a reasonable request while risking overstatement. A fair evaluator should ask both parties to define success, agree on the observation period, and treat unknown denominators as a reason for better evidence, not a reason to declare the problem imaginary.

Explicit instructional focus

Study claim, warrant, counterclaim, concession, sampling limitation, and relevance. Separate an argument's rhetorical presentation from the strength of its evidence. Compare possible actions using a common evaluation standard.

Worked rhetorical analysis

Venn's strongest point is that public accounts omit private time costs. Reed's strongest point is that alternatives should be compared. The report card supports concern but cannot prove a percentage of delayed crossings: the total trip count and distinct reporters are unknown.

Guided and independent writing

  1. Create a common-grounds and disagreement matrix, including a fair four-sentence summary for each writer.
  2. Explain why the fact sheet cannot establish a percentage of ferry trips delayed.
  3. Write a 170-word synthesis proposing a study design that could test both a bridge and an improved ferry service.

Reading self-check · Week 14

Can 140 voluntary reports establish the percentage of ferry journeys delayed?

Reveal the model response

No. The total number of trips is unknown; multiple reports may refer to one delay, and the reporters are not a representative random sample.

Practice only. This does not record a grade or alter the weekly assessment.

Teacher guidance and source ethics

Require readers to articulate the strongest counterargument before responding. Compare reasoning and evidence rather than choosing a favored policy on intuition.

WEEK 15 · ORIGINAL RHETORICAL READING

Two Writers, One Year — Contemporary Style and Context

Invented period-style instructional texts · not authenticated archival documents

FICTIONAL PERIODICAL ARCHIVE · Both pieces are presented as invented examples dated 1924 to support comparison of contemporaneous authors. They are not authentic newspaper transcriptions.

WRITER ONE · Mara Venn, public-address columnist. To speak of a town is to speak of motion. The classroom opens when the pupil arrives, the workshop opens when the worker does, and the future opens when opportunity can travel. Our river should not decide which bank participates first. A bridge would draw the banks into a single schedule, though only careful design could make that promise material. We must test what we claim, but we must also recognize whose hours our delay consumes.

WRITER TWO · Jonas Reed, accounts columnist. The first duty of a proposal is to define its result. If better access is the goal, name who requires it, at which hours, and under what conditions. The charm of a bridge is obvious; its efficiency is not. A reliable ferry timetable may achieve much of the same benefit at a lower cost, or it may fail utterly in flood season. We cannot tell until alternatives are compared with measurements made over the same period.

EDITOR'S FRAMING. Both authors wrote for a fictional town debating cross-river transport in the same invented year. Venn favors a feasibility study through images of connection, human cost, and parallel clauses. Reed favors comparative measurements through conditional sentences and definitions. Their disagreement does not prove that emotion and evidence are opposites: Venn requests records, and Reed acknowledges that travelers suffer inconvenience.

A later classroom reader notices how framing can influence interpretation. The headings call one an 'address' and the other 'accounts,' which may invite readers to hear passion in one and detachment in the other. Those labels are editorial choices. The reader must return to actual sentence structure and claims before deciding what each author values.

Explicit instructional focus

Contemporaneous means writing within the same historical period; it does not imply shared politics or identical literary form. Contrast repetition, metaphor, diction, parallelism, and conditional reasoning, using exact passages and explicit historical-fiction labels.

Worked rhetorical analysis

Venn repeats 'opens' to place access in a human frame. Reed begins with 'define its result,' establishing measurement as a condition for action. Both demand evidence, so describing one as purely emotional and the other as purely rational would ignore their actual sentences.

Guided and independent writing

  1. Build a six-row device/effect comparison with three quoted details from each author.
  2. Explain how the fictional 1924 setting and periodical labels frame expectations without proving historical authenticity.
  3. Write a comparative essay paragraph identifying shared concern and divergent methods, then note a limitation of this invented practice source.

Reading self-check · Week 15

Are the two pieces dated 1924 evidence of real historical events?

Reveal the model response

No. They are explicitly invented period-style texts. They can support literary comparison, but authentic historical claims require verified period sources.

Practice only. This does not record a grade or alter the weekly assessment.

Teacher guidance and source ethics

Pair with separately selected authentic speeches or essays from a real shared historical period. This invented archive trains comparison and does not fulfill independent historical-source authentication.

WEEK 16 · ORIGINAL FICTIONAL RHETORIC

A Public Promise Under Revision — Rhetorical Analysis Essay

Constructed source · not archival testimony

FICTIONAL SPEECH AT THE IMAGINARY RIVERFORD LEARNING FORUM: “A library can be a bridge, but not every bridge is open to every traveler. We built walls to shelter books, tables to welcome students, and doors wide enough for a crowd. Yet an open door at noon means little to a worker who arrives after closing. I ask this council to consider not only how many volumes we possess but how often a person may actually reach them. Access is a schedule, a route and a price as surely as it is a promise.” The invented speaker, Imani Bell, speaks to council members and residents.

Bell continues: “Our planning committee reports that the weekday room is occupied on most afternoons. That fact matters. It does not tell us who was turned away, nor whether the evening classes can be reached by bus. If we congratulate ourselves before asking those questions, the most polished report may become a mirror reflecting only those who were already inside.” The repetition of “does not” checks the original success claim. The mirror image turns a problem of data collection into a question about the ethics of public self-praise.

AN IMAGINED REPORTER'S FOLLOW-UP asks whether a longer opening schedule would cost money or reduce staffing elsewhere. Bell answers: “Yes. That is why I am asking for a public comparison of schedules, costs, accessibility and the number of people served before the vote.” The reporter also notes that Bell has not supplied attendance figures or a budget. A responsible analysis can explain the speech's strategic use of analogy, parallelism and audience values while also acknowledging that its effectiveness and feasibility remain questions.

Explicit instruction

Rhetorical analysis asks how choices respond to an audience, purpose, exigence and constraints; it is not an agreement essay. Evaluate the relationship between figurative framing and available evidence. Discuss ethos, syntax, repetition, concessions and the shift from ideal to operational definition.

Worked rhetorical analysis

A defensible thesis: Bell uses a bridge analogy and repeated negative clauses to redefine public access as measurable availability, then concedes costs to present investigation as a responsible next step. Cite the exact lines about noon and the committee's incomplete occupation report; do not claim the policy succeeded.

Guided and independent evidence

  1. Construct an evidence-to-effect chart with at least four language or structural choices.
  2. Draft a thesis and two body paragraphs explaining how choices work on distinct audiences.
  3. Revise one paragraph to distinguish intended effect from demonstrated audience response.

Misconception check: Rhetorical analysis is not a list of device labels or an argument for the policy itself.

Reading self-check · Week 16

Does recognizing a metaphor require claiming that it persuaded everyone?

Reveal the model response

No. The analysis may explain intended framing and possible effects without inventing measured reception.

Teacher guidance

Use the existing rhetorical analysis essay rubric; offer close-read annotation of syntactic moves and evidence scope.

WEEK 17 · ORIGINAL FICTIONAL RHETORIC

Seminar Transcript — Inquiry Instead of Performance

Constructed source · not archival testimony

FICTIONAL SEMINAR TRANSCRIPT, OPENING: Moderator Ada asks, “Is Bell's speech more ethical because she admits what the attendance data cannot show?” Tavi responds, “The concession sounds honest, and the metaphor helps an audience picture exclusion. But the speech gives no comparison of two schedules.” Noor replies, “A speaker can be credible in naming missing evidence even before a decision is possible.” Ada asks both readers to separate a judgment about the speech's rhetorical method from a judgment about the public policy proposal.

The discussion shifts. Tavi says that Bell's repetition of “does not” might leave the audience with anxiety rather than a plan. Noor points to the reporter's follow-up: Bell proposes comparing costs, access and service rather than presenting a fully designed policy. Ada summarizes fairly: “You agree that the speech identifies a data gap; you differ over whether its proposed procedure is sufficiently specific.” Rather than declare a winner, she asks what document or quotation could test each reading.

A participant who has said little adds: “The workers are discussed as an imagined audience, but no worker speaks in the source set.” The group revises its notes. They had been debating an absent perspective as though they could report it. For the next seminar, they propose inviting a real stakeholder or studying an ethically gathered firsthand account. Ada closes by naming three skills: source-specific quotation, accurate paraphrase of another participant's position, and a question that advances the inquiry.

Explicit instruction

An academic seminar requires equitable participation, active listening, fair restatement and evidence that can be checked. A participant's agreement with a policy is separate from an analysis of rhetorical effectiveness. Questions can seek a stronger definition, alternative evidence or an omitted viewpoint.

Worked rhetorical analysis

Model respectful response: 'I hear you arguing that Bell's concession builds credibility. I agree she marks missing evidence, but I question whether the proposed comparison has a defined timeline.' This restates fairly, cites a passage and advances the issue rather than attacking a speaker.

Guided and independent evidence

  1. Annotate the transcript for a fair restatement, evidence-based disagreement, and new question.
  2. Write three genuine open-ended seminar prompts that cannot be answered by one word.
  3. Prepare a two-minute rhetorical reading with evidence and a listening reflection in the original workspace.

Misconception check: Speaking most often or most confidently does not demonstrate intellectual leadership.

Reading self-check · Week 17

What makes a seminar contribution analytical instead of only persuasive?

Reveal the model response

It accurately represents another idea, refers to textual evidence, tests reasoning and makes space for an improved interpretation.

Teacher guidance

Provide written and spoken discussion routes; assess using the preexisting oral rubric and preserve the original learner record.

WEEK 18 · ORIGINAL FICTIONAL RHETORIC

The Midyear Evidence Conference — Transfer and Limitations

Constructed source · not archival testimony

FICTIONAL READER'S NOTE: In September, Eliot wrote that a speech was “effective because it used pathos.” Later, the same learner produced a paragraph identifying a speaker's audience, naming a repeated phrase and explaining how the phrase guided listeners toward a narrower proposal. The later analysis still called the audience “everyone” until a peer pointed out that the actual meeting record covered only people present. Eliot revised the audience description and added a limitation.

FICTIONAL CONFERENCE TRANSCRIPT: The teacher asks, “Which part of your method would transfer to an unfamiliar speech?” Eliot answers: “I would identify the claim and rhetorical situation before assigning device labels, then test each proposed effect against the actual words.” The teacher gives a short invented sentence for practice: “We have counted the seats, but we have not counted those left standing.” Eliot describes antithesis and a contrast between a convenient metric and uncounted people, but admits that no audience data or outcome is provided.

MIDYEAR PLAN: Eliot selects three artifacts—a baseline annotation, an analytical essay draft, and a seminar reflection—and documents one change made after feedback in each. The learner writes a next-step goal: choose stronger quotations and distinguish intended persuasion from actual effect. The class will complete the separate official midterm according to its own assessment rules. The conference offers a readiness check and a revision strategy; it is not an alternative exam, and the example sentence is not a leaked test passage.

Explicit instruction

Midyear academic evidence should demonstrate transfer, not simply a stack of finished assignments. Distinguish device naming, warranted effect analysis, ethical source reading, independent writing and oral inquiry. A cumulative exam still requires unfamiliar material and its original authorization.

Worked rhetorical analysis

Compare the baseline statement 'pathos makes it effective' with 'the repeated contrast between counted seats and uncounted people asks the audience to question the selected metric.' The second claim cites a choice and explains its possible function without asserting an observed effect.

Guided and independent evidence

  1. Write a three-artifact progression note: initial method, feedback, revision and transfer task.
  2. Apply a rhetorical situation/evidence/method/effect/limitation framework to an unfamiliar teacher-approved paragraph.
  3. Set two actionable revision goals and preserve them in the established portfolio before the official midterm.

Misconception check: A companion model response is not an authoritative midterm answer key.

Reading self-check · Week 18

Reveal the model response

No. Transfer across unfamiliar sources and modes, supported by multiple artifacts, is stronger evidence.

Teacher guidance

Maintain secure midterm procedures and direct teacher assessment of rhetoric, writing, oral reasoning and source integrity.
