Text A · Original extended literary narrative

A Margin Full of Questions

The archive reading room closed at four, and Imani had three quarters of an hour left. On the table before her were two accounts of a school's opening: a committee circular titled A Celebration for Every Household and a petition copied into an index that had not been updated for decades. The circular praised a new building and recorded the names of public officials. The petition asked for affordable transport and more evening access. Neither document described the other.

Imani began with a question about truth. If the committee had opened the school, did the petition prove the celebration dishonest? Her first margin note said yes. After reading again, she drew a line through it. A celebratory account could report a real opening while overlooking obstacles to attendance. What she knew was narrower: the documents had different purposes, audiences, and emphases.

The circular had been printed after the ceremony. Its introduction promised that the town had 'heard one united voice.' Imani circled one. The petition bore no complete date on the copy available to her. She could not establish whether it was written before the ceremony, after the ceremony, or in response to earlier plans. Nor did she know whether the people who signed the petition attended the celebration. She added question marks rather than arranging uncertain events into a smooth story.

Near the filing cabinet she met Tomas, who was preparing a community-history exhibition. He read her notes and suggested a sentence: 'The petition exposes the committee's deliberate deception.' Imani asked him what document would establish deliberateness. Tomas pointed to the committee's silence about transport. She replied that an omission might arise from purpose, limited knowledge, bias, or concealment. Without further evidence, those possibilities were not interchangeable.

They examined a third item, an editorial printed many years later. Its author described the school as the first step in a long, contested effort to make education accessible. The editorial offered useful historical distance, but no citation appeared beside its claim about the petition's date. Imani refused to import that date into her notes as fact. She recorded it instead as a secondary claim requiring verification.

Before leaving, she rewrote her thesis: 'The available accounts agree that the school opened, but they offer competing pictures of who could benefit from it; the committee's broad language exceeds the evidence provided by its commemorative circular.' She placed 'available accounts' at the beginning deliberately. The phrase acknowledged that documents not yet seen might complicate her reading.

She copied three quotations, preserving their original wording and identifying their sources. In the final space of her notebook she described a next step: locate a full dated petition, examine meeting minutes, and ask whose experiences had never been entered into the archive. She knew that a careful conclusion could be incomplete without being empty.

Read for focalization, claim scope, chronology, and the progression of Imani's thesis.

Text B · Fictional primary/secondary source simulation

Three Documents in Disagreement

ARCHIVE FOLDER 14 — FICTIONAL SOURCE SET. These three short documents were created for Academy instruction. They are not reproduced historical records and do not establish facts about an actual school.

Document 1, public committee circular (dated only 'Opening Week'): 'Our new classrooms stand ready. This achievement demonstrates the strength of a town moving forward in one united voice. The committee thanks those who contributed funds and labor.' The circular provides a ceremonial claim about the building, but it gives no survey of household opinion, no transport plan, and no attendance data.

Document 2, copied petition (date partly illegible): 'We welcome the chance for children to learn close to home. For several families, travel costs and the hours available for adult lessons remain difficult. We ask the committee to publish a practical plan for transport and evening access.' The copy shows the request, but not when it was delivered, how many households it represented, or whether any response followed.

Document 3, later editorial (written long after the opening): 'The school began with promise and disputes that took years to settle. The petition of the opening season reminds us that a new institution is not the same as universal access.' This later account offers an interpretation; its phrase 'opening season' remains unverified without a dated original.

Researcher's note: quotation marks in this dossier identify the fictional documents' exact practice wording. They are not permission to attribute them to a named historical person or to list them as external archival sources.

Read for provenance, primary versus later commentary, chronology, purpose, and omitted viewpoints.

Teacher-modeled analysis and paraphrase

Explicit text: The committee's circular uses the words "one united voice"; the petition asks for lower travel costs and adult access.

Inference: The public circular presents a more unified picture of the school opening than the petition does.

Qualification: The missing date on the petition means this dossier cannot establish the documents' exact chronological relationship.

Ethical paraphrase: Rather than copying the committee's celebratory language, write: "The circular portrays the opening as an achievement shared across the town (fictional Document 1)." That phrasing is distinct and still attributed. Do not convert fictional document names into real external citations.

Close-reading vocabulary

Provenance: when, why, and by whom a source was made.

Qualification: delimiting a claim to the evidence.

Corroboration: seeking independent evidence that bears on a claim.

Patchwriting: overly close borrowing of a source's structure or phrasing.

Misconception and understanding check

Misconception: a silence in a source is automatic proof of the author's intent.

Which of the fictional documents warrants claiming that the committee knowingly misled families?

Reveal the model response

None. The circular's wording is broad and the petition shows unresolved demands, but intent would require additional evidence such as correspondence, minutes, or testimony.

Teacher guidance, access, and formative criteria

For foundational support, divide Text A into three reading segments and mark source evidence with a color different from Imani's thoughts. For an extension, ask the learner to test whether the later editorial is a more comprehensive or merely more retrospective account. Permit oral rehearsal, audio rendering, and graphic organizers while preserving the same evidence target.

Evaluate: accuracy of attribution, faithful paraphrase, two textual anchors, a justified inference, an alternative interpretation, and a stated limitation. Provide feedback, but enter grades only through the existing course evaluation process.
