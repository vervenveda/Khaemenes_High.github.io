Paraphrase, Quotation, and Patchwriting

Credit borrowed ideas and language correctly while developing a genuinely independent analytical sentence.

Weekly Classroom & Saved WorkCourse HomePrevious DayNext Day

Read the original passage

A student essay used a striking sentence from an article but placed no quotation marks around it. The student had changed three words and thought that was sufficient. Her mentor asked her to trace both the idea and the phrasing. She rewrote the sentence in her own analytic language, cited the article, and placed the most precise original wording in quotation marks.

Read carefully. Highlight two exact details before forming a claim.

Explicit instruction

Patchwriting changes a few source words but retains the source's distinctive syntax or structure. A legitimate paraphrase communicates the idea in new language and a new sentence structure and still cites the source. A quotation preserves exact wording within quotation marks and identifies its source. Citation is necessary for a borrowed idea even when the language is original. The weekly story has no supplied real article; do not invent an author, page, or quotation.

Key language: paraphrase; quotation; patchwriting; attribution.

Worked reasoning model

For demonstration only, use this invented source sentence: “The committee delayed the vote because its records were incomplete.” A weak patchwrite says, “The group postponed the vote since its files weren't complete.” A responsible paraphrase can say, “Missing documentation prevented the committee from making a decision at that meeting (practice source).” The example citation is a placeholder for classroom instruction, not a publication reference.

Guided practice

Why does changing only three words fail to make borrowed prose independent? Identify both the structural borrowing and missing source credit.

Independent evidence

  1. Write an original three-sentence summary of the scenario without borrowing its phrasing.
  2. Draft an analytical claim about writing integrity supported by the scenario and clearly attributed.
  3. Explain the difference between quote, paraphrase, and original inference in your notes.

Misconception to correct

Changing a few synonyms is not sufficient paraphrasing; adding a citation does not turn copied syntax into independent prose.

Low-stakes understanding check

Does a correctly rewritten paraphrase of somebody else's idea need a citation?

Reveal and explain the model response

Yes, unless the idea is genuinely common knowledge in the relevant context.

Practice response only. It does not submit or alter course mastery.

Teacher guidance

Use an invented practice source so students cannot mistake a sample citation for an actual bibliographic record. Check whether students changed the structure and meaning, not merely word choice.
