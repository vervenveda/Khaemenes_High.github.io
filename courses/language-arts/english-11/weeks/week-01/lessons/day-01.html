Evidence, Audience, and Rhetorical Purpose

Distinguish the purposes and audiences of a public speech and a petition, and explain a textual contrast without inventing motive.

Weekly Classroom & Saved WorkCourse HomeNext Day

Read the original passage

The mayor's speech described the new school as proof of unanimous civic progress. In a file box, Anika found a petition written before the ceremony asking for lower fees and safer roads. The petition did not reject the school; it asked that the promise of access become practical. Anika resisted calling the speech dishonest without further evidence. Instead, she wrote that public celebration had concealed unresolved demands.

Read carefully. Highlight two exact details before forming a claim.

Explicit instruction

A source does more than state facts. Its genre, audience, and rhetorical situation shape which facts are foregrounded. The mayor's speech celebrates an achievement; the petition seeks changes necessary for people to benefit from it. Neither purpose proves the speaker is truthful or deceitful. When evaluating rhetoric, separate what a text explicitly claims from the interpretation that claim can reasonably support.

Key language: rhetorical situation; audience; purpose; qualification.

Worked reasoning model

Model annotation: Underline “unanimous civic progress” and label it a broad public claim. Underline “lower fees and safer roads” and label those concrete access barriers. Then write: “While the speech presents the school as a shared success, the earlier petition identifies barriers that the celebratory account leaves unresolved.” This is defensible because both observations appear in the story; it does not claim the mayor knowingly lied.

Guided practice

Which words characterize the speech as broad or sweeping? Which details make the petition more specific? Explain why a petition need not oppose the school itself.

Independent evidence

  1. Construct a two-column chart listing each document's audience, purpose, and quoted evidence.
  2. Write one 90–130-word analytical paragraph with a qualified claim and two precise textual references.
  3. Label one inference you cannot verify without obtaining the actual speech and petition.

Misconception to correct

Do not treat missing information as proof of deliberate deception. A gap in a source is a reason to seek corroboration.

Low-stakes understanding check

Can two sources disagree in emphasis without contradicting each other on every factual point?

Reveal and explain the model response

Yes. One can celebrate a school's opening while another documents the costs and access problems associated with it.

Practice response only. It does not submit or alter course mastery.

Teacher guidance

Read the original story aloud; model two different annotation colors for explicit statement and inference. Foundation learners use a claim/evidence/reasoning frame. Extended learners evaluate the limits of a fictional narrative representing two unseen documents.
