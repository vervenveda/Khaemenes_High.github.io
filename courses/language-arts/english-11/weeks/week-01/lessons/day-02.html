Provenance and Editorial Framing

Distinguish primary-source language from later editorial annotation and assess how provenance changes an interpretation.

Weekly Classroom & Saved WorkCourse HomePrevious DayNext Day

Read the original passage

An archived letter was dated September, but the editor who published it had added a note twenty years later. Eli first treated both statements as the author's words. On rereading, he noticed the different handwriting and the editor's initials. The distinction changed his analysis: one voice described an event, while another attempted to explain it for a later audience.

Read carefully. Highlight two exact details before forming a claim.

Explicit instruction

Provenance means tracing where a statement came from, who produced it, when, and for whom. A later editor may provide valuable context, but the editor's notes are not automatically the original writer's claims. Separate primary evidence from secondary interpretation before you quote either. The twenty-year gap matters: the editor can know later outcomes the letter's writer could not have known.

Key language: provenance; primary source; editorial frame; attribution.

Worked reasoning model

Draw a three-column record: “September letter / original author / contemporaneous account”; “editor's note / editor / twenty years later”; “my interpretation / learner / present day.” Sample analysis: “Because the note was written by an editor two decades later, it cannot establish what the letter writer intended at the time.” State that handwriting and initials are clues, not independently authenticated proof.

Guided practice

Which feature most strongly signals that the marginal note may have a different author: its topic, its initials, or your prior expectation? What further evidence could confirm its provenance?

Independent evidence

  1. Create a provenance table for the letter and editorial note, including who/when/genre/uncertainty.
  2. Draft a four-sentence paragraph distinguishing original claim, editorial explanation, and your own inference.
  3. Propose two verification steps, such as comparing signed editions or locating archive metadata.

Misconception to correct

A note printed on the same page is not necessarily part of the original primary source.

Low-stakes understanding check

What should a quotation from the later editor be attributed to?

Reveal and explain the model response

To the editor, not to the original letter writer; include the date or edition where possible.

Practice response only. It does not submit or alter course mastery.

Teacher guidance

Show how to distinguish author, editor, publisher, and narrator. Avoid claiming fictional handwriting is definitive authentication; discuss archival corroboration.
