WEEK 25 · UNIT 09

Literary Research Question & Scope

Primary text fragment · course-created

A narrator repeatedly describes a city bridge as “unfinished” even after engineers declare construction complete. In the final scene, the bridge is physically intact but the narrator refuses to cross it.

Background source A

A fictional historical note describes the bridge as a postwar reconstruction project celebrated in local newspapers as a symbol of renewal.

Critical source B

A fictional literary critic argues that incomplete structures in the novel represent private grief rather than public history.

Worked inquiry

Weak question: “What does the bridge symbolize?” Stronger question: “How does the novel’s changing description of the reconstructed bridge complicate the public language of renewal, and how far does the grief interpretation explain the narrator’s refusal to cross?”

Independent task

Develop a literary research question anchored in a primary-text pattern and a genuine interpretive tension. Define corpus, contextual scope, source types and one question your project will leave open.

WEEK 26 · UNIT 09

Primary, Secondary, Quantitative & Visual Evidence

Source taxonomy

Primary literary text: the work being interpreted. Primary contextual source: contemporaneous speech, letter, photograph or record. Secondary source: later scholarship or criticism. Quantitative/visual evidence may illuminate context but does not automatically explain literary meaning.

Source C · fictional archive photograph

A captioned image shows the bridge reopening ceremony with banners reading “A City Rejoined.” The photograph documents public presentation, not the private beliefs of every resident.

Source D · fictional demographic table

A table shows neighborhood population shifts over twenty years. It may help establish context, but using it to claim the novelist’s intention would require additional evidence.

Worked evaluation

The critic is directly relevant to interpretation; the photograph helps analyze public rhetoric; the table may contextualize change. Each source belongs for a different reason, and each has a different evidentiary limit.

Independent task

Evaluate four source types for authority, relevance, method, proximity to the literary question, and limits. Write an annotation explaining exactly how each source could—and could not—enter your argument.

WEEK 27 · UNIT 09

Synthesis Without Surrendering Interpretation

Scholarly disagreement

Critic 1 reads the bridge as grief; Critic 2 reads it as distrust of civic triumph; a contextual source documents renewal rhetoric but does not interpret the novel.

Worked synthesis

Do not write one paragraph per critic. Organize around the interpretive problem: where grief and public-history readings overlap, where textual details favor one, and what neither explains. The student’s claim should emerge from that comparison rather than merely selecting a critic.

Integration model

“The grief reading explains the narrator’s private avoidance, but the repeated word ‘unfinished’ gains additional force beside the city’s public claim that it has been ‘rejoined.’ The novel therefore makes personal grief and civic recovery mutually complicating rather than mutually exclusive.”

Independent task

Create a synthesis matrix with claim, evidence, method, agreement, disagreement, limitation and your response. Draft one integrated paragraph containing primary evidence, one secondary source, one contextual source and your own reasoning.
