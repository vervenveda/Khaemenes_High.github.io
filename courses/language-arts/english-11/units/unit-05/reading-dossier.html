WEEK 13 · ORIGINAL RHETORICAL READING

A Bridge Is More Than Stone — Rhetorical Address

Invented period-style instructional texts · not authenticated archival documents

FICTIONAL ADDRESS · Mara Venn, 1924 · Invented period-style source for classroom analysis. Fellow residents: A bridge is more than stone. It is a decision about whose journey matters. At the western bank, a worker waits for the first morning ferry and misses an hour of wages when the river is high. At the eastern bank, a pupil waits for an evening crossing and reaches home after dark. We measure the river in yards; we ought also to measure the time the river takes from its neighbors.

I have heard that a bridge will be costly. I agree. We should publish the estimate and name its uncertainty. I have heard that the ferries have served us for years. They have. But a service that works on quiet mornings may fail when floodwater rises. Shall we call an old arrangement fair simply because it is familiar? Shall we call an expense unnecessary simply because the people who pay it do not all endure the delay?

We need a bridge not for one trade but for several; not for one neighborhood but for every bank; not as a monument to our generosity but as a practical route open to examination. Let the engineer show the calculations. Let the treasurer show the accounts. Let the school show who arrives late and how that number was counted. If the evidence weakens our proposal, revise the proposal. If it supports the need, do not hide behind a further year of convenient postponement.

I do not promise that a bridge will solve every hardship in this district. Houses will remain far from workplaces, and some travelers will still need transport to the crossing. I ask for a public study with clear costs, accessible routes, and an honest vote. A town cannot cross a river on eloquence; it can only decide whether eloquence will be followed by work.

Explicit instructional focus

Identify anaphora in 'Let the...', antithesis in 'not for one...but...', rhetorical questions, and the extended metaphor of a bridge as civic connection. Analyze what each device does rather than merely naming it. This is an invented address, not a historical source.

Worked rhetorical analysis

The repetition of 'Let the' distributes responsibility among engineer, treasurer, and school. That syntax makes the speaker's appeal seem accountable to multiple forms of evidence rather than resting on emotion alone. Its limit is that none of the documents is actually supplied within this fictional speech.

Guided and independent writing

  1. Select three rhetorical devices, quote each exactly, explain their effects, and evaluate whether they support the claim.
  2. Analyze how the speaker concedes cost and imperfect outcomes without abandoning advocacy.
  3. Revise a short paragraph by removing one rhetorical device and explaining the change in persuasive force.
Teacher guidance and source ethics

Ask students to distinguish a rhetorical device from a logical warrant. The 1924 label is fictional; do not treat it as authentic archival evidence or use it for real historical sourcing.

WEEK 14 · ORIGINAL RHETORICAL READING

A Bridge and Its Alternatives — Competing Civic Arguments

Invented period-style instructional texts · not authenticated archival documents

TEXT A · Short editorial attributed to fictional Mara Venn. Delaying a formal feasibility study imposes costs even when the town writes no check. A ferry passenger who loses working hours bears a cost outside the public ledger. A family whose child arrives home late bears another. These burdens do not settle the bridge question, but they make it irresponsible to consider construction costs alone. Commission a comparative study and publish the assumptions behind its recommendations.

TEXT B · Response attributed to fictional accountant Jonas Reed. The delays described by Venn deserve attention, but a bridge is not the only possible remedy. A scheduled ferry with safer evening service, better landing ramps, and published reliability figures might help travelers sooner and for less money. No credible cost comparison has been produced for either plan. When the advocate calls another year 'convenient postponement,' she risks presenting a demand for alternatives as indifference to people who wait.

TEXT C · Joint fact sheet drafted for the fictional public meeting. Last quarter, travelers logged 140 late ferry arrivals on a voluntary report card. The organizers do not know how many distinct passengers submitted reports, how often the same incident was recorded, or how many trips occurred overall. The river flooded on five recorded days; reports often, but not always, coincided with flooding. The data indicate problems worth investigating; they cannot establish a delay rate, a representative sample, or the least costly remedy.

EDITOR'S NOTE. Venn's argument uses opportunity costs and distributed burdens. Reed's argument compares policy options and demands a common baseline. Each makes a reasonable request while risking overstatement. A fair evaluator should ask both parties to define success, agree on the observation period, and treat unknown denominators as a reason for better evidence, not a reason to declare the problem imaginary.

Explicit instructional focus

Study claim, warrant, counterclaim, concession, sampling limitation, and relevance. Separate an argument's rhetorical presentation from the strength of its evidence. Compare possible actions using a common evaluation standard.

Worked rhetorical analysis

Venn's strongest point is that public accounts omit private time costs. Reed's strongest point is that alternatives should be compared. The report card supports concern but cannot prove a percentage of delayed crossings: the total trip count and distinct reporters are unknown.

Guided and independent writing

  1. Create a common-grounds and disagreement matrix, including a fair four-sentence summary for each writer.
  2. Explain why the fact sheet cannot establish a percentage of ferry trips delayed.
  3. Write a 170-word synthesis proposing a study design that could test both a bridge and an improved ferry service.
Teacher guidance and source ethics

Require readers to articulate the strongest counterargument before responding. Compare reasoning and evidence rather than choosing a favored policy on intuition.

WEEK 15 · ORIGINAL RHETORICAL READING

Two Writers, One Year — Contemporary Style and Context

Invented period-style instructional texts · not authenticated archival documents

FICTIONAL PERIODICAL ARCHIVE · Both pieces are presented as invented examples dated 1924 to support comparison of contemporaneous authors. They are not authentic newspaper transcriptions.

WRITER ONE · Mara Venn, public-address columnist. To speak of a town is to speak of motion. The classroom opens when the pupil arrives, the workshop opens when the worker does, and the future opens when opportunity can travel. Our river should not decide which bank participates first. A bridge would draw the banks into a single schedule, though only careful design could make that promise material. We must test what we claim, but we must also recognize whose hours our delay consumes.

WRITER TWO · Jonas Reed, accounts columnist. The first duty of a proposal is to define its result. If better access is the goal, name who requires it, at which hours, and under what conditions. The charm of a bridge is obvious; its efficiency is not. A reliable ferry timetable may achieve much of the same benefit at a lower cost, or it may fail utterly in flood season. We cannot tell until alternatives are compared with measurements made over the same period.

EDITOR'S FRAMING. Both authors wrote for a fictional town debating cross-river transport in the same invented year. Venn favors a feasibility study through images of connection, human cost, and parallel clauses. Reed favors comparative measurements through conditional sentences and definitions. Their disagreement does not prove that emotion and evidence are opposites: Venn requests records, and Reed acknowledges that travelers suffer inconvenience.

A later classroom reader notices how framing can influence interpretation. The headings call one an 'address' and the other 'accounts,' which may invite readers to hear passion in one and detachment in the other. Those labels are editorial choices. The reader must return to actual sentence structure and claims before deciding what each author values.

Explicit instructional focus

Contemporaneous means writing within the same historical period; it does not imply shared politics or identical literary form. Contrast repetition, metaphor, diction, parallelism, and conditional reasoning, using exact passages and explicit historical-fiction labels.

Worked rhetorical analysis

Venn repeats 'opens' to place access in a human frame. Reed begins with 'define its result,' establishing measurement as a condition for action. Both demand evidence, so describing one as purely emotional and the other as purely rational would ignore their actual sentences.

Guided and independent writing

  1. Build a six-row device/effect comparison with three quoted details from each author.
  2. Explain how the fictional 1924 setting and periodical labels frame expectations without proving historical authenticity.
  3. Write a comparative essay paragraph identifying shared concern and divergent methods, then note a limitation of this invented practice source.
Teacher guidance and source ethics

Pair with separately selected authentic speeches or essays from a real shared historical period. This invented archive trains comparison and does not fulfill independent historical-source authentication.
