WEEK 19 · ORIGINAL FORMAL EXERCISES

Civic Language and the Limits of Public Praise

Original modern classroom poems · not archival, historical or translated primary sources

Poem A · At the Dedication (original fictional civic lyric)

The stone was named a doorway, Its builders called it ours. A ribbon crossed the entrance And caught the morning showers. The mayor read a roll of names; The crowd supplied applause. Behind the new-cut staircase A neighbor studied flaws: Her father used a walking frame; The ramp was still a plan. The doorway stood before them, But who could enter then?

Poem B · The Clerk's Reply (original fictional response lyric)

I counted those who came to see, Not those the steps kept out. A count can serve a narrow claim And still conceal a doubt. If we shall call this building ours, The name must reach the gate; Let not the praises for the stone Become the price of wait. The minutes hold our first applause; They must record the question too: What good is public language Unless its promise reaches you?

Explicit literary-historical instruction

Poetry can respond to civic issues through dramatic perspective, rhetorical address, imagery, omissions and formal choices. A text's imagined occasion does not establish a real historical event; historical interpretation of an authentic poem requires dated publication, reliable authorship and contextual sources. Here Poem A juxtaposes ceremonial language with a barrier, while Poem B revises the act of counting. Analyze each poem's implied audience, how its diction positions readers, and whether its challenge is to a shared civic goal or to the implementation of that goal.

Worked contextual close reading

The mayor's collective “ours” is complicated by the neighbor's observation that the ramp remains only “a plan.” The poem does not declare that every attendee was insincere. The reply's “I counted those who came to see, / Not those the steps kept out” exposes sampling limits. A comparative claim: the first lyric dramatizes a contradiction between ceremonial access and physical access, while the second uses parallel syntax and a direct question to propose accountability. Both are fictional practice poems; evidence of historical policy would require genuine records.

Guided and independent comparison

Who is represented inside the ceremony, and who is missing from the count? Mark one instance of shared-language rhetoric and one question that complicates its reach.

  1. Write a 180-word comparative close reading with two line-level references from each original poem.
  2. Separate what can be interpreted from the poems from what would require authentic historical testimony.
  3. Choose an educator-approved historical poem on a public issue and record its writer, publication date, intended audience and reliable source without assuming these invented poems are period documents.

Misconception and provenance self-check

A fictional poem written in a historical style is not evidence for an actual period's public attitudes.

Does the repeated word “ours” prove the building was accessible to every citizen?

No. Its rhetorical inclusiveness is challenged by the described missing ramp; the passage asks whether the language's implied access matches the scene.

Teacher guidance

Connect literary close reading to provenance. When assigning genuine poetry, distinguish a poet's published wording from classroom imitation. Offer an evidence table and oral analysis alternatives.

WEEK 20 · ORIGINAL FORMAL EXERCISES

Form, Inheritance and the Danger of Easy Labels

Original modern classroom poems · not archival, historical or translated primary sources

Poem A · Garden Argument (original formal exercise)

Within the wall a careful garden grew, Each path prescribed what feet were free to take; The gardener called the measured border true, And asked the wild vine not to climb or break. Yet in the seam between the stones a seed Found light the plotted beds had not supplied. The gardener paused beside that wordless need And wondered where his orderly paths had lied. He did not pull the thriving tendril down; He drew a second gate into the wall. A map may serve a garden, not a crown; No single path was meant to hold us all.

Poem B · Margins of the Chronicle (original irregular stanza)

In the copied book someone drew a small door beneath the king's long list of titles. No clerk explained the mark. The margin had no seal, only the dust of another hand. I read the names aloud, then read the space beside them; one was an order, one a question the parchment kept. Neither the ink nor silence would tell me whose voice had been left out.

Explicit literary-historical instruction

Formal English verse can use meter, patterned rhyme, argument, and volta, while other poems rely on irregular line lengths and syntactic movement. Such choices do not belong exclusively to a single period; do not infer exact chronology from a surface feature. Classical, medieval, Renaissance, British and colonial traditions span languages, regions, centuries, and unequal power relations. A historical study must use authentic texts with verified context and, where translation is involved, explain the translation's status. These two newly written poems are comparative exercises, not documents from any listed tradition.

Worked contextual close reading

Poem A uses a mostly patterned end rhyme and a turning point when the gardener notices the seed, so its structure helps enact a revision of authority. In Poem B, the clerk's records and the marginal drawing are contrasting sources whose creators cannot be identified from the poem. A responsible analysis might compare how both poems use bounded spaces—a garden wall and a book's margin—to frame a challenge to authority without claiming that the second is genuinely medieval or the first genuinely Renaissance.

Guided and independent comparison

Identify the argumentative turn in the garden poem and the textual uncertainty in the manuscript poem. Which structural choices might be useful in a genuine poem from a specific historical tradition?

  1. Annotate the rhyme pattern of Poem A, noting any slant or imperfect endings rather than inventing regularity.
  2. Compare the image of the wall with the book's margin in a 170–220-word paragraph.
  3. With an approved authentic poem, write a contextual note supported by a reliable edition: author, time, culture, genre, and any translation or editorial mediation.

Misconception and provenance self-check

Not every rhymed lyric is a sonnet, and not every manuscript image automatically belongs to a particular historical culture.

Why can't Poem B be used as proof that a medieval clerk drew a dissenting image?

It is an original fictional practice poem; it tells a made-up scene and has no archival provenance.

Teacher guidance

Invite close formal analysis first and historically grounded comparison second. Avoid compressing disparate poetic traditions into a single Western chronology or assigning cultural authenticity based on stylistic imitation.

WEEK 21 · ORIGINAL FORMAL EXERCISES

What Form Can Do with a City Scene

Original modern classroom poems · not archival, historical or translated primary sources

Poem A · Night Shift Inventory (original observation lyric)

At closing, Lena swept the floor: three paper cups, a ticket stub, a receipt that named a price but not the hands that paid it. The clock above the loading door ran seven minutes slow. Across the street a window shone; a worker folded up a coat. No omen moved the stars tonight. A bus arrived with weary brakes, and every person carried home a different portion of the day.

Poem B · Transit / Fragment (original modernist-style exercise)

fare paid— not the distance window: a city cut into squares her hands the folded timetable / a minute unprinted / announcement: THE NEXT SERVICE IS DELAYED outside, rain edits all departures

Explicit literary-historical instruction

Realist writing may foreground observed detail and ordinary social life; naturalist writing can examine environmental and material constraints. Modernist and contemporary poets use a range of experimental approaches to perspective, fragmentation, typographic space and disrupted sequence. These are analytical categories, not single inflexible checklists. The two classroom poems offer contrast in technique but cannot prove how historical movements actually developed. To make historical claims, compare authenticated poems, dates and critical scholarship from the relevant period.

Worked contextual close reading

“Night Shift Inventory” supplies continuous narrative observation and restrained objects: cups, ticket, receipt, and a slow clock. Its specificity invites social interpretation without naming a full socioeconomic theory. “Transit / Fragment” instead isolates ticketing, visual frames, a timetable, and a loud announcement in broken sequence. The slashes and capitalized delay interrupt the reader. A grounded comparison says that the second poem fragments an experience the first conveys through connected observation; whether either evokes a movement remains an interpretive analogy, not documented membership.

Guided and independent comparison

Compare how the reader encounters time and social routine in each poem. Which gives a stable narrated scene, and which asks you to assemble pieces?

  1. Create a structural annotation noting typography, line breaks, point of view, image and narrative continuity.
  2. Write a 200-word comparison supported by at least four brief quotations from the two poems.
  3. Select authentic poems from two different periods with established publication dates and write a separate sourced comparison between form and historical context.

Misconception and provenance self-check

Fragmented lines do not automatically make a poem Modernist, and detailed description does not automatically make it Naturalist.

What feature creates a distinct reading interruption in “Transit / Fragment”?

The isolated slashes, stanza gaps, and capitalized announcement interrupt syntactic and visual continuity, mirroring an interrupted journey.

Teacher guidance

Assess precise literary description before movement labels. For genuine period comparison require a bibliographic record and attention to differences among regions, voices and literary histories.
