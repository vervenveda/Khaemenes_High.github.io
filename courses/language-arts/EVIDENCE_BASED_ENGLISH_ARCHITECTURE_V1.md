# Khaemenes Academy High School English 9–12
## Evidence-Based Learning Architecture v1.0

**Adopted:** 2026-11-15  
**Scope:** English 9, English 10, English 11, English 12  
**Purpose:** Reorganize lesson delivery around well-supported findings from cognitive science, adolescent literacy research, secondary writing research, multimedia learning, and metacognition while preserving existing curriculum, assessments, learner records, and grade-specific academic rigor.

## Evidence boundary

No instructional design is scientifically guaranteed to increase every learner's capacity. This standard adopts practices with substantial empirical support for improving retention, transfer, comprehension, writing, and self-regulated learning under appropriate conditions. Teacher judgment, accessibility needs, prior knowledge, text quality, feedback quality, and implementation fidelity remain important.

## Research basis

1. **Retrieval practice / practice testing.** Roediger & Karpicke (2006); Karpicke & Blunt (2011); Adesope, Trevisan & Sundararajan (2017); Dunlosky et al. (2013). Retrieval is used as learning, not merely grading. Feedback follows retrieval.
2. **Spacing and cumulative review.** Cepeda et al. (2006); Pashler et al. / IES (2007); Dunlosky et al. (2013). Important knowledge returns after delays rather than appearing once and disappearing.
3. **Interleaving.** IES (2007); classroom meta-analysis (Educational Psychology Review, 2026). Previously learned reading, writing, language, rhetoric, and source-evaluation skills are mixed into later work where discriminating among strategies matters.
4. **Cognitive load / worked examples.** Sweller, Paas, van Gog and related cognitive-load research. New or complex work begins with an explicit model or worked exemplar before support is faded.
5. **Multimedia learning.** Mayer and colleagues: coherence, signaling, spatial contiguity, temporal contiguity, segmenting and pre-training. Pages remove avoidable distraction, visibly signal structure, place related information together, and divide complex material into learner-paced sections.
6. **Adolescent literacy.** IES/WWC, *Improving Adolescent Literacy* (2008): strong evidence for explicit vocabulary and comprehension-strategy instruction; moderate evidence for extended discussion of text meaning/interpretation and literacy engagement.
7. **Secondary writing.** IES/WWC, *Teaching Secondary Students to Write Effectively* (2016; revised 2019): strong evidence for explicit writing strategies through **Model → Practice → Reflect**; integrate reading and writing; use writing assessment to guide instruction and feedback.
8. **Metacognition and self-regulation.** EEF evidence syntheses: explicitly teach planning, monitoring, and evaluation within normal curriculum; model thinking aloud; make success criteria visible.
9. **Generative learning.** Fiorella & Mayer: self-explanation, teaching, mapping, drawing and other sense-making activities are used when they directly serve the learning goal.
10. **Feedback.** Feedback should answer: Where am I going? How am I going? What is the next useful action? It should target task/process/self-regulation more than praise alone.

## Canonical page principle

**A beautiful Academy page is cognitively quiet.**

The page must make the learning path obvious without forcing the student to decode the interface. Decorative material must not compete with the instructional goal.

### Required visual hierarchy

1. Academy chrome: one canonical header, required tickers, breadcrumb, one lower-right ecosystem toolbar.
2. Course/week identity.
3. Essential question and concise learning target.
4. **Learning cycle strip:** Recall → Model → Practice → Apply → Reflect.
5. Assigned reading/source adjacent to the task that uses it.
6. One lesson/day at a time in a stable order.
7. Quiz/mastery after instruction and application, never as a substitute for produced evidence.
8. Reflection and next step.
9. Support/extension content collapsed until requested unless required for access.

### Page design rules

- Body/control text floor: **16 px**.
- Section title: **33 px Cinzel**, weight 400.
- Primary page/course title: **66 px Cinzel** where appropriate.
- Body: Brandon Grotesque / Nunito-compatible fallback, weight <= 444.
- Normal reading measure: approximately **60–80 characters per line**; avoid very wide prose.
- Use whitespace, headings, short paragraphs, and nearby labels to signal organization.
- Do not duplicate spoken narration as dense on-screen prose when a graphic or demonstration already carries the same information.
- Graphics must explain something; decorative graphics cannot interrupt the instructional sequence.
- Related text and visual evidence stay together.
- Large optional resource collections remain collapsed or off the main cognitive path.
- No completion control may imply academic mastery unless the authoritative assessment/evaluator has established mastery.

## Universal daily cognitive sequence

Every normal lesson should implement these stages in order.

### 1. RETRIEVE · Recall before looking
A low-stakes prompt requires the student to reconstruct relevant prior learning before reopening notes. Monday may use a prequestion or prior-unit retrieval prompt.

Purpose: retrieval practice, spacing, attention to knowledge gaps.

### 2. ORIENT · Goal, vocabulary, context
State what the student will learn or produce, why it matters, and the small set of vocabulary/background knowledge needed to begin.

Purpose: pre-training, vocabulary, cognitive-load management.

### 3. MODEL · Show expert thinking
Provide a worked example, mentor text, annotated excerpt, think-aloud, or modeled decision. The model must expose reasoning, not just the final answer.

Purpose: worked examples, explicit strategy instruction.

### 4. PRACTICE · Guided attempt
Student attempts the target with prompts, cues, partial models, or feedback. Guidance fades as proficiency rises.

Purpose: deliberate practice with feedback.

### 5. APPLY · Independent transfer
Student applies the skill to new text, evidence, audience, question, or writing problem.

Purpose: transfer and independent performance.

### 6. EXPLAIN · Make thinking visible
Student explains a choice, inference, revision, interpretation, or process in their own words.

Purpose: generative learning and metacognition.

### 7. CHECK · Feedback and correction
Provide answer feedback where objective; for writing/interpretation provide rubric-aligned self/peer/teacher review. Errors become correction tasks.

Purpose: prevent fluent errors and guide the next attempt.

### 8. EXIT · Retrieve again
Close notes. State the central learning, evidence, misconception corrected, and next target from memory; then check/correct.

Purpose: retrieval, self-monitoring, consolidation.

## Weekly sequence

- **Monday — Build schema:** prequestion, context, vocabulary, explicit model.
- **Tuesday — Read closely:** retrieval, evidence discrimination, comprehension monitoring.
- **Wednesday — Compose / create:** mentor model, planning, drafting, explanation.
- **Thursday — Discuss / test / revise:** argumentation, feedback, counterreading, substantive revision.
- **Friday — Retrieve / transfer / master:** cumulative retrieval, unfamiliar transfer task, weekly quiz, corrections, portfolio evidence, reflection.

Weekly quizzes remain short formative evidence. Where the standard requires writing, speaking, research, analysis, or production, mastery requires the corresponding student artifact/evaluator evidence in addition to objective quiz performance.

## Spacing schedule

Key ideas should be revisited through brief retrieval approximately:
- next lesson,
- about one week later,
- about three to four weeks later,
- at the next unit boundary,
- at cumulative midterm/final preparation.

Exact timing may vary with complexity and learner need. Do not turn the schedule into repetitive busywork.

## Grade-by-grade fading of guidance

### English 9 — High structure
- Explicit stage labels and worked models.
- Vocabulary/context pre-training.
- Guided annotation and sentence/paragraph frames when helpful.
- Retrieval prompts name the expected components.
- Frequent checks for literal understanding before inference.
- Corrections explicitly model error diagnosis.

### English 10 — Moderate structure
- Preserve explicit model and guided practice, but reduce sentence-level prompting.
- Require comparison, source credibility, rhetorical choice, and multi-paragraph reasoning.
- Retrieval increasingly mixes current and previous-unit skills.

### English 11 — Advanced guided independence
- Shorter prompts; student selects evidence and strategy more often.
- Competing interpretations, rhetoric, synthesis, counterargument, research method.
- Models emphasize expert decisions and tradeoffs rather than templates.
- Reflection asks the learner to evaluate strategy effectiveness.

### English 12 — Independent scholarship
- Scaffolds become optional supports rather than default instructions.
- Student sets subgoals, chooses method, justifies sources/evidence, and identifies uncertainty.
- Retrieval and interleaving draw across prior years where appropriate.
- Capstone, oral defense, sustained argument, revision history, and ethical disclosure establish senior mastery.

**Fading support never means removing the stable learning cycle.** Grade 12 still retrieves, models when needed, practices, applies, receives feedback, corrects, and reflects.

## Reading architecture

Every required reading record should eventually contain:
- title and author/creator;
- exact passage/chapter/selection;
- provenance/licensing/public-domain status or approved access route;
- local/offline alternative where legally possible;
- brief context and essential vocabulary;
- passage-specific questions;
- at least one evidence-based written response;
- accessibility route;
- source attribution.

A label such as “literary passage” or “teacher-selected text” is not complete reading custody.

## Writing architecture

Use the IES **Model → Practice → Reflect** cycle throughout:
- analyze strong and weaker exemplars;
- model planning, drafting, evidence integration, evaluation, revision, and editing;
- practice the strategy on authentic course content;
- produce substantial writing;
- receive task/process feedback;
- revise meaningfully;
- explain what changed and why.

## Assessment architecture

- Prequestions are low stakes.
- Retrieval checks are learning events and normally ungraded or lightly weighted.
- Weekly objective quiz: 80% mastery target with correction/retry.
- Writing/speaking/research: rubric/evaluator evidence required.
- Repeated identical generic quiz items are prohibited.
- Retakes preserve the same learning target but should not reward memorizing answer position.
- Completion clicks record workflow only, never mastery.

## Accessibility and learner control

- Support options remain available without lowering the reasoning target.
- Complex multimedia is segmented and learner-controlled.
- Text alternatives preserve instructional meaning.
- Keyboard navigation, visible focus, labels, contrast, reduced-motion support, print usability, and responsive layout are required.
- A learner may use audio, text-to-speech, chunked text, extended time, oral rehearsal, or assistive technology without losing access to the same core reasoning goal.

## Implementation boundary — v1

The first implementation is a **progressive enhancement layer** over existing weekly English pages:
- inserts a compact learning-cycle map;
- inserts cumulative retrieval prompts;
- marks existing instructional sections as Model / Practice / Apply / Evidence;
- adds a brief exit retrieval prompt;
- tightens reading measure and visual signaling;
- keeps support/extension in progressive disclosure;
- varies prompt scaffolding by grade;
- does **not** rewrite course content, change learner storage, change grades, change quiz keys, or auto-award mastery.

Content-specific worked exemplars, spaced-review question banks, and reading-custody completion are subsequent curriculum-hardening passes.

## Validation target

A passing weekly page should let a student answer, without hunting:
1. What am I learning?
2. What do I already remember?
3. Where is the model?
4. Where do I practice with support?
5. Where do I apply it independently?
6. What evidence must I preserve?
7. How will I know what I got wrong?
8. What will I retrieve later?

That is the Khaemenes Academy English evidence-based lesson standard.
