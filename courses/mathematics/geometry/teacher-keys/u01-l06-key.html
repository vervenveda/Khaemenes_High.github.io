Learning intention

Use accurate diagrams, definitions, measurements, and reasoning to build a proof from givens through definitions, postulates, and theorems while comparing proof formats.

Success evidence

  • Label the figure and units before calculating.
  • Select and justify the governing definition, theorem, relationship, or construction.
  • Show enough work that another learner can audit the conclusion.
  • Check the result against the diagram, scale, and stated constraints.

Visual model

Worked reasoning

Process: State what is given, translate each fact into a definition or theorem, and justify every conclusion in order.

  1. Read the figure and rewrite all given information with correct notation.
  2. Identify the exact relationship that connects the known and unknown quantities.
  3. Substitute, construct, or prove one justified step at a time.
  4. State the result with units and a sentence explaining what it means.

Misconception check: A diagram suggests relationships, but only stated markings, definitions, and proven results may be used as evidence.

Name
Date
1

Two complementary angles measure 70° and y°. Find y.

Answer: 20
Reasoning: Complementary angles sum to 90°, so y=90−70.
2

Two supplementary angles measure 137° and y°. Find y.

Answer: 43
Reasoning: Supplementary angles sum to 180°, so y=180−137.
3

Name the angle formed by opposite rays.

Answer: A straight angle
Reasoning: Opposite rays form a 180° angle.
4

What is the difference between a postulate and a theorem?

Answer: A postulate is accepted without proof; a theorem is proven
Reasoning: Both may be used as reasons once established.
5

In a two-column proof, what belongs in the right column?

Answer: Reasons that justify each statement
Reasoning: Each step must cite a definition, property, postulate, or theorem.
6

Why is a diagram alone not sufficient proof?

Answer: It may not be drawn to scale and cannot establish every required relationship
Reasoning: Proof depends on stated or justified facts.
