Unit 13 · Lesson 01 · Teacher Key

Frame a Geometric Design or Investigation

Evaluate whether the learner has converted a broad idea into a measurable, constrained, testable capstone brief.

Math Word: Constraint

Expected retrieval: A constraint is a requirement or limitation a design must satisfy; “fit inside a 6 m × 8 m site” is testable, while “make it attractive” is a preference unless further defined.

1
Answer: Responses vary. Require a focused question containing measurable quantities and a decision or claim that can later be tested. Reject questions that are only broad topics.
2
Answer: Responses vary. The audience must be named and connected to at least one criterion—for example, student users may require circulation and accessibility constraints.
3
Answer: At least three constraints, each classified reasonably. Geometric examples include dimensions or angles; practical examples include material or access requirements; communication examples include readable scale/labels.
4
Answer: Verified measurements are observed or checked values; unknowns are quantities to determine; assumptions are accepted modeling choices that must remain visible and revisable.
5
Answer: At least three measurable success criteria, each paired with a future verification method such as measurement, calculation, construction, CAD inspection, or comparison to a threshold.
6
Answer: At least two earlier geometry concepts with genuine relevance—for example scale drawing for a site plan, area for material use, transformations for modular repetition, similarity for resizing, or distance for clearance.

Capstone checkpoint

Do not advance a weakly framed project merely because the learner is eager to build it. The question, constraints, assumptions, and success criteria become the audit trail for the rest of Unit 13.

Mastery: 80% plus revisions. A capstone brief should be understandable and testable by someone other than its author.
