📚 Math Word of the Day: Sufficiency

Technical definition: Sufficiency asks whether the available information and evidence are enough to justify a particular mathematical conclusion.

Student definition: Do we actually know enough to say this?

Critical-thinking rule: A diagram that looks convincing is not additional evidence. Use only stated facts, valid deductions, verified measurements, and justified assumptions.

Learning intention

Synthesize the capstone process and solve fair reasoning challenges in which irrelevant information, missing conditions, visual assumptions, or overconfident claims may be present.

Success evidence

  • Distinguish what is stated from what merely appears true.
  • Recognize when information is insufficient.
  • Reject invalid theorem use even when it produces the expected answer.
  • Ignore irrelevant data.
  • Identify conclusions that overstate the evidence.
  • Revise a position when stronger evidence overturns it.
  • Connect the final examination back to the L01–L05 capstone audit trail.

Fair reasoning traps

looks ≠ givencorrect answer ≠ valid reasoningmore data ≠ relevant datachange in value ≠ change in decision

These are not “gotchas.” Every problem contains enough information to determine what is justified—including when the correct conclusion is insufficient information.

Khaemenes Academy · Geometry Capstone

Final Synthesis — Critical Thinking Examination

Read carefully. Diagrams are not assumed to scale. Explain why your conclusion is justified.

U13 · L06
1

A quadrilateral is drawn to look like a rectangle. The only marked facts are that opposite sides are parallel. A student concludes all four angles are 90°. Is the conclusion justified? Explain.

2

A right triangle has legs 9 cm and 12 cm. The problem also states that the paper is 21.6 cm wide and the diagram was printed at 300 dpi. Find the hypotenuse and identify which information is irrelevant.

3

A student writes: “The distance is 10 because √((8−2)²+(9−1)²)=√100=10. Therefore the segment is horizontal.” Evaluate both the numerical result and the conclusion.

4

Two triangles have side lengths 5, 7, 9 and 5, 7, 9. A student says they are similar because all corresponding sides are proportional. Is that statement sufficient? State the strongest justified relationship.

5

A 1:50 plan shows a corridor width of 2.0 cm. The requirement is at least 0.95 m actual width. Does the corridor pass? Show the conversion and margin.

6

A model predicts 48.0 m² at the base input, 46.5 m² at the low case, and 49.5 m² at the high case. The decision requires at least 45 m². A critic says, “The output changes, so the decision is unreliable.” Is that criticism supported by these results?

7

A student correctly finds an angle is 40° by measuring a drawing with a protractor. The problem asks for a proof from stated relationships. Has the student proved the result? Explain the difference between measurement evidence and deductive proof.

8

A circle has radius 6 cm. Student A reports circumference 12π cm; Student B reports approximately 37.70 cm. Are these answers contradictory? Explain.

9

A triangle has two sides 7 cm and 11 cm. What is its exact area? If it cannot be determined, state what additional information would make the problem solvable.

10

A CAD tool reports that two lines are perpendicular, but the coordinate slopes calculated from the entered endpoints are 2 and −0.4. What should you do before accepting either result? Explain what the slopes suggest.

11

A design claim says, “Because our tested width range changed the area by less than 4%, the design will work under all possible real-world conditions.” Identify the reasoning problem and rewrite the claim so it matches the evidence.

12

During defense, a reviewer finds a verified measurement error that changes your final conclusion. Which response demonstrates stronger mathematical reasoning: defend the original conclusion, hide the error, or revise the conclusion? Explain why.

Capstone completion reflection

Attach or reference the final L01–L05 portfolio. Record one claim that became stronger through validation, one claim that became narrower through sensitivity/limitations, and one revision caused by critique. Then write a short statement explaining how your reasoning changed from the beginning of the capstone to the end.

Completion rule

80% plus corrections. A completion record represents demonstrated reasoning—not first-attempt perfection. Missed examination items must be corrected with an explanation of the original error before the capstone is closed.

Critical reader

Ask: What is given? What is inferred? What is missing? What would change my conclusion?

Capstone words

constraint · validation · sensitivity · traceability · rebuttal · sufficiency

Final standard

Reason carefully, communicate evidence, correct errors, and know the limits of the claim.
