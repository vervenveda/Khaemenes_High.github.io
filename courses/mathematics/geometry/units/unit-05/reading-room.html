Euclid Book VI — Similarity, Ratio & Proportion

Read selected propositions from The Elements beside modern similarity, dilation, scale drawing, and indirect-measurement work.

Essential reading question

When do proportional measurements establish the same shape—and when can a proportion be numerically correct but geometrically mismatched?

Critical-thinking rule: ratios only carry geometric meaning when the quantities being compared actually correspond.

Source

Euclid, The First Six Books of the Elements, Book VI, John Casey edition. Use selected definitions and propositions rather than assigning the complete book.

Project Gutenberg edition

Three-week reading arc

Week 11 — Read & Notice: Similar figures and correspondence

Read the opening Book VI material on similar rectilineal figures and proportional relationships. Build a two-column translation from historical language to modern terms: corresponding angles, corresponding sides, ratio, scale factor, similar figures.

Notice: same shape does not require same size. Similarity preserves angle measure and proportional structure, not ordinary side length.

Week 12 — Math Connection: Similar triangles and proportional sides

Read selected Book VI propositions involving equiangular triangles and proportional sides. Match each historical claim to modern AA, SSS~, SAS~, or triangle-proportionality reasoning used in Lessons 03–05.

Reasoning trap: 3/6=4/8 is a true numerical proportion. It does not prove two triangles similar unless those ratios represent correctly corresponding sides and the required theorem conditions are satisfied.

Week 13 — Evidence & Synthesis: Scale and indirect measurement

Use a selected proportional argument as a model for a modern scale drawing, shadow, mirror, or inaccessible-distance problem. Explicitly identify the similar triangles before writing a proportion.

Synthesis: explain why a scale calculation can be arithmetically correct but geometrically invalid when units or correspondence are wrong.

Geometry Reading Room

Euclid Similarity & Proportion Audit

Connect historical mathematical language to modern similarity reasoning.

U05
1

In your own words, distinguish congruent figures from similar figures. Which properties are preserved in both, and which may change under similarity?

2

Choose a selected Book VI statement about similar or equiangular figures. Rewrite it using modern vocabulary and symbols.

3

A student writes AB/DE = AC/DF but has not established that A corresponds to D, B to E, and C to F. What is missing from the argument?

4

Create two triangles for which one pair of side ratios is equal but the triangles are not similar. Explain why one matching ratio is insufficient.

5

A 1:50 drawing shows a length of 3.4 cm. Find the actual length in centimeters and meters. Then explain why unit conversion belongs in the evidence chain.

6

For an indirect-measurement problem, identify the two triangles, state why they are similar, write a proportion using corresponding sides, and name one assumption or measurement error that could weaken the result.

Evidence Citation Studio

Accessibility & mastery

Historical prose may be paraphrased aloud, chunked, translated, or paired with diagrams while preserving the mathematical claim. Reading completion supports mathematical literacy but does not replace the Unit 05 mathematics mastery requirement of 80% plus corrections.
