Unit 02 · Lesson 06 · Transformations, Symmetry & Congruence

Line, Rotational & Point Symmetry

Determine when a figure maps onto itself under reflection or rotation, quantify its symmetry, and justify symmetry using geometric and coordinate evidence.

Foundation · Core · ExtendedMastery target 80%20 problemsPrintable

Learning intention

I can identify and justify line symmetry, rotational symmetry, and point symmetry and distinguish self-symmetry from a transformation that merely moves a figure elsewhere.

Success evidence

  • Identify and count lines of symmetry.
  • Find rotational order and the smallest positive angle of rotation.
  • Recognize point symmetry as 180° rotational symmetry.
  • Use coordinates or corresponding features to verify that a figure maps onto itself.

Symmetry map

line symmetry: reflection maps F onto Frotational order n ⇒ smallest angle 360°/npoint symmetry ⇔ 180° rotational symmetry

A symmetry transformation must return the entire figure to itself. For a regular n-gon, there are n rotational positions in one full turn, so its rotational order is n and its smallest positive rotational angle is 360°/n.

Having rotational symmetry does not automatically determine the number of reflection axes for an arbitrary figure.

Worked reasoning

Example: A regular hexagon has rotational order 6.

  1. A full turn is 360°.
  2. 360°÷6=60°.
  3. Therefore 60° is the smallest positive rotation mapping the hexagon onto itself.
  4. Rotations of 60°, 120°, 180°, 240°, 300°, and 360° all return it to itself.

Misconception check: A transformation that produces a congruent copy somewhere else is not necessarily a symmetry. For symmetry, the image must coincide with the original figure.

Learning pathways

Foundation: Fold, sketch, or trace possible symmetry lines and rotation positions.

Core: Complete all 20 and calculate rotational order and angle accurately.

Extended: Verify symmetry with coordinates, compare symmetry classes, and design figures under symmetry constraints.
