Flatland: Movement, Symmetry, Congruence & Point of View

Edwin A. Abbott · 1884 · Public domain in the United States

Read selected passages as a mathematical thought experiment. Abbott's social satire includes dated and discriminatory Victorian ideas; those claims are historical context, not mathematical facts or course values.

Read Free at Project Gutenberg

Why this belongs in Unit 02

Flatland imagines geometric figures moving in a two-dimensional plane. Its descriptions create natural questions about position, orientation, invariants, recognition, regularity, symmetry, and what changes when a figure is viewed or moved differently.

Three-week reading arc

Week 3 — Read & Notice: Section 1, “Of the Nature of Flatland”

Focus on Abbott's two-dimensional world and the difference between a figure's actual geometry and its appearance from a restricted viewpoint.

Notice: What properties belong to the figure itself? Which descriptions depend on the observer?

Week 4 — Math Connection: Sections 5–7, recognition and regular figures

Use selected passages about recognizing figures and regularity. Translate literary descriptions into geometric claims that can be tested with coordinates or transformations.

Connection: Which properties are preserved when a figure is translated, reflected, or rotated? Which transformations reverse orientation?

Week 5 — Evidence & Synthesis: Sections 13 and 15–18, Lineland and Spaceland

Compare one-, two-, and three-dimensional viewpoints. Separate what a character observes from what the evidence actually establishes.

Synthesis: How can a change in viewpoint change appearance without changing the underlying object?

TEXTUAL CLAIM — Write one claim Abbott makes about what a Flatlander can see or recognize.

MATHEMATICAL TEST — Represent a figure with coordinates. Apply a translation, reflection, or rotation. List at least three properties preserved by the transformation.

4

CRITICAL THINKING — Does a changed appearance prove the original figure changed size or shape? Distinguish observation from inference.

5

COUNTEREXAMPLE — Give an example where two figures can look similar from one viewpoint but are not congruent, or where the same congruent figure looks different after a change of orientation/viewpoint.

6

SYNTHESIS — Complete: “A rigid motion changes ___ but preserves ___. Therefore congruence should be established by ___ rather than appearance alone.”

Critical-reading lens

Flatland mixes mathematical imagination with satire. Treat statements about geometry as claims to analyze, and statements about social groups as part of the book's historical/satirical context—not as evidence about real people.

Challenge: Find one place where the narrator assumes something about the world that later experience forces him to reconsider. What mathematical habit does that resemble?

Course integration

L01–L03: connect motion to coordinate rules. L04–L05: connect sequences of motion to congruence. L06–L07: connect regularity and repetition to symmetry, tessellation, and design.
