# Khaemenes Global Algebra I & Integrated Mathematics I

**Course Code:** KH-MATH-A1  
**Duration:** 36 weeks  
**Audience:** Placement-based learners with demonstrated Pre-Algebra readiness  
**Pathways:** Foundation · Core · Extended  
**Mastery Target:** 80%

Khaemenes Global Algebra I & Integrated Mathematics I is a complete 36-week mathematics course designed to move learners from Pre-Algebra readiness into formal algebraic reasoning, functions, modelling, polynomial and quadratic mathematics, statistics, financial mathematics, and integrated problem solving.

The course emphasizes mathematical understanding, accurate procedure, explanation, multiple representations, verification, application, and increasingly independent reasoning.

---

## Course Purpose

Algebra I is a major transition in a learner's mathematical development.

Students move from primarily numerical reasoning toward the study of generalized relationships, variables, functions, equations, constraints, patterns, and mathematical models.

Throughout the course, students are expected to:

- reason with variables and symbolic expressions;
- preserve equality while solving equations;
- interpret inequalities as constraints;
- represent relationships with equations, tables, graphs, and verbal descriptions;
- understand functions as relationships between quantities;
- analyze rates of change;
- solve and interpret systems;
- work with exponents, polynomials, radicals, and quadratics;
- use mathematics to model real situations;
- evaluate whether an answer is reasonable;
- explain assumptions, limitations, and conclusions;
- develop evidence of readiness for subsequent mathematics.

---

## Readiness

This course is placement-based rather than age-based.

Learners should enter with sufficient Pre-Algebra readiness in areas such as:

- arithmetic fluency;
- fractions, decimals, and percents;
- integers and rational numbers;
- ratios and proportional reasoning;
- order of operations;
- basic expressions and equations;
- coordinate-plane concepts;
- measurement and foundational geometry;
- basic data interpretation.

A readiness diagnostic is provided at the beginning of the course to identify strengths and areas that may need additional support.

The diagnostic is intended to guide instruction and pathway selection rather than label or punish a learner.

---

## Learning Pathways

The course provides three instructional pathways.

### Foundation

Provides additional scaffolding, prerequisite review, guided examples, and supported practice for learners who need more time developing a concept.

### Core

Represents the primary Algebra I learning pathway and expected course sequence.

### Extended

Provides additional challenge, deeper reasoning, transfer problems, modelling, and enrichment for learners ready to move beyond the core expectation.

The pathways are intended to adjust support and challenge without changing the identity or essential expectations of the course.

---

## Mastery Model

The course uses an **80% mastery target**.

Students are expected to demonstrate understanding through more than completion alone. Evidence may include classwork, mastery practice, corrections, modelling, portfolio work, assessments, explanations, and reflection.

A learner who has not yet reached mastery should receive additional instruction or practice before the concept is treated as secure.

Mastery is intended to represent both mathematical accuracy and growing independence.

# 36-Week Course of Study

The course contains an opening readiness period, 13 instructional units, and a final integrated modelling capstone.

## Readiness Diagnostic

**Week 1**

The opening diagnostic examines prerequisite knowledge and helps identify the most appropriate starting support.

Students use the results to identify strengths, unfinished learning, and an initial mathematics goal.

---

## Unit 01 — Algebraic Reasoning, Real Numbers & Precision

**2 weeks · 6 lessons**

Students establish the language and habits required for trustworthy algebraic reasoning.

Major ideas include:

- real-number classification;
- mathematical notation;
- properties of numbers and operations;
- units and quantities;
- precision;
- estimation;
- reasonableness;
- mathematical claims and verification.

**Essential Question:**  
How do notation, units, properties, and precision make mathematical claims trustworthy?

---

## Unit 02 — Expressions, Properties & Polynomial Foundations

**3 weeks · 7 lessons**

Students develop structural understanding of algebraic expressions.

Major ideas include:

- variables, constants, coefficients, and terms;
- properties of operations;
- equivalent expressions;
- combining like terms;
- distributive reasoning;
- evaluating expressions;
- introductory polynomial structure.

**Essential Question:**  
How can algebraic structure help us simplify, compare, and generalize relationships?

---

## Unit 03 — Linear Equations, Formulas & Proportional Models

**3 weeks · 7 lessons**

Students develop increasingly sophisticated equation-solving strategies and connect equations to real relationships.

Major ideas include:

- one-step and multistep equations;
- variables on both sides;
- equations involving distribution;
- literal equations and formulas;
- proportions;
- rates;
- distance and mixture situations;
- financial and practical models;
- checking solutions.

**Essential Question:**  
How do equations preserve equality while representing real constraints and relationships?

---

## Unit 04 — Linear Inequalities & Absolute Value

**2 weeks · 6 lessons**

Students use inequalities and absolute value to represent ranges, limits, tolerances, and constraints.

Major ideas include:

- inequality notation;
- solution sets;
- graphing inequalities;
- multistep inequalities;
- reversing inequality direction when required;
- compound constraints;
- absolute value;
- contextual interpretation.

**Essential Question:**  
How do inequalities and absolute value describe ranges, tolerances, limits, and feasible choices?

---

## Unit 05 — Functions, Domain, Range & Multiple Representations

**3 weeks · 7 lessons**

Students develop the foundational function concepts that support later mathematics.

Major ideas include:

- relations and functions;
- inputs and outputs;
- function notation;
- domain and range;
- tables;
- graphs;
- equations;
- verbal descriptions;
- transformations;
- piecewise relationships;
- sequences.

**Essential Question:**  
How do functions connect inputs and outputs across equations, tables, graphs, and contexts?

---

## Unit 06 — Linear Functions, Regression & Modelling

**4 weeks · 8 lessons**

Students study constant rates of change and use linear models to interpret and predict real phenomena.

Major ideas include:

- slope;
- rate of change;
- intercepts;
- linear equations;
- graphing linear relationships;
- comparing linear representations;
- scatterplots;
- trend and association;
- regression;
- prediction;
- model limitations.

**Essential Question:**  
How can linear functions describe constant change, support prediction, and reveal model limitations?

---

## Unit 07 — Systems of Equations & Inequalities

**3 weeks · 7 lessons**

Students analyze situations involving multiple simultaneous relationships or constraints.

Major ideas include:

- systems of linear equations;
- graphical solutions;
- substitution;
- elimination;
- interpreting intersections;
- systems of inequalities;
- feasible regions;
- contextual decision making.

**Essential Question:**  
How do simultaneous constraints determine intersections, feasible choices, and optimized decisions?

---

## Unit 08 — Exponents, Sequences & Exponential Functions

**2 weeks · 6 lessons**

Students connect repeated multiplication, exponent rules, sequences, and exponential change.

Major ideas include:

- exponent properties;
- powers and repeated multiplication;
- exponential expressions;
- arithmetic and geometric patterns;
- sequences;
- exponential growth;
- exponential decay;
- percent change.

**Essential Question:**  
How do repeated multiplication and constant percent change create exponential patterns?

---

## Unit 09 — Polynomials, Operations & Factoring

**3 weeks · 7 lessons**

Students develop fluency with polynomial structure and use factoring to reveal important mathematical relationships.

Major ideas include:

- polynomial vocabulary;
- addition and subtraction;
- multiplication;
- distributive structure;
- common factors;
- factoring;
- zeros;
- connections between algebraic and geometric representations.

**Essential Question:**  
How do polynomial operations and factoring expose structure, zeros, and geometric relationships?

---

## Unit 10 — Quadratic Functions & Equations

**4 weeks · 8 lessons**

Students investigate nonlinear relationships characterized by constant second differences and parabolic graphs.

Major ideas include:

- quadratic patterns;
- parabolas;
- standard, factored, and vertex forms;
- zeros and intercepts;
- factoring;
- solving quadratic equations;
- vertices;
- maximum and minimum values;
- transformations;
- quadratic modelling.

**Essential Question:**  
How do quadratic forms reveal zeros, vertices, rates of change, and maximum or minimum values?

---

## Unit 11 — Radicals, Rational Exponents & Coordinate Geometry

**2 weeks · 6 lessons**

Students connect radical expressions and algebraic reasoning to distance and geometric relationships.

Major ideas include:

- radicals;
- square roots;
- rational exponents;
- simplifying radical expressions;
- coordinate methods;
- distance;
- geometric constraints;
- algebra-geometry connections.

**Essential Question:**  
How do radical forms and coordinate methods connect algebra, distance, and geometric constraints?

---

## Unit 12 — Statistics, Financial Mathematics & Evidence

**3 weeks · 7 lessons**

Students use algebra, statistics, and quantitative reasoning to evaluate evidence and make responsible decisions.

Major ideas include:

- data interpretation;
- measures and representations;
- association and trends;
- statistical evidence;
- financial mathematics;
- rates and percent change;
- comparisons;
- assumptions;
- quantitative decision making.

**Essential Question:**  
How can algebra and statistics support responsible financial and evidence-based decisions?

---

## Unit 13 — Integrated Mathematical Modelling Capstone

**1 week · 5 lessons**

The final unit asks students to integrate mathematics from across the course into a defensible model.

Students may investigate a personal, scientific, environmental, financial, civic, or other appropriate real-world question.

The capstone emphasizes:

- defining a mathematical problem;
- identifying relevant quantities;
- selecting appropriate representations;
- choosing and defending a model;
- performing and checking calculations;
- interpreting results;
- identifying assumptions;
- acknowledging uncertainty and limitations;
- communicating conclusions;
- reflecting on mathematical growth.

**Essential Question:**  
How can a defensible mathematical model support a real decision while acknowledging uncertainty and limits?

## Weekly Learning Rhythm

A typical instructional week combines several forms of mathematical work.

Students encounter:

1. **Concept development** — vocabulary, prerequisite reasoning, definitions, properties, and examples.
2. **Guided mathematical work** — worked examples and structured practice.
3. **Application and modelling** — mathematics used to represent meaningful situations.
4. **Error analysis and mixed review** — misconceptions, corrections, and prerequisite reinforcement.
5. **Mastery and reflection** — evidence of understanding, portfolio selection, and planning the next step.

This rhythm is intended to develop both mathematical fluency and durable understanding.

## Assessment

Assessment is distributed throughout the course rather than reserved only for the end.

The course includes:

- readiness diagnostic;
- lesson and weekly mastery work;
- classwork evidence;
- projects and modelling;
- portfolio evidence;
- unit mastery checks;
- midterm assessment;
- final assessment;
- integrated modelling capstone;
- reflection and correction opportunities.

### Midterm

The midterm occurs after Week 18 and covers Units 01–06.

It contains **60 questions**.

### Final

The final occurs during Week 36 and draws from Units 01–13.

It contains **100 questions**.

### Definitive-Answer Standard

Objective scored questions should have a clearly defensible correct answer.

Assessment design should avoid ambiguous answer choices, equivalent duplicate choices, unstated conventions, and subjective “best answer” questions without a defined criterion.
