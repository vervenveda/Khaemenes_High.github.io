Reading and reference guides

1 · Evidence and equivalence

Checking a proposed answer tells us whether it works in the original equation. A general identity requires a property or argument valid for every allowed input.

Example: 3(x + 2) = 3x + 6 follows from distribution, not from testing one number.

Misconception: testing three values does not prove a claim for every real number. One valid counterexample can disprove it.

2 · Intervals and endpoints

An open endpoint excludes its number; a closed endpoint includes it. For -3 < x ≤ 2, write (-3, 2]. Infinity always uses parentheses.

Do not use a radical sign alone to classify a number: √36 = 6 is rational, while √7 is irrational.

3 · Units and dimensional meaning

A conversion factor must equal one, and unwanted units must cancel: 54 km/h × 1000 m/km × 1 h/3600 s = 15 m/s.

For distance, speed × time has units of length. A numerical answer with hours per mile is not a distance.

4 · Accuracy and precision

Accuracy is closeness to a reference; precision can describe repeatability. A tightly grouped set of readings can still be inaccurate.

Percent error = |measured - accepted| / |accepted| × 100%, when the accepted reference is nonzero. For a zero reference, use an absolute difference or an appropriately defined alternative.
