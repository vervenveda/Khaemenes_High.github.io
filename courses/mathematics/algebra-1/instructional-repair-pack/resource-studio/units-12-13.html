Unit 12 · Statistics and financial evidence

A statistical question anticipates variability, such as “How long do students travel to school?” A single person's commute is not a distribution. Sampling methods affect whose experiences are represented, and correlation alone does not prove causation.

Worked data: for 2, 4, 4, 6, 9, the mean is 5, median is 4, and range is 7. The population standard deviation is √(28/5) ≈ 2.37; the sample standard deviation is √(28/4) ≈ 2.65. Identify the definition and denominator before comparing results.

Financial example: $1,000 earning 5% per year produces $50 in simple interest after one year. Annually compounded interest also produces $50 in year one, but compounds over subsequent years. Actual loans may involve additional fees, taxes, and payment structures.

Unit 13 · Model, validate, revise and defend

Two transport providers quote A(n) = 180 + 8n dollars and B(n) = 80 + 12n dollars for n students. Equating costs gives 180+8n=80+12n, so n=25. Below 25, B is cheaper; above 25, A is cheaper, assuming both provide the same service.

At n=20, A costs $340 and B costs $320. At n=30, A costs $420 and B costs $440. If A's fixed charge rises to $200, the break-even count changes to 30 students. This sensitivity matters when the estimate of participants is uncertain.

Defence: define variables and domain, record assumptions, verify calculations, compare options, test uncertain inputs, and discuss accessibility, capacity, fees, and fairness. A cheapest numerical result is not automatically the best decision.
