Data Questions, Variables, Populations & Samples

Directions: Show calculations and evidence reasoning. Label displays, state units/assumptions, and distinguish what the data show from what you infer.

1. Which is a statistical question?

(A) How many minutes do students in this class sleep on school nights?   (B) What is 8+7?   (C) What is the capital of Peru?   (D) Is 12 even?

2. Which variable is numerical?

(A) bus route name   (B) eye colour   (C) minutes of exercise   (D) type of pet

3. Which sample best represents students across four grade levels?

(A) 10 randomly selected students from each grade   (B) the first 40 students entering one sports practice   (C) 40 volunteers from a social-media post   (D) one entire advanced mathematics class

4. Favorite school subject is usually:

(A) quantitative continuous   (B) a sample size   (C) a probability   (D) categorical

5. A sample is:

(A) always the whole population   (B) an outlier   (C) a variable   (D) a subset observed from a population

6. Height is commonly treated as:

(A) quantitative discrete only   (B) a sampling method   (C) quantitative continuous   (D) categorical

7. If surveying students, each student's response is:

(A) an observational unit   (B) the population mean   (C) a histogram   (D) a parameter automatically

8. A numerical summary calculated from a sample is a:

(A) category   (B) statistic   (C) parameter   (D) population

9. A question saying 'Don't you agree our excellent cafeteria is healthy?' is:

(A) neutral   (B) randomized   (C) quantitative   (D) leading and potentially biased

10. When collecting student data, a sound practice is:

(A) collect unrelated private details   (B) ignore consent rules   (C) collect only what is needed and protect identifiable information   (D) publish names by default

Reflection / Evidence Audit

Choose one data or probability claim from this lesson. Identify the evidence source, show the relevant calculation/display reasoning, name one possible bias or uncertainty, and write a conclusion that does not overstate what the evidence proves.
