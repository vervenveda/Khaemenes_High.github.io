Comparing Distributions & Detecting Misleading Displays

Class

Directions: Show calculations and evidence reasoning. Label displays, state units/assumptions, and distinguish what the data show from what you infer.

1. A complete comparison of two numerical distributions should discuss:

(A) center, spread, shape, and unusual features   (B) only the means   (C) only sample sizes   (D) only the graph colours

2. Group A has 20 successes out of 40; Group B has 30 out of 100. Which rate is larger?

(A) Group B, 70%   (B) they are equal   (C) Group A, 50%   (D) cannot compare percentages

3. Which practice supports a fair display?

(A) consistent scale and clear source   (B) unequal time intervals   (C) unlabelled axes   (D) pictograms scaled in height and width without warning

4. Truncating a bar-chart axis can:

(A) change raw data   (B) guarantee accuracy   (C) remove categories   (D) visually exaggerate differences

5. A graph comparison should ideally disclose:

(A) only title   (B) student names   (C) desired conclusion   (D) group sizes when relevant

6. An outlier can affect:

(A) mean and range strongly   (B) median more than all measures always   (C) category names   (D) sample definition only

7. A statistically visible difference may still be:

(A) small or unimportant in practical context   (B) always causal   (C) always large   (D) invalid

8. Before trusting a graph, inspect:

(A) largest bar only   (B) title only   (C) axes, units, baseline, intervals, labels, source, and omitted context   (D) font only

9. A chart uses inconsistent interval widths but equal-looking bars. Concern:

(A) mean becomes invalid   (B) visual comparison may be distorted   (C) intervals never matter   (D) all histograms require categories

10. A responsible conclusion should distinguish:

(A) what the data show from what is inferred   (B) data from units   (C) mean from median only   (D) title from axis

Reflection / Evidence Audit

Choose one data or probability claim from this lesson. Identify the evidence source, show the relevant calculation/display reasoning, name one possible bias or uncertainty, and write a conclusion that does not overstate what the evidence proves.
