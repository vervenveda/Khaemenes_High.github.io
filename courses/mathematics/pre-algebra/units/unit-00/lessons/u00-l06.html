Foundations & Readiness · Lesson 06

Measurement, Geometry & Integrated Readiness

Bring the refresher strands together. Use units, formulas, proportional reasoning, equations, coordinates, estimation, and evidence carefully enough to decide what can—and cannot—be concluded.

Today's Math Word · Sufficiency

Sufficiency asks whether the available information is enough to justify the requested conclusion. Mathematics sometimes requires an answer such as “cannot be determined from the information given.”

Given facts + valid reasoning + correct units → justified conclusion

1 · Refresh the ideas

Measurement and units

Units are part of the quantity. Convert incompatible units before combining them. For example, 2.5 m=250 cm because 1 m=100 cm.

Perimeter, area, and volume

Perimeter measures distance around a figure; area measures surface; volume measures three-dimensional space. Rectangle: P=2l+2w and A=lw. Rectangular prism: V=lwh.

Angles and geometric relationships

Complementary angles total 90°, supplementary angles total 180°, and the interior angles of a triangle total 180°. Use relationships that are given or established—not what a sketch merely appears to show.

Scale and proportional reasoning

A scale drawing uses a ratio. If 1 cm represents 2 m, then 4.5 cm represents 9 m. Preserve what each side of the ratio means.

Integrated reasoning

Real problems rarely announce which chapter they belong to. You may need measurement, decimals, percent, equations, estimation, and comparison in one solution.

2 · Worked examples

Example A. A rectangle is 9 cm by 4 cm.

P=2(9)+2(4)=26 cm. A=9×4=36 cm². Notice the different units.

Example B. Two angles form a straight line; one is 68°.

They are supplementary: x+68=180, so x=112°.

Example C. Scale 1 cm:2 m; plan length 4.5 cm.

4.5×2=9, so the actual length is 9 m.

Example D. A triangle has side lengths 6 cm and 10 cm only. Find its exact area.

There is not enough information. Without a corresponding height, included angle, or sufficient additional dimensions, many different triangles are possible.

3 · Guided practice

G1. A room is 5.5 m by 4 m. Find perimeter and area, including correct units.
G2. Convert 3.2 m to centimeters, then explain why 3.2 m+75 cm should not be added before conversion.
G3. A triangle has angles 47° and 61°. Find the third angle and write the equation you used.

5 · Capstone Lab · Community Room Flooring Decision

A rectangular community room measures 6 m by 4.5 m. Flooring costs $18 per square meter. The supplier offers a 10% material discount. The project budget is $450.

  1. Estimate the undiscounted cost before exact calculation.
  2. Find the floor area.
  3. Find the exact undiscounted material cost.
  4. Find the discount and discounted cost.
  5. Write an inequality comparing project cost with the budget.
  6. Determine whether the budget is sufficient and by how much.
  7. Explain how your estimate, percent reasoning, geometry, and inequality all support the same decision.

6 · Critical-thinking challenge · What does the diagram actually prove?

A diagram shows a quadrilateral that looks exactly like a rectangle. The problem states only that opposite sides are parallel. A learner immediately uses the rectangle area formula with two visible side labels. Decide what is justified. Which conclusions follow from the stated evidence, which are merely suggested by appearance, and what additional information would establish that the figure is a rectangle?
