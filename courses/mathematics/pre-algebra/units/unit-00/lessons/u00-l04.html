Foundations & Readiness · Lesson 04

Equations & Inequalities

Move from reading algebra to preserving relationships. Solve equations by maintaining equality, verify solutions, interpret inequalities as sets of possibilities, and audit invalid reasoning.

Today's Math Word · Inverse

Inverse operations undo one another: addition and subtraction; multiplication and division. They help isolate a variable without changing the truth of an equation.

Preserve the relationship → undo carefully → verify by substitution → interpret the result.

1 · Refresh the ideas

Equality is balance

An equation says two expressions have the same value. A valid operation applied equally to both sides preserves that relationship. From x+9=21, subtract 9 from both sides to obtain x=12.

Undo in reverse order

For 4x−7=29, first add 7, then divide by 4: 4x=36, so x=9. Checking gives 4(9)−7=29.

Inequalities describe ranges

x<5 does not name one answer. It describes every number less than 5. A solution should therefore be interpreted as a set or constraint, often represented on a number line.

The negative-number reversal

If −2x>10, dividing both sides by −2 gives x<−5. Reversing the symbol preserves the order relationship after multiplication or division by a negative number.

2 · Worked examples

Example A. Solve 3x+5=26.

Subtract 5: 3x=21. Divide by 3: x=7. Check: 3(7)+5=26.

Example B. Solve 3(x+2)=24.

Divide by 3: x+2=8. Subtract 2: x=6. Distribution would also work.

Example C. Solve 5x−4≤21.

Add 4: 5x≤25. Divide by 5: x≤5. This represents infinitely many values, including 5.

Example D. Audit: 5x+3=18 → 5x=21.

The learner added 3 instead of subtracting it. Correctly subtracting 3 gives 5x=15 and x=3.

5 · Application Lab · Activity Budget Constraint

A community program has at most $60 available for one student. There is a fixed registration fee of $12, and each activity costs $8.

  1. Define a variable for the number of activities.
  2. Write an inequality representing the budget.
  3. Solve the inequality.
  4. Interpret the solution in whole-number activities.
  5. Test the greatest allowable number directly in the original budget.
  6. Explain why the next whole number fails even though the algebraic solution includes many non-whole values.

6 · Critical-thinking challenge · A correct answer from bad reasoning?

A student performs an invalid algebra step but accidentally reaches the correct numerical answer. Another student says, “It does not matter because the answer is right.” Evaluate that claim. Explain why mathematical reasoning must be valid even when a final number happens to be correct, and give or construct a simple example.
