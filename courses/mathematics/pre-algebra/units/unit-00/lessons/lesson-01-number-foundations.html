Learn · Concept Study Guide

Structure before speed.

A student is ready for Unit 1 when these ideas are explainable, not just memorizable.

1 · Number families and membership

Numbers belong to families. A counting number such as 8 is also whole, integer, rational, and real. A number such as −8 is not natural or whole, but it is an integer, rational, and real. A square root must be simplified before classification: √49 is 7, but √11 is irrational.

  • Ask for the most specific family first.
  • Then name every larger family that also contains the number.
  • Use decimal behavior: terminating or repeating decimals are rational.
Try: classify 0, −6, 3/4, √16, and √10.

2 · Signed numbers and absolute value

A sign tells direction or position relative to zero. Positive can mean above, right, gained, deposited, or increased. Negative can mean below, left, lost, withdrawn, or decreased. Absolute value tells distance from zero, so it is never negative.

  • −12 is a signed position or change.
  • |−12| = 12 because the distance from zero is 12 units.
  • Opposites have the same absolute value but different directions.
Try: explain the difference between −9 and |−9|.

3 · Exponents and repeated multiplication

An exponent tells how many times the base is used as a factor. This is different from multiplying the base by the exponent. For example, 3² means 3 × 3, not 3 × 2.

  • 2³ = 2 × 2 × 2 = 8.
  • 4² = 4 × 4 = 16.
  • When an exponent appears in a longer expression, evaluate it before multiplication, division, addition, or subtraction.
Try: compare 2³ and 3². Which is larger?

4 · Order of operations

The order of operations preserves shared mathematical meaning. Grouping symbols come first, then exponents, then multiplication and division from left to right, then addition and subtraction from left to right.

  • Multiplication does not always happen before division; they share a level.
  • Addition does not always happen before subtraction; they share a level.
  • Left-to-right matters when operations share the same priority.
Try: evaluate 18 ÷ 3 × 2. Explain the left-to-right step.

5 · Factor structure

Factors build whole numbers. Prime factorization reveals that structure. This prepares students for GCF, LCM, fractions, ratios, and later algebraic factoring. A strong factor habit prevents guessing.

  • List factor pairs systematically.
  • Check divisibility before claiming a number is prime.
  • Use prime factorization to see the hidden structure of a number.
Try: write 36 as a product of prime factors.

6 · Reasonableness checks

Before accepting an answer, check whether its sign and size make sense. Estimation is not careless arithmetic; it is a quality-control habit. An answer can look neat and still be impossible.

  • Predict whether the result should be positive, negative, or zero.
  • Estimate the magnitude before or after calculating.
  • Ask whether the operation should make the value larger or smaller.
Try: decide whether 49 × 21 = 10,290 is reasonable without exact multiplication.

Reading room

How to study this week

Use a three-pass method

  1. Pass 1 · Meaning: identify what each sign, symbol, or operation is asking.
  2. Pass 2 · Structure: decide which rule applies and why it applies.
  3. Pass 3 · Check: verify the sign, magnitude, and context before submitting.

Write one sentence after each hard problem

A short sentence such as “I multiplied before adding,” “absolute value is distance,” or “the negative sign means below the reference point” turns a calculation into a reusable strategy. This is how the refresher becomes a bridge into Unit 1 rather than a repeated worksheet.

Worked examples

Slow reasoning first, then practice speed.

Order of operations

Evaluate: 6 + 4 × 3

  1. Multiplication has priority over addition.
  2. Compute 4 × 3 = 12.
  3. Add 6 + 12.
Answer: 18

Signed addition

Evaluate: −9 + 5

  1. The signs differ, so compare magnitudes.
  2. 9 is larger than 5.
  3. Subtract 9 − 5 = 4 and keep the sign of −9.
Answer: −4

Absolute value

Evaluate: |−7 − 3|

  1. Work inside the absolute-value bars first.
  2. −7 − 3 = −10.
  3. |−10| asks for distance from zero.
Answer: 10

Exponent meaning

Evaluate: 2⁴

  1. The base is 2.
  2. The exponent 4 means use 2 as a factor four times.
  3. 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 16.
Answer: 16

Grouping with signed result

Evaluate: (8 − 11) × 3

  1. Grouping symbols come first.
  2. 8 − 11 = −3.
  3. Multiply −3 × 3.
Answer: −9

Factor structure

Prime factorize: 36

  1. Start with a factor pair: 36 = 6 × 6.
  2. Break each 6 into primes: 6 = 2 × 3.
  3. Collect the prime factors.
Answer: 36 = 2 × 2 × 3 × 3 = 2² × 3²

Common error traps

Catch these before they become habits.

Adding left-to-right too soon

In 6 + 4 × 3, do not add 6 + 4 first. Multiplication happens before addition.

Making absolute value negative

|−10| is 10, not −10, because absolute value is distance from zero.

Confusing exponent with multiplication

3² means 3 × 3, not 3 × 2. Say “three used as a factor two times.”
