      Deeper concept guide

      How to compare signed numbers without getting tricked by magnitude.

      This lesson is not only about choosing the correct inequality symbol. It is about learning to separate position, direction, and distance from zero.

      1 · Start with the number line

      On a horizontal number line, values increase as you move right. This rule is stronger than any shortcut. When a comparison feels confusing, imagine both integers placed on the line and ask which one is farther right.

      • Farther right means greater.
      • Farther left means less.
      • Zero is greater than every negative integer and less than every positive integer.

      2 · Treat negative numbers carefully

      Negative integers can feel backward because the number with the larger absolute value is often smaller. For example, −12 is less than −5 because −12 lies farther left, even though 12 is a greater distance from zero than 5.

      • Among negatives, closer to zero is greater.
      • More negative means farther left.
      • Do not compare only the digits after the sign.

      3 · Read inequalities as sentences

      An inequality should make sense when read from left to right. The open side of the symbol faces the greater value, and the pointed side faces the lesser value. After choosing a symbol, read the whole statement aloud.

      • −3 > −8 reads “negative three is greater than negative eight.”
      • −12 < −5 reads “negative twelve is less than negative five.”
      • The symbol must match the number-line order.

      4 · Use context to check meaning

      Signed numbers often describe temperature, elevation, money, score, or movement. The mathematical comparison should agree with the story: −1°C is warmer than −6°C, and −8 meters is higher than −25 meters when both are below sea level.

      • Ask what zero represents.
      • Ask which direction is positive or negative.
      • Translate the comparison back into context words.

      Study routine: plot or imagine each number, choose the farther-right value, write the inequality, then explain it in the real-world context. This prevents the common mistake of treating absolute value as signed order.
