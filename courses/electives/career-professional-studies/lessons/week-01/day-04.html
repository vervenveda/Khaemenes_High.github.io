2 · Core Lesson

Strengths, interests & transferable skills

Career planning begins with evidence, not labels. A transferable skill is a capability that can be useful in more than one setting: organizing information, explaining an idea, solving a problem, revising a draft, coordinating a group, learning a tool, or noticing patterns. Interests can guide exploration, but they are not permanent identities and they do not prove ability. Strong career reasoning separates preference from demonstrated skill and separates demonstrated skill from future potential. Evidence may come from school projects, family responsibilities, clubs, volunteer work, paid work, creative practice, independent learning, or other non-sensitive experiences. The goal is to describe what happened, what you contributed, and what changed because of your work. A useful skills inventory also names gaps without treating them as failures; a gap is a target for practice.

4 · Worked Model

Reasoning you can inspect

Instead of writing “I am a leader,” a learner writes: “During a three-person project, I created the task board, checked dependencies twice, and helped the group finish one day early. Evidence: completed task board and peer feedback.”

Common misconception

A career strength is a permanent personality label.

Correction

Strength claims should be supported by examples and can grow or change with practice.

7 · Test & Revise

Stress-test the work

  1. What claim or decision does your artifact make?
  2. What evidence supports it?
  3. What constraint or alternative could change it?
  4. What would a skeptical but fair reviewer ask?
  5. What revision improves accuracy, usefulness, or ethics?
8 · Reflection & Transfer
