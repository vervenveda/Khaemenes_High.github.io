Original Senior Reading

1. Framing the Inquiry

Iran, Brazil, and South Africa begins with a problem of disciplined judgment: How do constitutional design, identity, inequality, social movements, and political conflict shape different paths of change? A senior-level answer cannot rest on a label or personal preference. It must define the relevant concepts, identify the institutions and actors involved, locate trustworthy evidence, and explain what the evidence can and cannot establish.

This week’s conceptual vocabulary—theocracy, guardian institution, coalition presidentialism—provides an analytical language. These terms should not be memorized as isolated definitions. Students must use them to classify evidence, distinguish competing explanations, and test whether a conclusion changes across countries, institutions, or historical conditions.

The central field of study is iran’s theocratic-republican institutions; brazil’s presidential federalism and coalition politics; south africa’s constitutional democracy and post-apartheid transformation. The inquiry deliberately connects political science with history, economics, geography, law, and ethics. Each discipline contributes a different kind of question, and a defensible synthesis must keep factual, causal, legal, and normative claims distinct.

2. Institutions, Evidence, and Context

The principal evidence laboratory uses Constitutions, court decisions, election results, protest records, and inequality indicators. These records have different purposes, audiences, methods, and limitations. An official record may be authoritative about what an institution formally decided, but it may not explain implementation or lived effects. A news account can add chronology and testimony, but it should be traced to documents and corroborated when the claim is consequential.

Hybrid institutional design can combine elected and unelected authority. Constitutional transformation does not by itself eliminate social and economic inequality. Students should therefore create an evidence table that records source, authority, date, claim, supporting detail, limitation, and corroboration. Contradictory evidence is not an inconvenience to hide; it is a reason to refine the question or qualify the conclusion.

Context matters because the same institutional rule can operate differently under different party systems, social divisions, administrative capacities, economic conditions, or historical experiences. Comparison is strongest when students explain both the shared dimension being compared and the contextual differences that limit easy generalization.

3. Competing Explanations and Ethical Tensions

Coalition incentives influence both governability and corruption risk. Movements, courts, parties, and economic structures interact in political change. A senior analyst should reconstruct the strongest version of more than one explanation before judging among them. This requires identifying assumptions, mechanisms, expected evidence, and possible counterexamples.

Political and global questions frequently involve values that cannot be reduced to a single measurement. Liberty, equality, security, representation, prosperity, sustainability, and human dignity can reinforce one another, but they can also conflict. Ethical reasoning should state the principle being used, identify who bears costs and risks, and consider whether the same rule would be accepted if positions were reversed.

The goal is not artificial neutrality between accurate and inaccurate claims. It is intellectual fairness: use consistent standards, represent disagreement honestly, distinguish uncertainty from falsehood, and change a conclusion when better evidence requires revision.

4. Graduate Application and Performance

The principal performance for the week is the Iran–Brazil–South Africa Political Change Seminar. Students will move from comprehension to application by defining the problem, selecting evidence, comparing alternatives, and producing a conclusion that can survive questioning.

Every submission must contain a claim, cited evidence, an explanation connecting evidence to the claim, a limitation or counterargument, and a revision condition. A revision condition states what new evidence or failed assumption would justify changing the conclusion. This prevents certainty from becoming detached from proof.

The week ends with a mastery quiz and constructed response. A score below 80 percent triggers correction and reassessment. Corrections must explain why the original response was wrong, identify the controlling concept or evidence, and state the corrected reasoning. The purpose is durable mastery rather than punishment for an initial error.

Vocabulary

theocracy
A system in which religious authority or doctrine has a governing role.
guardian institution
An unelected body empowered to supervise or constrain elected institutions.
coalition presidentialism
Presidential governance dependent on multiparty legislative coalitions.
transformative constitution
A constitution designed to restructure institutions and remedy entrenched injustice.
racialized inequality
Unequal conditions structured by historical and institutional racial classification.
constitutional court
A court with special responsibility for constitutional interpretation.
