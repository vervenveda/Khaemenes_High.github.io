Weekly Reading

Setting the Stage

World War II grew from expansion by Nazi Germany, Fascist Italy, and militarist Japan, unresolved postwar tensions, and failures of collective security. The conflict became global, involving mass mobilization, occupation, strategic bombing, forced labor, and civilian displacement.

People, Power, and Systems

The Holocaust was not an accidental byproduct of war. Nazi racial ideology and antisemitism were translated into laws, bureaucratic procedures, property seizure, ghettos, shootings, deportations, forced labor, and killing centers. War created opportunities for radicalization, but people and institutions made choices at each stage.

Reading the Evidence

Survivor testimony, laws, photographs, diaries, transport records, perpetrator documents, artifacts, and trial evidence must be studied carefully. Ethical teaching avoids simulations that ask students to pretend to be victims or perpetrators. It centers human beings and precise evidence.

Why It Matters

Genocide study asks how identity, propaganda, law, institutions, war, and dehumanization interact. It also teaches that prevention depends on accurate warning, protected rights, accountable institutions, and action before mass violence reaches its final stages.

Source Study: Laws, Testimony, and Bureaucratic Records

Analyze a progression from discriminatory law to personal testimony and administrative evidence. Identify escalation, agency, and limits of each source.

Vocabulary

genocideActs committed with intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnic, racial, or religious group.
HolocaustThe systematic, state-sponsored persecution and murder of six million European Jews by Nazi Germany and its allies and collaborators.
antisemitismPrejudice, hostility, or discrimination directed against Jews.
ghettoA confined urban district where Jews were forcibly concentrated under Nazi occupation.
collaborationCooperation with an occupying or oppressive power, under conditions that may vary in coercion and choice.
resistanceOpposition to persecution or occupation through armed, unarmed, spiritual, cultural, or rescue activity.

Key Understandings

  • The Holocaust developed through escalating exclusion, law, dispossession, deportation, occupation, and mass murder.
  • Nazi Germany targeted Jews for total destruction and also persecuted Roma, disabled people, Poles, Soviet prisoners, political opponents, LGBTQ people, and others.
  • Choices by institutions, officials, collaborators, bystanders, rescuers, and resisters shaped events within unequal and dangerous conditions.
