Week 24 · Enlightenment and Political Thought

Essential Question: Who should hold political authority, and what rights or duties limit government?

Mastery: 80% minimum. Grade political reasoning, source analysis, institutional tradeoffs, contradiction/exclusion analysis, and evidence use—not philosopher-name recall.

Historical & Civic Safeguards

  • Do not teach “the Enlightenment” as one unified program. Require comparison among competing theories of authority, liberty, security, property, consent, religion, and representation.
  • Political texts are arguments written for historical audiences, not timeless neutral rulebooks.
  • Separate what a thinker claimed from what later political movements did with that claim.
  • Universal-rights language must be tested against slavery, colonialism, gender exclusion, property restrictions, and unequal citizenship.
  • Do not grade students on agreement with a political philosophy. Grade evidence, reasoning, tradeoffs, consequences, and counterargument.
  • Institutional diagrams show intended design; they do not prove that institutions functioned as designed.

Reading Lab

Use the synchronized Reading Lab: OpenStax 7.1 for broad context; selected Locke and Wollstonecraft excerpts; a balanced teacher-selected source set for comparison. Require creator, date, genre, audience, claim, evidence, limitation, and corroboration.

Research Studio

ARSHIF: Build a balanced source set across political theories and critics of exclusion.

Evidence & Citation Studio: Record claim → source → audience → political principle → institutional implication → exclusion/limitation → corroboration.

Atlas Evidence Analysis: Map print/public-sphere nodes and imperial/Atlantic connections while avoiding the claim that circulation equals acceptance.

PROSE: Write an evidence-based argument about how authority should be limited, using historical theories as evidence rather than treating the essay as partisan advocacy.

Constructed Response Rubric · 5 points

Require a defensible claim; at least three specific pieces of evidence from multiple source types; one institutional mechanism; one serious counterargument; and one qualification involving exclusion, implementation, or historical context.

Correction Protocol

Below 8/10: identify the reasoning error, reopen the relevant reading/source, correct the claim with evidence, and apply the repaired reasoning to a new political-design example before reassessment.
