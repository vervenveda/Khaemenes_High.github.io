Weekly Reading & Source Study

Ancient societies developed rich traditions for understanding human purpose and social order. Vedic traditions contributed to diverse forms of Hindu thought. Buddhism emerged in South Asia and examined suffering, desire, ethical conduct, and liberation. Ancient Israelite religion developed a covenantal and ethical monotheism. Confucian and Daoist thinkers responded to political disorder in China with different visions of harmony.

These traditions shaped institutions as well as personal practice. Ideas about duty, compassion, family responsibility, ritual, justice, and legitimate government affected education and law. Yet no tradition remained uniform. Teachers, communities, rulers, reformers, and ordinary practitioners interpreted ideas differently.

Read contextualized selections from the Hebrew Bible, Buddhist teachings, the Bhagavad Gita, Confucian Analects, and Daoist writings. Identify the question, audience, ethical claim, historical setting, useful evidence, and limitation. Do not treat one text as representative of every believer or period.

Key Vocabulary

  • monotheism — belief in one God.
  • dharma — a complex South Asian concept involving duty, order, conduct, and right living.
  • karma — a principle concerning moral consequences of actions, interpreted in varied ways across South Asian traditions.
  • nirvana — in Buddhism, liberation from craving, suffering, and the cycle of rebirth.
  • filial piety — responsibilities and respect within family relationships, emphasized in Confucian thought.
  • ethical tradition — teachings about right conduct, responsibility, and human flourishing.

Three Assignments

1 · Source Lab

Compare contextualized selections from at least three traditions. Complete origin, purpose, value, limitation, audience, claim, and corroboration notes. Write 150–250 words explaining what the evidence contributes to the essential question.

2 · Atlas and Data Investigation

Map the regions in which the traditions emerged and major routes of later diffusion. Create a concept matrix comparing duty, suffering, authority, community, and moral action. State one observed pattern, one plausible explanation, and one limitation.

3 · Evidence-Based Argument

Respond to: Can government create moral citizens, or must morality begin outside the state? Use at least three pieces of evidence, include a counterclaim or qualification, and conclude with broader historical significance.
