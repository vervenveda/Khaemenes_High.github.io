Weekly Reading

Building an Atlantic System

The Atlantic slave trade developed through interacting commercial demand, shipping networks, political decisions, finance, warfare and capture, colonial law, plantation production, and consumer markets. African societies were diverse and participated in different ways; Atlantic demand changed incentives and intensified capture and human trafficking in many regions. European colonial economies increasingly tied coerced labor to racialized, hereditary legal status.

Numbers and Human Lives

Voyage records help historians reconstruct patterns of embarkation, disembarkation, mortality, and scale, but databases are built from incomplete surviving evidence. Estimates should be treated as estimates. First-person narratives, petitions, advertisements, archaeology, material culture, family histories, and resistance records reveal experiences that aggregate data cannot.

Law, Property, and Racialization

Colonial laws could define enslaved people as property, restrict movement and family rights, and make status hereditary. A law reveals an official rule or claim, not automatic uniform implementation. Historians compare legal texts with court records, plantation records, petitions, local enforcement, and testimony.

Resistance Beyond Revolt

Resistance included survival, family and community building, maintaining cultural practices, work slowdowns, negotiation, escape, maroon communities, petitions, legal challenges, revolt, testimony, and abolitionist organizing. Because archives were often created by enslavers and governments, quieter forms of resistance may be harder to document.

Evidence Safeguards

  • Do not reduce enslaved people to cargo, totals, or passive victims.
  • Do not treat Africa as one political actor or all slavery systems as identical.
  • State the denominator, time span, units, and uncertainty when using quantitative evidence.
  • A law shows official classification or policy; practice requires corroboration.
  • Resistance includes everyday, cultural, legal, migratory, and collective forms.
  • Moral explanation is graded on reasoning, evidence, power, consequences, perspective, and justification—not agreement with a preferred position.
