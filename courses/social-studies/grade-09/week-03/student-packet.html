Weekly Reading

Mobility Before Farming

For most of human history, people lived in mobile or semi-mobile communities. They used detailed environmental knowledge to gather plants, hunt, fish, manage fire, make tools, create art, exchange goods, build shelter, and move with seasons. Archaeological evidence shows complex adaptation and innovation long before settled agriculture.

A Gradual Agricultural Transition

Between roughly 12,000 and 4,000 BCE, communities in several regions increasingly domesticated plants and animals. This process did not begin once and spread everywhere in the same way. Many groups combined cultivation, herding, hunting, gathering, and fishing for long periods. Farming could support larger populations and stored food, but it could also increase labor demands, disease exposure, dependence on harvests, land claims, and conflict.

Reading Archaeological Evidence

Archaeologists examine seeds, animal bones, pollen, grinding stones, storage pits, settlement layers, burials, tools, and human remains. A single artifact rarely proves a broad social claim. Researchers compare location, date, pattern, and multiple evidence types while accounting for preservation bias and uncertainty.

Surplus, Specialization, and Inequality

Food surplus could support craft specialists, exchange, leadership, and protection against short shortages. It could also make stored food, fertile land, and labor resources worth controlling. Differences in houses, burials, diet, or access to goods may suggest inequality, but the exact social meaning requires corroboration.

Five Daily Lessons

Monday · Mobility, Adaptation, and Deep Human Time

Map early human movement and identify how mobility could function as an adaptation. Separate evidence, inference, and uncertainty.

Tuesday · Reading an Early Village Through Material Evidence

Analyze storage pits, bones, tools, site layout, and other remains. Record an observation, a supported inference, and one claim the evidence cannot establish alone.

Wednesday · Independent Domestication and Agricultural Transition

Map multiple centers of domestication and compare mobility, diet, labor, population, disease risk, storage, land claims, and inequality.

Thursday · Surplus, Specialization, and Unequal Access

Sort evidence into benefits, costs, and ambiguous effects. Construct a qualified argument that avoids a universal progress story.

Friday · Agricultural Revolution Mastery Audit
