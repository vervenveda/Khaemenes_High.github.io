Part I · Stimulus-Based Multiple Choice · 60 points

Set A · Evidence, Environment, and Early Societies

An excavated settlement contains storage pits, grinding stones, domesticated-grain remains, houses rebuilt over generations, burials, and specialized stone-working debris. A nearby pollen record shows environmental change. No written records survive.

1. Which claim is best supported?

2. Why is the pollen record useful?

3. Which method is strongest when no alphabetic archive survives?

4. Which statement best avoids environmental determinism?

Set B · States, Law, Belief, and Membership

A ruler's inscription announces fair administration, public order, religious duty, and protection of subjects. Local records from another province preserve tax disputes and petitions against officials.

5. What does the inscription establish most directly?

6. Why are the local records important?

7. Which comparison of political membership is strongest?

8. Which evidence most directly demonstrates state capacity?

Set C · Knowledge, Africa, and Indigenous Evidence

A scholarly work survives in several languages with commentary and corrections. Elsewhere, an excavation contains locally made goods and imports; oral traditions preserve genealogy and political memory; an outsider traveler describes the society dramatically.

9. Which interpretation of translation is strongest?

10. What do imported objects in secure archaeological context establish most directly?

11. Why should oral tradition not automatically rank below written evidence?

12. What is an appropriate rule when using living Indigenous knowledge?

Set D · East Asia, Medieval Europe, and Exchange

One region expands examinations, printing, commercial cities, canals, and state administration. Another contains royal law, customary law, religious courts, local charters, guilds, and negotiated obligations. Both connect to wider trade systems.

13. Why should examinations not automatically be described as complete meritocracy?

14. What can expanded printing establish most directly?

15. Why does “feudalism” require caution as a historical category?

16. Why are long-distance exchange systems better modeled as networks than as single roads?

Set E · Mongol Expansion, Plague, and Global Comparison

A map shows Mongol-controlled territories and commercial corridors. Separate evidence documents plague occurrence at several sites and regional population losses, but dates and paths do not align perfectly.

17. Which explanation of Mongol expansion is strongest?

18. What can the route map establish about plague transmission by itself?

19. Why should exact Black Death mortality percentages be treated cautiously?

20. Which statement best synthesizes global comparison and connectivity?

Part II · Constructed Responses · 20 points

21 · Source & Implementation · 10 points

A ruler's law, edict, charter, or decree is discovered. Explain how a historian should investigate whether the policy shaped local life. Use at least three evidence categories and identify one limitation.

22 · Networks & Uneven Consequences · 10 points

Using examples from at least three course regions or units, explain how connectivity could create both opportunity and vulnerability. Trace at least two mechanisms and qualify one comparison.
