Part I · Stimulus-Based Multiple Choice · 60 points

Set A · Evidence, Geography, and Early Human Societies

Stimulus: At an excavated settlement, archaeologists find storage pits, grinding stones, domesticated-grain remains, animal bones, houses rebuilt over generations, and burials. A nearby pollen record shows changing vegetation. No written records survive.

1. Which claim is best supported?

2. Why is the pollen record useful?

3. Which additional evidence would best test whether food surplus supported specialization?

4. Which statement best avoids environmental determinism?

5. Which method is strongest when no written archive survives?

Set B · States, Law, Belief, and Political Membership

Stimulus: A ruler's inscription announces fair administration, public order, religious duty, and protection of subjects. Local records from another province show tax disputes and petitions against officials.

6. What does the inscription establish most directly?

7. Why are the local records important?

8. Which comparison of political membership is strongest?

9. Which statement best describes the role of belief in political systems?

10. Which evidence most directly demonstrates state capacity?

Set C · Empires and Knowledge Networks

Stimulus: A scholarly work survives in several languages. One translation changes technical vocabulary and includes commentary. A later scholar cites that version while correcting one calculation.

11. Which interpretation is strongest?

12. Which research design best tests transformation?

13. Which statement best avoids a single-civilization progress narrative?

14. What can a map linking scholarly cities establish most safely?

15. Which statement best describes imperial continuity?

Set D · African and Indigenous Histories

Stimulus: A coastal excavation contains locally made pottery, imported ceramics, architectural remains, and coins. A traveler describes the city in dramatic language. Oral historical traditions preserve genealogies and political memory.

16. What do imported ceramics establish most directly?

17. How should the traveler's dramatic description be used?

18. Why should oral tradition not automatically rank below written evidence?

19. Which rule is appropriate when using living Indigenous knowledge?

20. Which claim best avoids measuring complexity by alphabetic writing?

Set E · East Asia, Medieval Europe, and Exchange Networks

Stimulus: A government expands examinations and printing while commercial cities grow. Another region uses charters, guilds, religious courts, local customs, and royal law. Both participate in long-distance exchange.

21. Why should examinations not automatically be called complete meritocracy?

22. What can expanded printing establish most directly?

23. Why is “feudalism” a category that requires caution?

24. Which interpretation of cultural borrowing is strongest?

25. Why are exchange networks better modeled as nodes and intermediaries than as one road?

Set F · Mongol Expansion, Plague, and Global Comparison

Stimulus: A map displays Mongol-controlled territories and commercial corridors. Separate datasets show plague evidence at several sites and regional population declines. The dates do not perfectly align.

26. Which explanation of Mongol expansion is strongest?

27. What can the map establish about plague transmission by itself?

28. Why should exact mortality percentages be treated cautiously?

29. Which comparison method is strongest for the world of 1200–1450?

30. Which statement best synthesizes connectivity?

Part II · Constructed Responses · 20 points

31 · Source & Implementation · 10 points

32 · Networks & Uneven Consequences · 10 points

Using examples from at least three course regions or units, explain how connectivity could create both opportunity and vulnerability. Trace at least two mechanisms and qualify one comparison.
