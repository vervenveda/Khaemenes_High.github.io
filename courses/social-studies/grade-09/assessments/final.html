Assessment Purpose

This examination measures durable understanding of the full 36-week course—not vocabulary recall alone. Students must interpret unfamiliar evidence, compare societies, reason about causation and continuity/change, analyze geography and networks, evaluate claims, and construct evidence-based arguments.

Time: 150 minutes   Total: 100 points   Mastery: 80%

Mastery rule: A student must demonstrate at least 80% overall. A score below 80% requires correction by error category and reassessment. Objective accuracy alone does not constitute mastery if constructed reasoning is deficient.

Part I · Stimulus-Based Multiple Choice · 40 points

Choose the best-supported answer. Several questions intentionally require qualification rather than the most absolute statement.

Source Set A · Evidence and Early Complexity

An excavated settlement contains domesticated-grain remains, storage facilities, houses rebuilt over generations, craft debris, burials, and evidence of changing vegetation. No written archive survives.

1. Which conclusion is best supported?
2. Which additional evidence would best test a claim that agricultural surplus increased inequality?
3. What is the strongest methodological response to the absence of deciphered writing?
4. Which statement best avoids environmental determinism?
Source Set B · States, Law, and Membership

A ruler's inscription announces fair administration and protection. Local petitions from another province complain about taxes and official conduct. An archaeological survey documents roads, storage facilities, and administrative buildings.

5. What does the ruler's inscription establish most directly?
6. Why are the petitions especially valuable?
7. Which evidence most directly demonstrates state capacity?
8. Which comparison of political membership is strongest?
Source Set C · Networks and Regional Worlds

A map shows ports, caravan routes, river corridors, and inland exchange nodes. Imported materials occur at several sites. A travel account describes one marketplace, while local records show different goods moving through different intermediaries.

9. What can secure provenance of a nonlocal material establish most directly?
10. Why should Silk Roads and Indian Ocean exchange be modeled as networks rather than single routes?
11. What can a map of ports establish without additional evidence?
12. Which statement best describes cultural diffusion?
Source Set D · Political Thought and Revolution

A political pamphlet argues that government exists to protect rights. Another text emphasizes social order and legitimate authority. A revolutionary declaration asserts equality while the society issuing it continues to exclude some groups from political membership.

13. Which historical interpretation is strongest?
14. Which evidence would best test whether an announced right was implemented?
15. Which statement best distinguishes revolution from reform?
16. Why should a historian distinguish political ideals from political implementation?
Source Set E · Industrialization, Imperialism, and Global Change

A city expands rapidly as factories grow. Production increases, but workers report long hours and unsafe conditions. An imperial government describes overseas expansion as modernization; local writers describe land loss, labor coercion, and resistance.

17. Which explanation best captures industrialization?
18. Which source comparison best tests an imperial government's modernization claim?
19. Why can imperialism not be explained by technology alone?
20. Which evidence most directly demonstrates a change in workers' lived experience?
Source Set F · World Wars, Interwar Crisis, and Mass Politics

A society experiences war losses, inflation, unemployment, political polarization, and distrust in institutions. Some political movements promise democratic reform; others promise national restoration through concentrated authority and mass propaganda.

21. Which explanation best addresses why societies responded differently to interwar crisis?
22. What is the strongest way to analyze propaganda?
23. Why should exact historical mortality estimates sometimes be qualified?
24. Which statement best captures continuity and change?
Source Set G · Contemporary World and Civic Life

A current civic claim cites a statistic, a short video, and an AI-generated summary. The statistic's denominator is unclear, the video lacks its original context, and the AI summary provides no sources. A separate public dataset contains a methodology note.

25. Which source should receive the strongest initial evidentiary weight for the numerical claim?
26. What is the key problem with an unclear denominator?
27. Which practice best applies the course's source-evaluation method to AI output?
28. Which statement best answers the course's final civic question?
29. A historian sees the same institution in two societies. What is the strongest next step?
30. Which claim demonstrates causal reasoning rather than sequence alone?
31. Which statement best distinguishes a historical claim from a description?
32. Which evidence combination is strongest for testing a broad historical interpretation?
33. Why should historians identify whose experience is missing from a source set?
34. What is the strongest use of a counterexample?
35. Which student response demonstrates the highest level of historical reasoning?
36. Which statement best represents the course's approach to “civilization”?
37. Which statement best explains why historical categories can be useful yet imperfect?
38. Which statement best describes state capacity across the course?
39. Which statement best captures global interconnection?
40. Which principle should govern the final historical judgment?

Part II · Source Analysis · 20 points

41. A central government announces a new tax policy. A local official's report praises compliance. A merchant petition disputes the tax burden. Archaeological evidence shows new storage facilities in some districts but not others.

Write a source-analysis response explaining what each source can establish, what it cannot establish alone, and how you would corroborate the competing claims. Identify at least three source types and one limitation.

10 points. Origin/context 2 · Evidence 3 · Corroboration 3 · Limitation/qualification 2.

42. A map shows a trade corridor expanding during a period of political consolidation. Population increases at two nodes, while a third node declines. A later historian claims that trade caused prosperity everywhere.

Evaluate the claim. Distinguish spatial association from causation, identify at least two mechanisms that could connect trade to population change, and provide one alternative explanation.

10 points. Evidence interpretation 3 · Causal mechanism 3 · Alternative explanation 2 · Qualification 2.

Part III · Comparative Historical Reasoning · 20 points

43. Compare two societies studied in the course that faced a similar problem—such as administering territory, managing labor, legitimizing authority, organizing exchange, or responding to crisis.

Your response must:

  • make a defensible comparative thesis;
  • use evidence from both societies;
  • explain at least one meaningful similarity and one meaningful difference;
  • identify why the outcomes differed; and
  • include a qualification or counterexample.

20 points. Thesis 4 · Evidence 6 · Comparison 4 · Causal/contextual reasoning 4 · Qualification 2.

Part IV · Cumulative DBQ / Civic Transfer · 20 points

44. Use the following course question:

How can historical reasoning help people make wiser civic decisions when modern claims are incomplete, persuasive, or contested?

Construct a response that connects at least four different course periods or regions to a modern evidence-evaluation problem. Your argument must:

  • state a defensible thesis;
  • use at least four specific historical examples;
  • explain mechanisms rather than merely listing examples;
  • include one counterexample or competing interpretation;
  • distinguish historical analogy from proof;
  • identify at least one limitation or uncertainty; and
  • conclude with a practical principle for evaluating a contemporary claim.

20 points. Thesis 4 · Evidence 5 · Historical reasoning 5 · Counterexample/qualification 3 · Civic transfer 2 · Limitation 1.

Mastery & Recovery Protocol

A final score below 80% is not a failure of the student; it is evidence that specific concepts or reasoning skills require additional work.

  1. Classify each missed item as knowledge, evidence analysis, geography/data, causation, comparison, continuity/change, source evaluation, or argumentation.
  2. Revisit the relevant course lesson(s) and complete a targeted correction.
  3. For constructed responses, revise the deficient reasoning rather than merely copying a model answer.
  4. Complete a reassessment containing new stimuli and parallel skills.
  5. Mastery is recorded only when the reassessment demonstrates the required standard.
