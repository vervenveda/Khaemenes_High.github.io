Week 15: Networks of Exchange

Essential Question: How did long-distance exchange transform societies far from the merchants who carried the goods?

Mastery: 80% minimum. Grade students on network reasoning, mechanisms, evidence handling, chronology, geographic qualification, and uncertainty—not vocabulary recall or tool completion.

Core Safeguards

  • Teach Silk Roads, Indian Ocean, trans-Saharan, and regional systems as overlapping networks rather than single continuous routes.
  • A mapped route line does not prove simultaneous operation, direction, frequency, volume, political control, or causation.
  • An imported artifact can establish material connection but not its complete route, ownership history, or meaning without corroboration.
  • “Trade,” “diffusion,” and “connectivity” name processes or relationships; students must identify actors, mechanisms, chronology, and local conditions.
  • Traveler accounts are evidence, not neutral windows. Evaluate provenance, audience, expectations, language, context, and corroboration.
  • Premodern exchange may be compared with modern supply chains only when differences in scale, speed, technology, institutions, and data are preserved.

Research · Evidence · Production Studio

ARSHIF: Locate approved materials for one exchange-network case: merchant/traveler accounts, port or caravan evidence, commodities, coins, ceramics, shipwrecks, plants, technologies, cultural/religious transmission, state policy, or trust/credit institutions. Preserve provenance and chronology.

Evidence & Citation Studio: Require at least three source categories. Students record stage/node, claim, provenance, useful evidence, limitation, confidence, inference, and corroboration needed.

Atlas Evidence Analysis: Use verified Protools/Atlas_Evidence_Analysis.html. Organize production zones, transfer nodes, ports/caravan sites, waterways/desert/ocean corridors, political jurisdictions, claims, timeline, evidence status, provenance, and alternatives. Atlas organizes evidence and uncertainty; it does not determine historical truth.

PROSE: 700–1000 word honors report explaining how one exchange network transformed societies beyond the merchants carrying goods. Require a mechanism, uneven effects, multi-source evidence, an evidentiary limitation, and qualification of any modern analogy.

Assignment Guidance

Network Map: Full credit requires nodes and actors, not decorative route lines. Students must identify what is directly evidenced and what is reconstructed.

Commodity Biography: Do not award full credit to a seamless narrative that hides gaps. Strong work explicitly labels uncertain transitions.

Risk & Institutions: Students should connect specific problems—loss, theft, information, payment, seasonality, taxation, political instability—to practices that reduce or redistribute them.

Diffusion Case: Require transmission plus local adoption, adaptation, rejection, or unequal consequence. “It spread through trade” is insufficient by itself.

Constructed Response Rubric · 5 points

Require at least three specific pieces of evidence from at least three source categories; a defensible network claim; one traced mechanism; one uneven consequence; and one meaningful evidentiary limitation or uncertainty.

Correction Protocol

Below 8/10: identify the missed network-reasoning concept, reopen the relevant source/Atlas dossier, explain why the original answer failed, correct it with evidence, and retake. Vocabulary copying is not a correction.
