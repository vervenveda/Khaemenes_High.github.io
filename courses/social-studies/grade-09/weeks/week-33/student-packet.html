Weekly Reading

Setting the Stage

European empires weakened during two world wars, while colonized soldiers, workers, students, veterans, women, religious leaders, and political organizations demanded self-government. India’s independence and partition, African liberation movements, Arab nationalism, and Caribbean independence followed different paths.

People, Power, and Systems

Nonviolent campaigns could mobilize mass participation and expose repression, while armed movements argued that colonial regimes would not leave voluntarily. In many cases, strategies overlapped. Cold War powers supported or opposed movements according to strategic interests.

Reading the Evidence

The Universal Declaration of Human Rights established a common standard after wartime atrocities. Anticolonial leaders used human-rights and self-determination language, but new governments faced poverty, borders drawn by empires, unequal trade, regional divisions, and institutional shortages.

Why It Matters

Decolonization did not simply replace foreign rulers with stable nation-states. It opened ongoing debates about citizenship, language, land, minority rights, development, and historical memory.

Source Study: Independence Voices and the UDHR

Compare speeches or writings from India, Ghana, Algeria, Kenya, or South Africa with selected human-rights articles. Identify shared principles and distinct strategies.

Vocabulary

decolonizationThe ending of colonial rule and creation or recovery of political sovereignty.
partitionThe division of territory into separate political units.
nonviolenceOrganized resistance that rejects physical violence while using protest, boycott, noncooperation, or civil disobedience.
apartheidSouth Africa’s legally enforced system of racial classification and domination.
Universal Declaration of Human RightsThe 1948 United Nations statement of common human rights principles.
nation-buildingThe political and social work of building institutions, identity, and legitimacy within a state.

Key Understandings

  • Decolonization resulted from organized movements, imperial weakness, global war, international pressure, and changing ideas of legitimacy.
  • Independence strategies ranged from negotiation and mass nonviolence to armed struggle and international diplomacy.
  • New states inherited borders, economies, institutions, and conflicts shaped by colonial rule but made consequential choices of their own.
