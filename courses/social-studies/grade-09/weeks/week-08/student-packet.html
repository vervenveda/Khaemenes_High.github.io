Weekly Reading

State formation and political change

Rome expanded from a city-state through republican government and eventually imperial rule. Civil conflict and concentration of authority transformed Roman politics, but some republican institutions and offices continued in altered forms. In East Asia, Qin unification preceded the Han dynasty, which developed and adapted imperial institutions across a large territory.

Administration from center to locality

Roman government used law, taxation, provincial administration, military institutions, officials, and local elites. Han government used imperial officials, commanderies and kingdoms, household registration, taxation, legal administration, and relationships with local elites. Later Chinese examination systems should not simply be projected backward onto the Han period.

Networks and frontiers

Roads, sea lanes, rivers, defenses, and exchange corridors moved soldiers, officials, messages, taxes, and goods. The Silk Roads were connected networks involving many regions and intermediaries rather than one road directly linking Rome and the Han capital. Infrastructure increased imperial reach, but control remained uneven.

Membership and cultural integration

Roman citizenship was a changing legal status that expanded over centuries, while social inequality remained. Han political and social membership followed different institutions and categories. Romanization is best understood as uneven spread and local adaptation of Roman language, law, institutions, and practices—not the automatic replacement of local cultures.

Transformation rather than disappearance

Neither empire simply vanished. Han rule ended and political division followed, but institutions and traditions continued in changed forms. Western Roman imperial government fragmented in the fifth century while the eastern Roman Empire continued. Warfare, fiscal strain, succession struggles, disease, migration, frontier pressures, elite competition, and local adaptation interacted differently in each case.

Vocabulary

republicA political order in which public authority operates through civic institutions rather than hereditary monarchy alone.
imperial bureaucracyA network of officials and administrative institutions used to govern an empire.
citizenshipA recognized legal or political status carrying particular rights and obligations; its meaning changes across societies and periods.
RomanizationThe uneven spread, negotiation, and local adaptation of Roman language, law, customs, and institutions.
Silk RoadsConnected overland exchange networks linking regions of East, Central, South, and Southwest Asia and the Mediterranean world through many intermediaries.
continuity and changeHistorical reasoning that identifies what persisted, what transformed, and why.

Daily Lessons

Monday · From Republics and Warring States to Empires

Create parallel Rome and Qin-Han timelines. Identify three turning points for each and label each continuity, change, or both.

Tuesday · Law, Office, Taxation, and Administration

Analyze Roman and Han administrative evidence. Ask how orders, revenue, information, and personnel moved from central institutions to local communities.

Wednesday · Roads, Frontiers, Exchange, and Uneven Integration

Map Roman roads and sea lanes beside Han roads, rivers, frontier defenses, and exchange corridors. Explain what moved through each network and what the map cannot prove.

Thursday · Citizenship, Elites, Cultural Integration, and Imperial Legitimacy

Build a comparison matrix for legal status, local elites, service obligations, culture, law, and access to authority. Do not assume Roman citizenship and Han membership were equivalent.

Friday · Transformation, Fragmentation, and Legacy

Create a causal web separating long-term pressures, triggers, feedback loops, political fragmentation, and continuities. Then complete the mastery assessment.

Week 8 Mastery Quiz

Mastery threshold: 80% (8 of 10 objective questions).

1. Which statement best describes a major political transformation in Rome?
□ A. Republican institutions disappeared immediately when Rome expanded beyond Italy.
□ B. Rome moved from republican government through civil conflict toward imperial rule while some earlier institutions and offices continued in altered forms.
□ C. Roman citizenship never changed after the early republic.
□ D. The Roman Empire was governed through the same institutions as Han China.

2. Which statement best describes Han imperial administration?
□ A. It depended only on hereditary kings with no central officials.
□ B. It used the fully developed examination system of much later imperial China without modification.
□ C. It combined imperial officials, territorial administration, household registration, taxation, law, and local relationships to govern a large realm.
□ D. It copied Roman provincial government.

3. Why should historians be cautious about describing the Silk Roads as one road connecting Rome directly to Han China?
□ A. Long-distance exchange did not exist.
□ B. The networks involved multiple routes, regions, and intermediaries, and goods could travel farther than individual merchants.
□ C. Only armies used overland routes.
□ D. Han China prohibited all foreign exchange.

4. What is the strongest interpretation of Romanization?
□ A. Roman culture completely replaced every local culture.
□ B. Every province adopted Roman practices at the same rate.
□ C. Roman language, law, institutions, and practices spread unevenly and were adapted within local contexts.
□ D. It refers only to Roman military conquest.

5. Which comparison of Roman citizenship and Han political membership is most defensible?
□ A. They were identical legal systems.
□ B. Roman citizenship was a changing legal status, while Han subjects were organized through different imperial, household, administrative, and social categories.
□ C. Han subjects voted in Roman-style assemblies.
□ D. Neither empire distinguished among legal or social statuses.

6. Which evidence best demonstrates that a central imperial policy reached local communities?
□ A. A law issued in the capital by itself
□ B. A modern textbook summary
□ C. Local administrative records, inscriptions, artifacts, or correspondence showing implementation or response
□ D. The size of the empire on a map

7. Which statement best explains the role of infrastructure in both empires?
□ A. Roads automatically produced complete political control.
□ B. Infrastructure helped move officials, soldiers, information, taxes, and goods, but control and integration remained uneven.
□ C. Infrastructure was used only for trade.
□ D. Frontier regions had no effect on imperial government.

8. Why is “collapse” an insufficient explanation for the end of Han rule and western Roman imperial government?
□ A. Neither experienced political fragmentation.
□ B. One single cause explains both cases.
□ C. Political fragmentation resulted from interacting pressures and was accompanied by significant institutional and cultural continuities.
□ D. Both empires ended in the same century.

9. Which statement about Rome after the fifth century CE is most accurate?
□ A. All Roman government and institutions disappeared everywhere in 476 CE.
□ B. The western imperial government fragmented while the eastern Roman Empire continued for centuries.
□ C. Han officials took control of the eastern Mediterranean.
□ D. Roman law immediately ceased to influence later societies.

10. Which comparison best demonstrates continuity-and-change reasoning?
□ A. Rome and Han were the same because both were empires.
□ B. Both empires weakened only because of invasions.
□ C. Identify which institutions, identities, practices, and networks persisted, which transformed, and how interacting pressures altered political structures in each case.
□ D. List rulers without explaining relationships among events.

Constructed Response

Using at least three specific pieces of evidence from Rome and Han China and at least two source types, explain how large empires built durable institutions and why those institutions later weakened, fragmented, or changed. Identify at least one important continuity after political fragmentation.
