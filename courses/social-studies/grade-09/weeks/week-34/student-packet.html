Globalization, Trade, Development, and Migration

Essential Question: How should societies evaluate the gains, risks, and unequal effects of global interdependence?

Weekly Reading

Setting the Stage

Modern supply chains divide design, extraction, manufacturing, logistics, marketing, and finance across many countries. Container shipping, digital communication, trade agreements, and global finance reduced some transaction costs while increasing dependence on distant systems.

People, Power, and Systems

Comparative advantage explains why specialization can raise total output, but real trade also involves bargaining power, labor standards, strategic industries, environmental costs, and adjustment for displaced workers. Consumers may benefit from lower prices while communities bear hidden costs.

Reading the Evidence

Development indicators such as income, life expectancy, schooling, inequality, and emissions answer different questions. A country can improve one measure while worsening another. Migration data must distinguish stocks, flows, refugees, internally displaced people, and temporary workers.

Why It Matters

Globalization is neither a single force nor a yes-or-no choice. Policy determines how risks and benefits are distributed and how resilient communities are when supply chains, climate, or conflict disrupt normal exchange.

Source Study: A Global Product Investigation

Trace one product through raw materials, labor, transport, ownership, retail, disposal, and recycling. Cite at least four evidence types.

Vocabulary

globalizationIncreasing cross-border connection in production, finance, information, culture, and movement.
supply chainThe linked stages through which a good or service is designed, produced, transported, and sold.
comparative advantageThe ability to produce a good at a lower opportunity cost than another producer.
development indicatorA measure used to describe economic or social conditions.
remittanceMoney sent by migrants to people in another place, often family members.
sustainabilityMeeting present needs while protecting long-term ecological and social capacity.

Key Understandings

  • Global production can lower prices and expand opportunity while shifting labor, environmental, and security risks across borders.
  • Development must be evaluated through health, education, rights, inequality, resilience, and environment as well as income.
  • Migration is shaped by work, family, conflict, climate, policy, networks, and individual agency.
