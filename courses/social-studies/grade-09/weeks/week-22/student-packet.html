Weekly Reading

Oceanic Expansion Was a System

Portuguese and Spanish voyages did not depend on individual courage alone. Sailors, pilots, shipbuilders, financiers, rulers, translators, merchants, port communities, navigational knowledge, and earlier Mediterranean and Afro-Eurasian connections all contributed. Technologies changed what voyages were possible, but institutions, labor, money, political goals, and accumulated knowledge shaped what actually happened.

Encounter, Alliance, Resistance, and Conquest

European expeditions entered worlds already shaped by complex Indigenous political relationships. Spanish conquests depended partly on Indigenous allies, rivalries, translators, local political decisions, disease, military factors, and contingent events. Indigenous peoples negotiated, resisted, allied, adapted, fled, fought, preserved communities, and continued shaping colonial societies.

The Columbian Exchange

After sustained Atlantic contact, plants, animals, pathogens, people, commodities, and knowledge moved between ecological systems that had long been separated. Maize, potatoes, cassava, tomatoes, horses, cattle, sugar, and many other organisms moved across oceans. Movement did not mean equal adoption or equal benefit. Local ecology, labor systems, markets, institutions, and power shaped outcomes.

Demographic Catastrophe and Colonial Labor

Indigenous population losses were catastrophic in many regions, but historians should avoid one-cause explanations. Pathogen exposure interacted with warfare, forced labor, displacement, hunger, ecological disruption, and social breakdown. Exact population estimates remain uncertain because evidence is incomplete and uneven. Colonial institutions such as encomienda also require law-versus-practice analysis: official obligations did not necessarily match lived labor conditions.

Reading Conquest Evidence

Expedition accounts can be valuable while still reflecting political goals, audience, translation, and self-justification. The Florentine Codex preserves Nahua testimony and images about conquest, but it was compiled after the events through a colonial-era collaborative process. Historians therefore compare perspectives and preserve provenance rather than treating any single account as a complete record.

Evidence & Ethics Safeguards

  • Explorer biography is not a complete causal explanation.
  • European victory was not automatic; preserve Indigenous agency and contingency.
  • Eyewitness evidence is not automatically neutral.
  • Movement on a map does not prove adoption, scale, or consequence.
  • One epidemic does not explain every demographic change.
  • Official colonial law does not automatically describe lived practice.
  • Historical explanation should contextualize actions without erasing harm or unequal power.

Daily Investigations

Monday · Oceanic Expansion as a System

Build a mechanism map connecting navigational knowledge, ship technology, crews/pilots, ports, state finance, commercial competition, political goals, and prior connections. Identify which relationships are directly evidenced and which remain inferred.

Tuesday · Encounter, Alliance, Resistance & Conquest

Compare an expedition account, an Indigenous/Nahua account or image, and another critical or colonial source. Record creator/community, date, audience, language/translation, claim, useful evidence, limitation, and corroboration.

Wednesday · Columbian Exchange & Demographic/Ecological Change

Use Atlas Evidence Analysis to map selected biological transfers and documented encounter zones. For one transfer, trace movement → local adoption or exposure → mechanism → consequence → evidence → uncertainty. Build a separate demographic model including disease, warfare, forced labor, displacement, hunger, and ecological disruption.

Thursday · Encomienda and Colonial Institutions

Compare an official description or law with evidence about implementation and lived experience. Separate stated obligation, actual practice, coercion, resistance, economic purpose, and what the evidence cannot establish.

Friday · Connection, Catastrophe & Responsibility

Synthesize the week's evidence. Explain how connection and coercion operated together, then complete the mastery assessment and begin the PROSE argument.

Week 22 Mastery Quiz

Mastery threshold: 80% (8/10).

  1. Which explanation best accounts for sustained European oceanic expansion after 1450?
    □ A Individual courage alone. □ B Accumulated maritime knowledge, ship technology, pilots and crews, state finance, commercial competition, political goals, ports, and prior Afro-Eurasian connections interacted. □ C The compass automatically created overseas empires. □ D Europe had no earlier maritime traditions.
  2. Why is it misleading to describe Spanish conquest in the Americas as an automatic victory of Europeans over Indigenous peoples?
    □ A Military technology had no importance. □ B Conquests depended on Indigenous alliances and rivalries, translation, disease, local politics, contingent decisions, and military factors as well as Spanish forces. □ C Indigenous peoples never resisted. □ D All Indigenous societies responded identically.
  3. What is the strongest way to use Columbus's journal as historical evidence?
    □ A Treat it as neutral because Columbus witnessed events. □ B Analyze audience, purpose, language, political goals, and claims, then compare it with Indigenous, archaeological, and other independent evidence. □ C Reject it because all European sources are false. □ D Use only passages describing geography.
  4. Why is the Florentine Codex useful but methodologically complex for studying conquest?
    □ A It contains no Indigenous perspectives. □ B It preserves Nahua accounts and images gathered after the conquest through a colonial-era collaborative process, so timing, mediation, language, and compilation context must be considered. □ C It was written before Spanish contact. □ D It is an unmediated transcript of every witness.
  5. Which statement best explains the Columbian Exchange?
    □ A It was only a trade in food crops. □ B It involved movement of plants, animals, pathogens, people, commodities, and knowledge between previously separated ecological systems, with uneven consequences shaped by local conditions. □ C Every transfer produced the same effect. □ D It affected only the Americas.
  6. Why should Indigenous demographic collapse after contact be explained through multiple interacting mechanisms?
    □ A Disease played no role. □ B Pathogen exposure interacted with warfare, forced labor, displacement, hunger, ecological disruption, and social breakdown, with regional variation and uncertain estimates. □ C One epidemic explains every population change. □ D Population decline occurred uniformly.
  7. What can a law defining encomienda establish by itself?
    □ A Exact labor conditions everywhere. □ B Official legal claims and obligations; implementation, coercion, resistance, and lived experience require additional evidence. □ C Indigenous workers freely accepted the system. □ D The institution operated identically in every colony.
  8. A map shows a crop moving from the Americas to several Eurasian regions. What can it establish most safely?
    □ A The crop immediately transformed every region. □ B A spatial/chronological pattern of documented movement; adoption, scale, diet, economic impact, and causation require corroboration. □ C Every farmer adopted it. □ D It moved by one route only.
  9. Which approach best analyzes moral responsibility in conquest and colonization?
    □ A Judge only intentions. □ B Distinguish intention, action, institutional structure, coercion, consequence, available choices, and evidence while recognizing that explanation does not erase harm. □ C Avoid moral analysis entirely. □ D Treat every participant as having equal power and choices.
  10. Which synthesis best answers how oceanic expansion created connection while producing catastrophe?
    □ A Connection was automatically beneficial. □ B Maritime expansion linked ecological and political systems through trade, migration, knowledge, and biological exchange while conquest, forced labor, disease, dispossession, and ecological change produced profoundly unequal consequences. □ C Only military conquest mattered. □ D The Columbian Exchange had no relationship to colonial institutions.
