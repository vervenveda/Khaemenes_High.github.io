Week 17: Global Comparison, 1200–1450

Essential Question: Which patterns of power, exchange, belief, and inequality best explain the world of 1200–1450?

Mastery: 80% minimum. Evaluate comparative reasoning, contextualization, sourcing, corroboration, geographic/quantitative interpretation, thesis construction, and qualification—not vocabulary recall.

Comparative Safeguards

  • Comparison is not ranking. Do not organize societies on an “advanced/primitive” ladder.
  • Apply consistent analytical categories while preserving differences in region, chronology, ecology, institutions, and evidence.
  • Do not treat modern nation-states as timeless historical units.
  • Official laws, tribute claims, religious ideals, and administrative texts show important intentions or norms but do not automatically prove uniform implementation.
  • State capacity is multidimensional: taxation, labor mobilization, military force, administration, legitimacy, infrastructure, information, and negotiated local authority may vary independently.
  • Exchange-network connectivity was uneven and could create opportunity, coercion, vulnerability, and differential access.
  • DBQ grouping should emerge from evidence rather than forcing every source into a predetermined thesis.
  • Contradictory or outlier evidence should be analyzed, not hidden.

Research · Evidence · Production Studio

ARSHIF: Assemble eight approved sources from at least five regions across Africa, Asia, Europe, the Americas, and Oceania where appropriate to the selected comparison. Preserve creator, date, place, genre, audience, provenance, and access context.

Evidence & Citation Studio: Create an eight-source DBQ matrix: source → region/time → analytical category → claim → evidence → provenance → value → limitation → corroboration → possible grouping. Require at least one source that complicates or contradicts the emerging thesis.

Atlas Evidence Analysis: Build a thematic global comparison using consistent symbols/categories. Students must state what the map can show, what it cannot prove, and where category choice may hide local complexity.

PROSE: 900–1200 word comparative DBQ answering the essential question. Require evidence from at least five regions, explicit grouping, one counterclaim/alternative thesis, an outlier or contradictory source, and one methodological limitation.

Assignment Guidance

Comparison Matrix: Full credit requires the same analytical question across cases while retaining regional specificity. Similarity and difference must be explained rather than merely listed.

State Capacity Lab: Students should distinguish claims of authority from demonstrated capacity. Encourage comparisons of taxation, labor, military organization, bureaucracy, infrastructure, legitimacy, and negotiated local power.

Atlas/Data Investigation: Require a consistent symbol system, chronological/geographic qualification, and explicit discussion of what the visualization reveals or conceals.

Mini-DBQ: Students should source before grouping. Strong grouping creates analytical relationships among documents rather than sorting them only by continent or source type.

Constructed Response Rubric · 5 points

Require a defensible comparative thesis; at least four specific pieces of evidence from at least four regions and multiple source categories; explanation of at least two analytical patterns; one meaningful difference or exception; and one evidentiary/category limitation or counterclaim.

Correction Protocol

Below 8/10: identify the missed comparative-reasoning concept, reopen the relevant evidence matrix or Atlas comparison, explain why the original reasoning failed, revise using evidence, and retake. Vocabulary copying does not satisfy correction.
