Week 19: Renaissance, Humanism, and Printing

Essential Question: How did renewed study, urban wealth, artistic experimentation, and printing reshape authority and knowledge?

Mastery: 80% minimum. Grade continuity/change, evidence reasoning, access and inequality, patronage, information circulation/control, geographic/quantitative qualification, and argument—not vocabulary recall.

Historical Safeguards

  • Do not present the Renaissance as a sudden rebirth after an intellectually empty “Dark Age.” Require medieval continuity and Mediterranean transmission.
  • Humanism was an educational/intellectual movement with unequal access; do not equate it automatically with modern secularism or universal individualism.
  • Art is evidence of patronage, symbolism, technique, social hierarchy, religious practice, civic identity, and political messaging, but a single artwork does not prove society-wide beliefs.
  • Patronage could enable creativity while shaping subjects, audiences, status, and political meaning.
  • Printing changed reproduction and circulation but did not automatically produce literacy, equality, freedom, or political reform.
  • Printers faced capital costs, censorship, licensing, language markets, literacy constraints, and commercial risk.
  • Do not treat Europe as isolated: preserve Byzantine, Islamic, Jewish, Mediterranean, and medieval European knowledge transmission.
  • Modern digital-media comparisons must preserve differences in speed, scale, infrastructure, regulation, literacy, and access.

Research · Evidence · Production Studio

ARSHIF: Build a source set spanning humanist texts, manuscript/print evidence, artworks, patronage records, city institutions, censorship/licensing, vernacular publication, and knowledge transmission. Preserve creator, date, place, audience, medium, and provenance.

Evidence & Citation Studio: Record claim, source type, provenance, useful detail, mechanism, limitation, confidence, and corroboration. Require at least one visual source, one text, and one material/quantitative/institutional source.

Atlas Evidence Analysis: Map Italian city-states, Mediterranean connections, manuscript/knowledge-transfer nodes, and early printing centers. Distinguish documented dates from approximate diffusion and do not infer literacy or influence from a printing-center marker alone.

PROSE: 800–1100 word argument: To what extent did humanism and printing redistribute authority over knowledge, strengthen existing authority, or do both? Require medieval continuity, multiple source categories, one circulation mechanism, one control mechanism, and one access/inequality limitation.

Assignment Guidance

Continuity/Change: Full credit requires evidence for both inheritance and innovation. “Renaissance means rebirth” is not sufficient analysis.

Visual Evidence: Students must distinguish observation from interpretation and identify corroboration needed for broader social claims.

Printing Model: Treat simplified production estimates as models. Students should state assumptions, units, and what the model cannot establish about readership or influence.

Authority Argument: Accept “weakened,” “strengthened,” or “both” when the student traces mechanisms and qualifies access.

Constructed Response Rubric · 5 points

Require at least three specific pieces of evidence from at least three source categories; one continuity from medieval/Mediterranean knowledge networks; one mechanism by which printing or patronage changed circulation/authority; and one limitation involving access, censorship, literacy, market conditions, or inequality.

Correction Protocol

Below 8/10: identify the missed reasoning concept, reopen the relevant source/Atlas dossier, explain why the original answer failed, correct it with evidence, and transfer the repaired reasoning to a new example before reassessment.
