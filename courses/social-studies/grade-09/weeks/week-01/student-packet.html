Setting the Stage
Social studies begins with questions. Historians, geographers, economists, and civic researchers do not simply collect facts; they decide what evidence is relevant, test explanations, and communicate conclusions. Because the past cannot be rerun, every historical account depends on surviving traces such as letters, laws, artifacts, maps, photographs, oral histories, buildings, and statistical records.
People, Power, and Systems
Sources were created by people with purposes. A tax register records what officials wanted counted; a speech attempts to persuade; a diary may reveal private experience but only one person’s view. Researchers therefore ask who created a source, for whom, under what conditions, and with what possible limits. These questions do not automatically disqualify a source. They help determine what the source can reliably show.
Reading the Evidence
Corroboration is the practice of comparing sources. Two accounts may agree on the sequence of events while disagreeing about motives. An artifact may confirm that a technology existed but not prove how widely it was used. A historian builds a stronger conclusion by combining different kinds of evidence and explaining contradictions rather than hiding them.
Why It Matters
These habits matter beyond history. Citizens face claims in news reports, campaign messages, social media, advertisements, and artificial-intelligence outputs. The same habits—checking origin, context, evidence, and independent confirmation—help people make responsible decisions in the present.

Source A · Clerk’s minutes
Origin and audience: Written at 8:15 p.m., immediately after the meeting; intended as an official record. Clerk at (5,9).
At 7:35 the chair proposed opening the north footbridge at 8 a.m. instead of 6 a.m. for the next seven days while repairs were made. The recorded vote was 12 in favor and 8 against. Several voices called out after the vote. The chair ended the meeting at 8 p.m. No speakers’ reasons were entered.
Check the source’s scope
Records official decisions and counts; its omission of reasons does not establish that nobody gave a reason.
Source B · Mira’s notebook
Origin and audience: Written at 8:30 p.m. by a bakery worker seated at (4,7); a private account.
I heard the chair say the bridge would open at eight for a week because of repairs. A person near the west door called, “Some of us start before eight!” Other voices followed. I could not tell how many joined. I think the delay will make mornings harder for workers like me.
Check the source’s scope
Close to the chair and west door; interpretation about workers is a reasoned perspective, not proof of every worker’s opinion.
Source C · Leon’s letter
Origin and audience: Written the following morning by a repair volunteer seated at (8,2), behind a screen; sent to a neighbor.
I saw hands rise and heard cheering after the vote. I support careful repairs. Everyone seemed pleased that the bridge would be safer. I could not make out every word from the front.
Check the source’s scope
Supports a repair-focused perspective. The claim that everyone was pleased exceeds what this position or testimony can establish.
Source D · Door attendant’s tally
Origin and audience: Made during the meeting by Suri at (1,4) for room-capacity monitoring.
Twenty adults were recorded in the room when the vote was called. Three people stood near the west door afterward. This tally did not identify how they voted or why they stood there.
Check the source’s scope
Counts people and position at a specified time; cannot establish support, motives, or the entire town’s views.
Source E · Illustrator’s floor sketch
Origin and audience: Drawn at 7:30 p.m. by Toma at (2,2), before the vote, to document room layout.
The chair’s table was on the north side. A folding screen stood along x=6 from y=3 to y=7. Witness positions are shown on the supplied map. No vote outcome or conversation is recorded in this sketch.
Check the source’s scope
Useful for layout and possible lines of sight; a pre-vote drawing cannot independently establish what happened after the vote.
Source F · Nell’s community notice
Origin and audience: Written two days later by Nell at (9,7), who attended but used the clerk’s minutes for the vote count; intended to inform residents.
The bridge schedule proposal passed 12–8. The meeting ended at 8 p.m. Some residents raised concerns about early starts. The notice’s count comes from the clerk’s minutes; I did not make a separate count.
Check the source’s scope
Partly dependent on A. Repeating A’s count does not create an independent corroborating count.
