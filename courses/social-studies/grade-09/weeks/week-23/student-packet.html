Weekly Reading

Building an Atlantic System

The Atlantic slave trade developed through interacting commercial demand, shipping networks, political decisions, finance, warfare and capture, colonial law, plantation production, and consumer markets. African societies were diverse and participated in different ways; Atlantic demand changed incentives and intensified capture and human trafficking in many regions. European colonial economies increasingly tied coerced labor to racialized, hereditary legal status.

Numbers and Human Lives

Voyage records help historians reconstruct patterns of embarkation, disembarkation, mortality, and scale, but databases are built from incomplete surviving evidence. Estimates should be treated as estimates. First-person narratives, petitions, advertisements, archaeology, material culture, family histories, and resistance records reveal experiences that aggregate data cannot.

Law, Property, and Racialization

Colonial laws could define enslaved people as property, restrict movement and family rights, and make status hereditary. A law reveals an official rule or claim, not automatic uniform implementation. Historians compare legal texts with court records, plantation records, petitions, local enforcement, and testimony.

Resistance Beyond Revolt

Resistance included survival, family and community building, maintaining cultural practices, work slowdowns, negotiation, escape, maroon communities, petitions, legal challenges, revolt, testimony, and abolitionist organizing. Because archives were often created by enslavers and governments, quieter forms of resistance may be harder to document.

Evidence Safeguards

  • Do not reduce enslaved people to cargo, totals, or passive victims.
  • Do not treat Africa as one political actor or all slavery systems as identical.
  • State the denominator, time span, units, and uncertainty when using quantitative evidence.
  • A law shows official classification or policy; practice requires corroboration.
  • Resistance includes everyday, cultural, legal, migratory, and collective forms.
  • Moral explanation is graded on reasoning, evidence, power, consequences, perspective, and justification—not agreement with a preferred position.

Friday · Economy, Responsibility, Memory & Agency

Synthesize how institutions and choices embedded slavery in Atlantic economies while preserving enslaved people's agency. Complete the mastery assessment and begin the PROSE report.

  1. Which explanation best describes how racial slavery became embedded in Atlantic economies?
    □ A One invention created the system. □ B Commercial demand, finance, shipping, capture, colonial law, plantation labor, racialization, and consumer markets interacted over time. □ C African societies acted as one unified government. □ D Colonial law had no relationship to labor.
  2. What is the strongest way to use a voyage database?
    □ A Treat every estimate as exact. □ B Use it to reconstruct documented/estimated patterns while preserving missing records, uncertainty, and database methodology. □ C Use totals without source notes. □ D Assume every person's experience was identical.
  3. A source reports 120 deaths among 600 embarked people. What should a student do before interpreting the number?
    □ A Ignore the denominator. □ B Calculate the rate, state units/time/context, and examine how the figures were recorded or estimated. □ C Treat 120 as a percentage. □ D Assume the record is complete.
  4. What can a colonial slave law establish most directly?
    □ A Every rule was enforced identically. □ B Official legal classifications, restrictions, and power claims; implementation and lived experience require other evidence. □ C Every enslaved person accepted the law. □ D Private belief.
  5. Which explanation of racialization is historically strongest?
    □ A Racial hierarchy was biologically fixed and timeless. □ B Law, institutions, economic interests, ideology, and social practice assigned hierarchical meaning to perceived differences and reproduced it over time. □ C Race had no connection to colonial institutions. □ D One pamphlet created racial slavery.
  6. Which approach best analyzes resistance?
    □ A Count only armed revolts. □ B Examine survival, family/community, cultural continuity, work slowdowns, negotiation, escape, maroon communities, petitions, courts, revolt, testimony, and organizing while noting archival visibility. □ C Treat compliance in a record as proof of consent. □ D Assume resistance always succeeded.
  7. How should a first-person narrative of enslavement be used?
    □ A As a perfectly complete account of all enslaved people. □ B Source its creation, audience, publication context, memory, and purpose while treating it as indispensable evidence of experience and interpretation. □ C Reject it because it has a point of view. □ D Use it without corroboration.
  8. What can an Atlantic route map establish most safely?
    □ A The exact experience of every person transported. □ B Spatial and chronological patterns represented by the evidence; individual routes, motives, local conditions, and causation require additional evidence. □ C That all routes operated at the same volume. □ D That every mapped relationship was voluntary.
  9. Which economic analysis is strongest?
    □ A Plantation profits explain the system by themselves. □ B Connect production, coerced labor, finance, insurance/shipping, law, consumption, trade, and distribution of gains and costs while distinguishing evidence from inference. □ C Ignore consumers. □ D Treat enslaved labor as a neutral input.
  10. Which approach best evaluates historical responsibility?
    □ A Treat all actors as having equal power and choices. □ B Distinguish actions, institutions, coercion, available choices, benefits, harms, consequences, and evidence while preserving historical context. □ C Judge only stated intentions. □ D Avoid ethical reasoning entirely.
