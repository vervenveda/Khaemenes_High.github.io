Week 11: African Kingdoms and Trading Societies

Essential Question: How did African states and communities build power and participate in regional and long-distance networks, and how does surviving evidence shape the histories we tell?

Mastery: At least 80% (8/10 objective questions). Tool use alone never earns mastery; source handling and historical reasoning must be evaluated.

Historical & Evidence Safeguards

  • Teach African histories regionally and comparatively; never treat the continent as one political, ecological, or cultural experience.
  • Geography creates opportunities and constraints but does not mechanically determine political outcomes.
  • Trade is one component of state power alongside agriculture, institutions, taxation/tribute, military capacity, diplomacy, urban systems, and local production.
  • Do not infer foreign political control from imported objects alone.
  • Treat Swahili societies as locally rooted African coastal communities participating in wider Indian Ocean networks, not merely foreign colonies.
  • Famous accounts of Mansa Musa or travelers can illuminate elite politics and exchange but cannot stand in for ordinary life.
  • Do not automatically privilege written evidence over oral tradition, archaeology, material culture, spatial evidence, or linguistic evidence. Evaluate each according to creation, transmission, context, preservation, and corroboration.
  • Approach oral traditions as historically situated knowledge and performance. Avoid extracting culturally held narratives as context-free data; use approved/public materials and preserve attribution and provenance.

Research · Evidence · Production Studio

ARSHIF: Use approved African-history, oral-tradition/storywork, travel, manuscript, archaeology, and Indian Ocean corridors. Students preserve provenance and community/historical context. Archive placement is not itself proof.

Evidence & Citation Studio: Require at least three source categories, ideally including written/traveler evidence, oral or locally rooted tradition, and archaeological/material evidence; spatial, manuscript, or linguistic evidence may strengthen the dossier. Record origin, transmission/context, claim relationship, limitation, missing voices, and citation.

Atlas Evidence Analysis: Map relevant Sahara/Sahel, Niger, Red Sea, southern African, or Swahili/Indian Ocean networks. Students must label inferred links and state what mapped routes cannot prove about political control, frequency, volume, direction, or causation.

PROSE Editorial Suite: Recommended honors report: 700–1000 words answering “How does the evidence that survives shape the history we tell about African societies?” Require a defensible thesis, multiple African settings or processes, at least three source categories, corroboration, a missing voice or survival bias, revision, and conclusion.

Assignment Guidance

1 · Sahelian Power Matrix: Reject monocausal “gold created Mali” explanations. Strong work explains interactions among production, political institutions, taxation/tribute, military capacity, diplomacy, urban centers, religion/scholarship where relevant, and trade.

2 · Mansa Musa / Traveler Evidence Dossier: Require sourcing before judging reliability. Students identify what spectacular elite evidence can establish, what it cannot establish, and which voices are absent.

3 · African Network Atlas: Compare at least one inland and one coastal or Red Sea/Indian Ocean case. Full credit requires local agency, source basis for connections, and map limitations.

4 · Corroboration & PROSE Report: Score how well students combine evidence categories without ranking them automatically. A tension among sources is not a failure; students should explain plausible reasons and uncertainty.

Constructed Response Rubric

Prompt: Using at least three specific pieces of evidence from at least three source categories, explain how African states and communities built power and participated in regional or long-distance networks. Then explain one way surviving evidence limits or shapes interpretation.

5 points: defensible multicausal claim; accurate evidence across multiple processes/settings; three source categories; explanation/corroboration rather than listing; meaningful limitation, survival bias, or missing voice.

Correction Protocol

Below 8/10: identify the missed reasoning concept, reopen the relevant dossier/map/source category, explain why the original answer failed, correct it using evidence, and retake. Vocabulary memorization alone is insufficient.
