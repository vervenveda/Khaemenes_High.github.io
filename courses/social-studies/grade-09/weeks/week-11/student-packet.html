Weekly Reading

Many Africas

African societies developed across deserts, Sahel, savannas, forests, highlands, river systems, plateaus, and coasts. Environments shaped opportunities and constraints, but they did not mechanically determine political outcomes. Historians compare specific regions and periods rather than treating Africa as one historical experience.

Sahelian states and trans-Saharan networks

Ghana, Mali, Songhai, and other West African states developed through changing combinations of agricultural production, political authority, military capacity, taxation or tribute, diplomacy, urban centers, and participation in exchange. Gold, salt, textiles, books, and enslaved people moved through networks involving producers, merchants, intermediaries, communities, and states. Trade mattered, but trade alone did not create state power.

Spectacular sources and missing voices

Accounts associated with Mansa Musa's pilgrimage, traveler descriptions such as Ibn Battuta's, manuscripts, architecture, oral traditions, and material evidence reveal different aspects of West African societies. Famous rulers and unusual events can dominate surviving narratives, while farmers, craftspeople, women, enslaved people, merchants, and other communities may be less visible.

Eastern and southern African worlds

Aksum participated in Red Sea networks. Great Zimbabwe developed through local agriculture, cattle, political organization, craft production, and connections to gold exchange. Swahili coastal cities grew from African communities engaged in Indian Ocean commerce. Imported objects demonstrate connection when their context is secure, but they do not by themselves prove foreign political control.

How historians reconstruct the past

Written accounts, oral traditions, archaeology, material culture, manuscripts, spatial evidence, and—where appropriate—linguistic evidence have different creation and survival histories. Historians ask what each source can establish, what it cannot establish, whose voices it preserves, and how independent evidence corroborates or complicates a claim.

Evidence Safeguards

  • Do not treat Africa as one society, environment, or political system.
  • Do not explain state power through gold or trade alone.
  • Do not assume a mapped trade route proves political control of every segment.
  • Do not infer foreign rule merely because imported objects are present.
  • Treat Swahili societies as locally rooted African communities participating in wider ocean networks.
  • Do not automatically rank written sources above oral tradition or archaeology.
  • Preserve provenance and community/historical context when using oral or locally rooted historical knowledge.

Daily Investigations

Monday · Many Africas

Map major ecological and regional zones. Separate environmental observations from historical explanations. Write three claims showing how geography can influence without determining history.

Tuesday · Sahelian States and Trans-Saharan Networks

Trace one commodity through source region, intermediaries, and market nodes. Build a multicausal state-power matrix including production, political institutions, taxation/tribute, military capacity, diplomacy, urban centers, and trade.

Wednesday · Mansa Musa and Spectacular Evidence

Compare an outside written account, locally rooted evidence where available, and archaeological/material evidence. Identify provenance, perspective, useful claims, limitations, corroboration needs, and missing voices.

Thursday · Aksum, Great Zimbabwe, and Swahili Ocean Worlds

Compare one inland and one coastal/Red Sea network. Distinguish local production and institutions from imported materials and long-distance exchange. Mark every inferred connection.

Friday · Reconstructing African Histories

Build a corroboration matrix using written/traveler, oral, archaeological/material, spatial, and other appropriate evidence. Identify agreement, tension, silence, and uncertainty before completing the mastery assessment.

Week 11 Mastery Quiz

Mastery threshold: 80% (8 of 10 objective questions).

1. Which approach best avoids treating African history as a single experience?
□ A. Explain all African societies through one climate zone.
□ B. Compare specific regions, periods, environments, institutions, and evidence while recognizing connections among them.
□ C. Use only states that left large written archives.
□ D. Assume trade produced the same political system everywhere.

2. Which explanation of Sahelian state power is strongest?
□ A. Gold alone automatically created powerful states.
□ B. States gained power only by controlling religion.
□ C. Agriculture, political authority, taxation or tribute, military capacity, diplomacy, urban centers, and participation in trade could interact in different combinations.
□ D. Caravan merchants directly ruled every Sahelian state.

3. What is the strongest way to use a famous account of Mansa Musa's pilgrimage?
□ A. Treat every memorable detail as exact because the story is famous.
□ B. Use it as evidence with provenance and audience, then corroborate claims with independent written, material, spatial, or locally rooted evidence.
□ C. Reject it because elite rulers are never useful historical subjects.
□ D. Assume it describes ordinary life throughout Mali.

4. What can an imported ceramic found in secure archaeological context at an African trading settlement establish most directly?
□ A. That a foreign empire politically controlled the settlement
□ B. That the object or related exchange connected the site to a wider material network, while the mechanism and political meaning require additional evidence
□ C. That every resident used imported ceramics
□ D. That local production had ended

5. Which statement best characterizes Swahili coastal history?
□ A. Swahili cities were simply foreign colonies with no African foundation.
□ B. Indian Ocean exchange prevented local cultural development.
□ C. African coastal communities developed locally while participating in wider Indian Ocean commercial, religious, linguistic, and cultural networks.
□ D. All Swahili cities had identical political institutions.

6. Why should written evidence not automatically outrank oral tradition?
□ A. Written sources have no bias.
□ B. Each source type has distinct creation, transmission, context, strengths, and limitations; claims should be evaluated and corroborated rather than ranked by medium alone.
□ C. Oral traditions never change.
□ D. Oral evidence can answer every historical question by itself.

7. Why is archaeological context important?
□ A. It proves an artifact's exact owner.
□ B. The location, layer, association, and relationship of finds help establish historical meaning beyond the object alone.
□ C. It makes written evidence unnecessary.
□ D. It guarantees an artifact was locally produced.

8. Which map interpretation is strongest?
□ A. A caravan route line proves one state controlled every segment.
□ B. A trade-route map can show spatial relationships and possible corridors, but political control, volume, frequency, and direction require additional evidence.
□ C. All mapped routes operated continuously for centuries.
□ D. The largest symbol always represents the most important society.

9. A traveler describes a custom as strange or remarkable. What should a historian do first?
□ A. Treat the judgment as a neutral description.
□ B. Discard the entire source.
□ C. Analyze the traveler's background, audience, expectations, context, and wording, then compare the observation with independent evidence.
□ D. Assume everyone in the region followed the custom.

10. Which research design best addresses the question “How does surviving evidence shape African history?”
□ A. Use only the longest written source.
□ B. Compare written/traveler, oral, archaeological/material, and spatial evidence; identify agreement, tension, missing voices, survival bias, and uncertainty.
□ C. Count artifacts without recording archaeological context.
□ D. Choose whichever source confirms the initial thesis.

Constructed Response

Using at least three specific pieces of evidence from at least three source categories, explain how African states and communities built power and participated in regional or long-distance networks. Then explain one way the surviving evidence limits or shapes our interpretation.
