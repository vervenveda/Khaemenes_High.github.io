Week 14: Indigenous Americas and Oceania

Essential Question: How did Indigenous societies develop complex systems of governance, knowledge, environmental adaptation, recordkeeping, and navigation, and how does surviving evidence shape what outsiders think they know?

Mastery: 80% minimum. Evaluate historical reasoning, source handling, ethical research, and qualification. Tool use or vocabulary recall alone does not demonstrate mastery.

Historical & Ethical Safeguards

  • Do not treat Indigenous peoples of the Americas or Oceania as one culture or one developmental path.
  • Do not use resemblance to Europe, monumental stone architecture, alphabetic writing, or centralized empire as universal measures of complexity.
  • Absence of alphabetic writing is not absence of history, recordkeeping, science, literature, law, memory, or complex knowledge.
  • Do not infer total political control from tribute, roads, settlement hierarchy, or mapped connections alone.
  • Environmental adaptation reflects active knowledge, experimentation, and organization; avoid environmental determinism.
  • Colonial written sources can be useful but must be contextualized and corroborated with Indigenous/locally rooted, archaeological, material, spatial, environmental, and other appropriate evidence.
  • Living Indigenous knowledge is not automatically open-access research material. Use appropriate public/authorized sources, preserve attribution and context, respect restrictions and community authority, and distinguish outsider interpretation from community voice.

Research · Evidence · Production Studio

ARSHIF: Locate approved Indigenous-history, archaeology, material-culture, oral-history/storywork, environmental-knowledge, recordkeeping, and Pacific navigation materials. Students record creator/community, date, place, provenance, and access context. Archive placement is not itself validation.

Evidence & Citation Studio: Require at least three source categories. Where appropriate and publicly authorized, include an Indigenous or locally rooted source, archaeological/material evidence, and an outsider/written source. Record provenance, context, useful claim, limitation, missing voices, and corroboration.

Atlas Evidence Analysis: Use the verified Protools/Atlas_Evidence_Analysis.html. Students may organize settlements, agricultural landscapes, roads, exchange, voyaging, chronology, claims, evidence status, provenance, and alternative interpretations. A mapped connection does not prove political control, direction, frequency, volume, or causation.

PROSE Editorial Suite: 700–1000 word honors report: How do the kinds of evidence that survive shape what outsiders think they know about Indigenous societies? Require multiple source categories and discussion of destruction/survival bias, colonial framing, or a missing voice.

Assignment Guidance

1 · Comparative Knowledge Systems Lab: Students compare specific societies rather than ranking them. Full credit requires locally specific evidence and recognition that analytical categories may fit unevenly.

2 · Atlas & Environmental Engineering Case: Require observed feature → knowledge required → coordination → benefit → limitation. Students must label inference and avoid “environment caused society” explanations.

3 · Recordkeeping & Corroboration Dossier: Compare Maya writing/calendrical evidence, quipu or another Andean evidence form, archaeological/material evidence, and/or appropriate oral/locally rooted knowledge. Do not force every recording system into the category of alphabetic text.

4 · Wayfinding / Colonial-Source Case: Treat Pacific navigation as accumulated observational and transmitted knowledge. Outsider descriptions must be sourced and corroborated; community-held knowledge restrictions must be respected.

Constructed Response Rubric · 5 points

Prompt: Using at least three specific pieces of evidence from at least three source categories, explain how Indigenous societies in the Americas and/or Oceania developed complex systems of governance, knowledge, environmental adaptation, recordkeeping, or navigation. Then explain one limitation created by the evidence that survives.

5 points: specific defensible claim; accurate evidence; at least three source categories; explanation rather than listing; avoids collapsing distinct societies; meaningful evidentiary limitation, colonial-source problem, survival/destruction bias, or missing voice.

Correction Protocol

Below 8/10: identify the missed reasoning concept, reopen the relevant evidence/Atlas case, explain why the original answer failed, correct it using evidence, and retake. Vocabulary copying alone is insufficient.
