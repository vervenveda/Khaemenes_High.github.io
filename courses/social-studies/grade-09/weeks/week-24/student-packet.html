Weekly Reading

Setting the Stage

Seventeenth- and eighteenth-century thinkers reconsidered political authority amid religious conflict, scientific change, commercial expansion, and stronger states. Hobbes emphasized security and sovereign power; Locke defended natural rights and government by consent; Montesquieu examined divided power; Rousseau explored popular sovereignty.

People, Power, and Systems

Women writers, abolitionists, religious dissenters, and thinkers outside Europe challenged the limits of supposedly universal claims. Mary Wollstonecraft argued that denying education to women contradicted rational equality. Enslaved and colonized people used rights language to expose hypocrisy.

Reading the Evidence

Political texts must be read as arguments, not timeless rulebooks. Students identify assumptions, intended audiences, historical problems, and terms whose meanings changed over time.

Why It Matters

Modern constitutional systems still debate liberty, equality, security, property, representation, and the limits of majority rule. Studying competing theories helps students recognize that democratic design involves tradeoffs.

Source Study: Political Philosophers in Conversation

Compare selections from Hobbes, Locke, Montesquieu, Rousseau, Wollstonecraft, and an abolitionist voice. Identify claims about human nature and authority.

Vocabulary

natural rightsRights understood as belonging to people by nature rather than granted solely by government.
social contractA theory that political authority arises from an agreement among people or between people and rulers.
separation of powersDivision of government authority among institutions to reduce concentration of power.
popular sovereigntyThe principle that legitimate political power originates with the people.
salonA gathering for intellectual and social discussion, often hosted by elite women in early modern Europe.
public sphereSpaces and networks in which people debate matters of common concern.

Key Understandings

  • Enlightenment thinkers debated sovereignty, rights, religious toleration, law, economy, and human nature rather than sharing one program.
  • Print culture, salons, coffeehouses, scientific societies, and colonial encounters expanded political debate.
  • Claims of universal rights often coexisted with slavery, empire, patriarchy, and exclusions that critics exposed.
