Weekly Reading

Setting the Stage

United States history begins before the United States existed. In 1491, North America contained hundreds of Indigenous nations with distinct languages, governments, trade networks, agricultural systems, and relationships to land. The continent was neither empty nor culturally uniform. Beginning the course here changes the questions historians ask about encounter, sovereignty, migration, and the meaning of American history.

People, Power, and Systems

Historical narratives reflect choices about where to begin, whose evidence survives, and which developments receive emphasis. Oral traditions, archaeology, ecological evidence, maps, diplomatic records, and later written accounts can illuminate different dimensions of the past. No single source type is sufficient by itself, and gaps in evidence should be acknowledged rather than filled with certainty.

Reading the Evidence

Students compare an archaeological interpretation, an Indigenous oral history excerpt, and an early European map. They identify what each source can establish, what it cannot establish, and how the creator’s purpose and knowledge shaped the record. Corroboration does not require sources to say identical things; it asks whether they independently support, complicate, or contradict a claim.

Why It Matters

Beginning with evidence and Indigenous sovereignty prevents the course from treating colonization as an automatic or uncontested process. It also establishes habits used throughout the year: sourcing, contextualization, comparison, causation, continuity and change, and argumentation.

Source Study: Three Ways of Knowing 1491

Compare an Indigenous oral tradition, an archaeological site interpretation, and a sixteenth-century European map. Evaluate the evidence each preserves and the limits of each form.

Vocabulary and Key Understandings

historiographyThe study of how history is researched, interpreted, and written.
periodizationDividing history into named eras to make change over time easier to study.
primary sourceEvidence created by people who lived during the period under study.
corroborationTesting a source by comparing it with independent evidence.
historical contextThe conditions and ideas surrounding an event or source.
national narrativeA broad account used to explain a nation’s origins, identity, and development.
  • Every national history is shaped by evidence selection and periodization.
  • Indigenous societies had complex political, economic, and cultural systems before European colonization.
  • Responsible history distinguishes evidence-based interpretation from unsupported memory or myth.

Daily Lessons

Monday · Frame the Inquiry

Objective: Place History, Memory, and the American Story in chronological and geographic context and construct an initial response to the essential question.

Warm-up: Write three observations or questions suggested by the title “History, Memory, and the American Story.” Then identify one fact you believe you know and one uncertainty.

Lesson: United States history begins before the United States existed. In 1491, North America contained hundreds of Indigenous nations with distinct languages, governments, trade networks, agricultural systems, and relationships to land. The continent was neither empty nor culturally uniform. Beginning the course here changes the questions historians ask about encounter, sovereignty, migration, and the meaning of American history.

Guided practice: Build a class chronology for 1491–1607 and locate the focal region(s): North America and Atlantic World. Label turning points, continuities, and unresolved questions.

Independent practice: Complete the weekly inquiry launch: initial claim, two supporting questions, a labeled time-space frame, and one source needed.

Exit check: Explain how chronology or geography prevents one misleading interpretation of this week’s topic.

Tuesday · Source, Contextualize, and Corroborate

Objective: Evaluate primary and secondary sources for origin, purpose, context, evidence, and limitation.

Warm-up: Preview the source study, “Three Ways of Knowing 1491.” Predict two creator purposes and identify evidence needed to test the prediction.

Lesson: Historical narratives reflect choices about where to begin, whose evidence survives, and which developments receive emphasis. Oral traditions, archaeology, ecological evidence, maps, diplomatic records, and later written accounts can illuminate different dimensions of the past. No single source type is sufficient by itself, and gaps in evidence should be acknowledged rather than filled with certainty.

Guided practice: Compare an Indigenous oral tradition, an archaeological site interpretation, and a sixteenth-century European map. Evaluate the evidence each preserves and the limits of each form.

Independent practice: Complete Assignment 1 using OPVL or HIPP analysis, then corroborate the source with at least one independent item.

Exit check: State one claim the source can support and one claim it cannot establish alone.

Wednesday · Map, Measure, and Model

Objective: Interpret geographic and quantitative evidence while distinguishing pattern, correlation, and causation.

Warm-up: Study the week’s map or data without reading its title. List two visible patterns, one anomaly, and one question.

Lesson: Students compare an archaeological interpretation, an Indigenous oral history excerpt, and an early European map. They identify what each source can establish, what it cannot establish, and how the creator’s purpose and knowledge shaped the record. Corroboration does not require sources to say identical things; it asks whether they independently support, complicate, or contradict a claim.

Guided practice: Map major Indigenous cultural regions, selected nations, trade routes, and ecological zones without treating cultural boundaries as fixed state borders.

Independent practice: Compare estimated population ranges from several scholarly reconstructions. Explain why estimates differ and what evidence would narrow uncertainty.

Exit check: Describe one pattern, one plausible explanation, and one important limitation of the evidence.

Thursday · Deliberate and Write

Objective: Develop an evidence-based historical argument that addresses a competing interpretation.

Warm-up: Choose an initial position on: Should a United States history course begin in 1491, 1607, 1776, or another date? Defend a choice using historical reasoning.

Lesson: Beginning with evidence and Indigenous sovereignty prevents the course from treating colonization as an automatic or uncontested process. It also establishes habits used throughout the year: sourcing, contextualization, comparison, causation, continuity and change, and argumentation.

Guided practice: Use a claim-evidence-reasoning organizer requiring evidence from the weekly reading, source study, and map/data investigation.

Independent practice: Complete Assignment 3 as a seminar contribution, policy-style brief, or formal historical argument with a counterclaim.

Exit check: Revise the claim after hearing a counterargument and identify what evidence caused or resisted revision.

Friday · Synthesize, Assess, and Correct

Objective: Synthesize content and disciplinary skills, complete the weekly assessment, and plan a targeted correction.

Warm-up: Retrieve six key terms and three major understandings without notes; then correct in another color.

Lesson: Review History, Memory, and the American Story through chronology, causation, continuity and change, comparison, geography, institutions, economy, culture, and evidence.

Guided practice: Complete a four-part synthesis: one continuity, one change, one cause, and one consequence.

Independent practice: Complete the weekly quiz and constructed response. Use the correction form to explain each missed item and cite the correct evidence.

Exit check: Answer the essential question in two precise sentences: How should historians construct a national history when evidence, memory, and identity do not always agree?

Assignments

Assignment 1 · Source Lab — Three Ways of Knowing 1491

Compare an Indigenous oral tradition, an archaeological site interpretation, and a sixteenth-century European map. Evaluate the evidence each preserves and the limits of each form. Complete an origin-purpose-value-limitation analysis and write a 200–300 word evidence-based interpretation.

Deliverables: Completed source-analysis chart; One analytical response; One corroboration question

Assignment 2 · Atlas and Data Investigation

Map major Indigenous cultural regions, selected nations, trade routes, and ecological zones without treating cultural boundaries as fixed state borders. Compare estimated population ranges from several scholarly reconstructions. Explain why estimates differ and what evidence would narrow uncertainty. Submit a labeled product and a 180–250 word interpretation that distinguishes observation, explanation, and limitation.

Deliverables: Labeled map, graph, table, or model; Interpretive response; Source and units note

Assignment 3 · Evidence-Based Seminar or Argument

Respond to: Should a United States history course begin in 1491, 1607, 1776, or another date? Defend a choice using historical reasoning. Use at least three pieces of evidence from two source types, include a serious counterclaim or qualification, and explain broader significance.

Deliverables: Defensible thesis; Three cited pieces of evidence; Counterclaim and response; Conclusion
