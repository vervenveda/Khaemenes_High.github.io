Weekly Reading

Setting the Stage

By 1860, nearly four million people were enslaved in the United States. Enslaved labor generated enormous wealth, especially through cotton, while laws denied family security, mobility, education, and legal personhood. Enslaved people resisted through work slowdowns, cultural preservation, escape, legal petitions, rebellion, and the daily defense of family and community.

People, Power, and Systems

Abolitionists included free Black organizers, formerly enslaved speakers, religious activists, women’s-rights advocates, political abolitionists, and allies abroad. They disagreed about tactics but helped transform slavery from a subject often avoided in national politics into an unavoidable moral and constitutional crisis.

Reading the Evidence

Students analyze a formerly enslaved person’s narrative, a proslavery argument, an abolitionist newspaper, and a map of congressional compromises. They evaluate how firsthand testimony, political rhetoric, and legal arrangements answer different questions.

Why It Matters

Compromises over Missouri, western territories, fugitive slaves, and Kansas delayed disunion but did not reconcile incompatible visions of labor, rights, and federal power. The Dred Scott decision and the election of 1860 further narrowed political space for settlement.

Source Study: Freedom Claims and Proslavery Claims

Compare a slave narrative, a proslavery defense, an abolitionist speech, and the Dred Scott opinion. Identify claims about law, labor, race, and citizenship.

Vocabulary and Key Understandings

abolitionismThe movement to end slavery.
sectionalismStrong regional loyalty that can override national unity.
slave powerAn abolitionist term for the political influence of slaveholders.
popular sovereigntyAllowing territorial voters to decide an issue such as slavery.
fugitive slave lawFederal law requiring the return of people claimed as escaped slaves.
secessionFormal withdrawal of a state from the Union.
  • Slavery was an economic, legal, political, and human system sustained by violence and resistance.
  • Abolitionists used moral argument, political action, self-emancipation, print, and organized networks.
  • Territorial expansion repeatedly reopened the question of slavery’s future and exposed limits of compromise.
