Weekly Reading

Setting the Stage

The Constitution establishes institutions and powers but does not mechanically resolve every modern dispute. Amendments, statutes, executive practices, and court decisions have changed how its principles apply to states, individuals, businesses, and new technology.

People, Power, and Systems

Judicial review allows courts to decide constitutional cases, but courts operate within jurisdictional limits and depend on other institutions for implementation. Precedent promotes stability while legal reasoning permits distinction or overruling.

Reading the Evidence

Students compare the reasoning—not merely outcomes—of landmark cases involving speech, criminal procedure, equal protection, privacy, religious liberty, and federalism. They identify facts, constitutional question, holding, rationale, and dissent.

Why It Matters

Florida’s constitution, courts, open-records provisions, local governments, and ballot-amendment process provide a state-level comparison. Civic literacy requires knowing which institution has authority and which remedy is legally available.

Source Study: Reading a Supreme Court Case

Brief selected majority and dissenting opinions using facts, issue, rule, reasoning, holding, and significance.

Vocabulary and Key Understandings

incorporationApplication of Bill of Rights protections to states through the Fourteenth Amendment.
precedentA prior judicial decision used to guide later cases.
strict scrutinyA demanding judicial test applied to certain classifications or fundamental rights.
standingA legal requirement that a party show a sufficient stake in a dispute.
federal preemptionDisplacement of state law by valid federal law.
civil libertyA freedom protected against improper government interference.
  • Constitutional change occurs through text, amendment, precedent, statute, practice, and political development.
  • Courts depend on cases, standing, evidence, enforcement, and public institutions rather than acting as abstract councils.
  • Federalism can protect diversity and experimentation while also creating unequal rights or services across states.

Wednesday · Map, Measure, and Model

Objective: Interpret geographic and quantitative evidence while distinguishing pattern, correlation, and causation.

Warm-up: Study the week’s map or data without reading its title. List two visible patterns, one anomaly, and one question.

Lesson: Students compare the reasoning—not merely outcomes—of landmark cases involving speech, criminal procedure, equal protection, privacy, religious liberty, and federalism. They identify facts, constitutional question, holding, rationale, and dissent.
