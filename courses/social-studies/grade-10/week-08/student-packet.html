Weekly Reading

Setting the Stage

Eleven slaveholding states seceded after Abraham Lincoln’s election, and Confederate leaders defended slavery and state sovereignty as they formed a new government. The Union initially fought to preserve the nation. Enslaved people who reached Union lines forced commanders and lawmakers to confront the relationship between slavery and Confederate power.

People, Power, and Systems

The Emancipation Proclamation changed the war’s legal and diplomatic meaning, authorized Black military service, and linked Union victory to slavery’s destruction in rebelling areas. Nearly 180,000 Black men served in the Union Army, while women sustained households, worked in hospitals and government, and participated in aid and intelligence networks.

Reading the Evidence

Students compare secession declarations, Lincoln’s first inaugural, the Emancipation Proclamation, a Black soldier’s letter, and casualty data. They track change in policy over time rather than reading later outcomes backward into 1861.

Why It Matters

The Union victory preserved the United States and the Thirteenth Amendment abolished slavery. Yet wartime destruction, mass death, contested civil liberties, and the unfinished status of freedpeople made Reconstruction a decisive test of what freedom would mean.

Source Study: From Union to Emancipation

Trace change across secession documents, presidential messages, emancipation policy, and Black military testimony.

Vocabulary and Key Understandings

emancipation proclamationThe 1863 executive order declaring freedom for enslaved people in areas in rebellion.
contraband policyUnion treatment of escaped enslaved people as seized enemy property before broader emancipation policy.
total warMobilization aimed at an opponent’s military capacity, economy, infrastructure, and public will.
habeas corpusProtection against unlawful detention.
conscriptionCompulsory military service.
Thirteenth AmendmentThe constitutional amendment abolishing slavery except as punishment for crime.
  • Secession declarations centered slavery and the perceived threat to slaveholding power.
  • Military necessity, enslaved people’s actions, abolitionist pressure, and policy choices moved the Union toward emancipation.
  • The war expanded federal capacity and ended legal slavery while leaving citizenship and equality unresolved.
