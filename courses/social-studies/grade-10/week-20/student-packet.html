Weekly Reading

Setting the Stage

The United States became a central producer of ships, aircraft, vehicles, food, and weapons. Women entered war industries, Black migration accelerated, Native code talkers served, Mexican labor expanded through the Bracero Program, and servicemembers from many communities fought in segregated and integrated contexts.

People, Power, and Systems

The Holocaust developed from exclusion and persecution to ghettoization, mass shooting, deportation, forced labor, and industrialized murder. Jews were the principal targets of Nazi genocide, while Roma, disabled people, Soviet prisoners, Poles, political opponents, gay men, and others were also persecuted or killed.

Reading the Evidence

Students analyze wartime production data, a Holocaust survivor account, government propaganda, a Double V editorial, and evidence concerning strategic bombing and atomic decisions. They distinguish explanation from justification.

Why It Matters

World War II destroyed fascist regimes in Europe and militarist rule in Japan, accelerated decolonization, and left the United States and Soviet Union as superpowers. The Nuremberg trials and new international institutions attempted to establish accountability for crimes against humanity.

Source Study: War, Rights, and Responsibility

Compare survivor testimony, wartime propaganda, a Double V editorial, military planning evidence, and postwar legal principles.

Vocabulary and Key Understandings

genocideThe intentional destruction, in whole or in part, of a national, ethnic, racial, or religious group.
HolocaustThe systematic persecution and murder of six million Jews and millions of other victims by Nazi Germany and collaborators.
rationingGovernment allocation of scarce goods.
double V campaignA Black American campaign for victory against fascism abroad and racism at home.
D-DayThe Allied invasion of Normandy on June 6, 1944.
atomic bombA nuclear weapon based on energy released through atomic fission.
  • U.S. industrial mobilization contributed decisively to Allied capacity while transforming work and migration.
  • The Holocaust resulted from antisemitic ideology, dictatorship, war, bureaucracy, collaboration, and escalating policy.
  • Victory ended Axis regimes but raised lasting moral questions about bombing, displacement, justice, and nuclear weapons.

Exit check: Explain how chronology or geography prevents one misleading interpretation of this week’s topic.

Tuesday · Source, Contextualize, and Corroborate

Objective: Evaluate primary and secondary sources for origin, purpose, context, evidence, and limitation.

Warm-up: Preview the source study, “War, Rights, and Responsibility.” Predict two creator purposes and identify evidence needed to test the prediction.

Independent practice: Complete Assignment 1 using OPVL or HIPP analysis, then corroborate the source with at least one independent item.

Exit check: State one claim the source can support and one claim it cannot establish alone.

Wednesday · Map, Measure, and Model

Objective: Interpret geographic and quantitative evidence while distinguishing pattern, correlation, and causation.

Warm-up: Study the week’s map or data without reading its title. List two visible patterns, one anomaly, and one question.

Lesson: Students analyze wartime production data, a Holocaust survivor account, government propaganda, a Double V editorial, and evidence concerning strategic bombing and atomic decisions. They distinguish explanation from justification.

Thursday · Deliberate and Write

Objective: Develop an evidence-based historical argument that addresses a competing interpretation.

Warm-up: Choose an initial position on: How should historians assess the atomic-bomb decision using evidence available in 1945 rather than hindsight alone?

Exit check: Revise the claim after hearing a counterargument and identify what evidence caused or resisted revision.

Friday · Synthesize, Assess, and Correct

Objective: Synthesize content and disciplinary skills, complete the weekly assessment, and plan a targeted correction.

Warm-up: Retrieve six key terms and three major understandings without notes; then correct in another color.
