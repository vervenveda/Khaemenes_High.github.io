Week 5 · Democracy, Markets, and Reform

Evidence resource: Library of Congress · American Memory / Primary Source Sets

Source type: Curated historical sources and teaching interpretation

Use maps, printed accounts, and historical images as evidence of economic change. This source set is a general comparative resource; distinguish late-nineteenth-century industrialization from the earlier Market Revolution.

The instructional context and prompts are available offline. Accessing the original archive requires a connection.

Analyze and write

  1. Compare a transportation map with an account of working conditions. What can each establish, and what remains uncertain?
  2. How might expanding economic opportunity have differed for enslaved workers, wage earners, and landowners?
  3. State a historical claim using two identified forms of evidence.
Week 6 · Expansion, Removal, and the Mexican-American War

Evidence resource: National Archives · Indian Removal Act (1830)

Source type: Primary-source law with archival historical context

The law authorized federal removal policy; it does not by itself establish consent from the Native nations affected. Compare statutory power with community testimony and treaty obligations.

The instructional context and prompts are available offline. Accessing the original archive requires a connection.

Analyze and write

  1. Who is granted authority in the statute, and whose voice is missing?
  2. How could a government's legal authority differ from the legitimacy of a policy for those displaced?
  3. What other primary source would help test claims that removal was voluntary?
Week 7 · Slavery, Abolition, and Sectional Crisis

Evidence resource: Library of Congress · Born in Slavery collection

Source type: 1930s oral-history accounts recalling slavery decades earlier

These accounts are valuable testimony, but their recording dates, interviewer relationships, memory, and the era of Jim Crow must be considered when interpreting them.

The instructional context and prompts are available offline. Accessing the original archive requires a connection.

Analyze and write

  1. Why is an interview recorded in the 1930s not a contemporaneous written source from 1850?
  2. How would you corroborate an individual testimony without dismissing the speaker's experience?
  3. What can personal testimony reveal that a census table cannot?
Week 8 · Civil War, Emancipation, and Union

Evidence resource: National Archives · Emancipation Proclamation (1863)

Source type: Primary-source executive proclamation with contextual notes

The Emancipation Proclamation was a wartime measure with defined geographic scope. It was not identical to the Thirteenth Amendment, which later established a national constitutional prohibition of slavery except as punishment for crime.

The instructional context and prompts are available offline. Accessing the original archive requires a connection.

Analyze and write

  1. Identify the wartime scope of the proclamation. Which areas were not covered by its operative terms?
  2. Distinguish wartime emancipation from constitutional abolition in 1865.
  3. How might military policy and enslaved people's actions have interacted in shaping emancipation?
