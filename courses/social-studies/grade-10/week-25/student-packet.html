Weekly Reading

Setting the Stage

President Lyndon Johnson used a large congressional majority to enact Medicare, Medicaid, education aid, antipoverty programs, environmental protections, consumer safeguards, and civil-rights laws. Many programs produced lasting benefits, though implementation and results varied.

People, Power, and Systems

The Immigration and Nationality Act ended the old national-origin quota structure. Family reunification and employment categories contributed over time to increased immigration from Asia, Latin America, the Caribbean, Africa, and the Middle East.

Reading the Evidence

Students compare a Great Society speech, participant testimony, budget and poverty data, an immigration-law excerpt, and movement documents. They distinguish immediate program outputs from long-term social outcomes.

Why It Matters

The era expanded expectations that federal policy could address unequal opportunity. At the same time, Vietnam spending, urban conflict, administrative complexity, and ideological opposition helped produce skepticism about government capacity.

Source Study: Promises and Limits of Federal Reform

Analyze Johnson’s goals, program data, a community participant account, immigration reform, and movement demands.

Vocabulary and Key Understandings

Great SocietyLyndon Johnson’s domestic reform agenda addressing poverty, health, education, cities, and rights.
MedicareFederal health insurance primarily for older Americans.
MedicaidJoint federal-state health coverage for eligible low-income people.
War on PovertyFederal programs aimed at reducing poverty and expanding opportunity.
Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965A law ending national-origin quotas and reshaping immigration policy.
affirmative actionPolicies intended to address barriers and expand opportunity for historically excluded groups.
  • Great Society legislation expanded federal roles in health, education, poverty reduction, environment, and civil rights.
  • The 1965 immigration law contributed to major demographic change over subsequent decades.
  • Women, Latino, Native, Asian American, disability-rights, and gay-rights movements adapted civil-rights strategies.
