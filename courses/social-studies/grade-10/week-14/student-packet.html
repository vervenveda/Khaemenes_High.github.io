Weekly Reading

Setting the Stage

By the 1890s, the United States possessed industrial power, a growing navy, and expanding commercial interests. War with Spain followed Cuban rebellion, humanitarian concern, sensational reporting, strategic calculations, and the explosion of the USS Maine, whose cause was uncertain at the time.

People, Power, and Systems

Victory brought U.S. control of Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippines and temporary military authority in Cuba. Filipino nationalists who had fought Spain resisted American rule, leading to a brutal war. The annexation of Hawaiʻi also followed years of business influence, settler politics, and the overthrow of the Hawaiian monarchy.

Reading the Evidence

Students compare President McKinley’s justification, an Anti-Imperialist League statement, a Filipino declaration, and newspaper images. They distinguish public arguments from underlying interests and evaluate evidence of popular consent.

Why It Matters

The era expanded U.S. influence in the Caribbean and Pacific and raised constitutional questions about citizenship and rights in territories. Puerto Rico’s status and debates over military, commercial, and humanitarian intervention continue to reflect these unresolved issues.

Source Study: Empire or Responsibility?

Compare presidential statements, anti-imperialist arguments, a Filipino independence claim, and political cartoons about territorial acquisition.

Vocabulary and Key Understandings

imperialismThe extension of political, military, or economic control over other peoples or territories.
yellow journalismSensationalized reporting designed to attract attention and shape opinion.
protectorateA territory that retains formal government while a stronger state controls key affairs.
Open Door PolicyA U.S. policy favoring equal commercial access in China.
anti-imperialismOpposition to colonial expansion or imperial rule.
Roosevelt CorollaryA policy asserting a U.S. policing role in parts of the Western Hemisphere.
  • Economic interests, strategic thinking, humanitarian claims, nationalism, and media all influenced expansion.
  • The Spanish-American War produced new U.S. territorial responsibilities and conflicts over consent.
  • Anti-imperialists used constitutional, racial, economic, and moral arguments, sometimes for sharply different reasons.

Daily Lessons

Monday · Frame the Inquiry

Objective: Place American Expansion and the Spanish-American War in chronological and geographic context and construct an initial response to the essential question.

Warm-up: Write three observations or questions suggested by the title “American Expansion and the Spanish-American War.” Then identify one fact you believe you know and one uncertainty.

Lesson: By the 1890s, the United States possessed industrial power, a growing navy, and expanding commercial interests. War with Spain followed Cuban rebellion, humanitarian concern, sensational reporting, strategic calculations, and the explosion of the USS Maine, whose cause was uncertain at the time.

Guided practice: Build a class chronology for 1890–1914 and locate the focal region(s): Caribbean, Pacific, Latin America, and United States. Label turning points, continuities, and unresolved questions.

Independent practice: Complete the weekly inquiry launch: initial claim, two supporting questions, a labeled time-space frame, and one source needed.

Exit check: Explain how chronology or geography prevents one misleading interpretation of this week’s topic.

Tuesday · Source, Contextualize, and Corroborate

Objective: Evaluate primary and secondary sources for origin, purpose, context, evidence, and limitation.

Warm-up: Preview the source study, “Empire or Responsibility?.” Predict two creator purposes and identify evidence needed to test the prediction.

Lesson: Victory brought U.S. control of Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippines and temporary military authority in Cuba. Filipino nationalists who had fought Spain resisted American rule, leading to a brutal war. The annexation of Hawaiʻi also followed years of business influence, settler politics, and the overthrow of the Hawaiian monarchy.

Guided practice: Compare presidential statements, anti-imperialist arguments, a Filipino independence claim, and political cartoons about territorial acquisition.

Independent practice: Complete Assignment 1 using OPVL or HIPP analysis, then corroborate the source with at least one independent item.

Exit check: State one claim the source can support and one claim it cannot establish alone.

Wednesday · Map, Measure, and Model

Objective: Interpret geographic and quantitative evidence while distinguishing pattern, correlation, and causation.

Warm-up: Study the week’s map or data without reading its title. List two visible patterns, one anomaly, and one question.

Lesson: Students compare President McKinley’s justification, an Anti-Imperialist League statement, a Filipino declaration, and newspaper images. They distinguish public arguments from underlying interests and evaluate evidence of popular consent.

Guided practice: Map war theaters, acquired territories, naval routes, the Panama Canal zone, and Caribbean interventions.

Independent practice: Compare trade growth, military spending, territorial populations, and newspaper circulation without assuming correlation proves causation.

Exit check: Describe one pattern, one plausible explanation, and one important limitation of the evidence.

Thursday · Deliberate and Write

Objective: Develop an evidence-based historical argument that addresses a competing interpretation.

Warm-up: Choose an initial position on: Can a republic govern overseas territories without the consent of their inhabitants and remain faithful to its principles?

Lesson: The era expanded U.S. influence in the Caribbean and Pacific and raised constitutional questions about citizenship and rights in territories. Puerto Rico’s status and debates over military, commercial, and humanitarian intervention continue to reflect these unresolved issues.

Guided practice: Use a claim-evidence-reasoning organizer requiring evidence from the weekly reading, source study, and map/data investigation.

Independent practice: Complete Assignment 3 as a seminar contribution, policy-style brief, or formal historical argument with a counterclaim.

Exit check: Revise the claim after hearing a counterargument and identify what evidence caused or resisted revision.

Friday · Synthesize, Assess, and Correct

Objective: Synthesize content and disciplinary skills, complete the weekly assessment, and plan a targeted correction.

Warm-up: Retrieve six key terms and three major understandings without notes; then correct in another color.

Lesson: Review American Expansion and the Spanish-American War through chronology, causation, continuity and change, comparison, geography, institutions, economy, culture, and evidence.

Guided practice: Complete a four-part synthesis: one continuity, one change, one cause, and one consequence.

Independent practice: Complete the weekly quiz and constructed response. Use the correction form to explain each missed item and cite the correct evidence.

Exit check: Answer the essential question in two precise sentences: Did overseas expansion protect American interests, extend republican ideals, or create an empire?
