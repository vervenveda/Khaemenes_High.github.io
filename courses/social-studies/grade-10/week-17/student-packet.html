Weekly Reading

Setting the Stage

The stock-market crash of 1929 did not by itself cause the Great Depression. Bank failures, unequal purchasing power, debt, weak agricultural prices, falling investment, policy mistakes, and international financial connections turned contraction into catastrophe.

People, Power, and Systems

Unemployment reached extraordinary levels, families lost homes and farms, and local charities were overwhelmed. Black, Mexican American, Asian American, and immigrant workers often faced 'last hired, first fired' discrimination. Drought and farming practices contributed to the Dust Bowl and migration.

Reading the Evidence

Students compare unemployment data, a bank depositor’s account, photographs, a business leader’s statement, and testimony from unemployed people. They examine how images can humanize hardship yet still reflect a photographer’s selection and agency.

Why It Matters

The Depression changed expectations about federal responsibility for economic stability and social welfare. It also demonstrates why economic analysis requires systems thinking rather than assigning a single cause.

Source Study: Explaining Economic Collapse

Analyze bank data, unemployment testimony, photography, and competing policy explanations.

Vocabulary and Key Understandings

stock-market crashA rapid, severe decline in stock prices.
bank runA surge of withdrawals caused by fear that a bank will fail.
deflationA sustained decline in the general price level.
unemploymentThe condition of seeking work but lacking a job.
Dust BowlSevere drought and soil erosion affecting the Great Plains during the 1930s.
reliefImmediate aid to people facing economic hardship.
  • The Depression resulted from interacting financial, industrial, agricultural, monetary, and international weaknesses.
  • Bank failures and deflation reduced spending and investment, creating reinforcing decline.
  • Families and communities responded through migration, mutual aid, protest, and political change.

Daily Lessons

Monday · Frame the Inquiry

Objective: Place The Great Depression and the Crisis of Confidence in chronological and geographic context and construct an initial response to the essential question.

Warm-up: Write three observations or questions suggested by the title “The Great Depression and the Crisis of Confidence.” Then identify one fact you believe you know and one uncertainty.

Lesson: The stock-market crash of 1929 did not by itself cause the Great Depression. Bank failures, unequal purchasing power, debt, weak agricultural prices, falling investment, policy mistakes, and international financial connections turned contraction into catastrophe.

Guided practice: Build a class chronology for 1929–1933 and locate the focal region(s): United States and global economy. Label turning points, continuities, and unresolved questions.

Independent practice: Complete the weekly inquiry launch: initial claim, two supporting questions, a labeled time-space frame, and one source needed.

Exit check: Explain how chronology or geography prevents one misleading interpretation of this week’s topic.

Tuesday · Source, Contextualize, and Corroborate

Objective: Evaluate primary and secondary sources for origin, purpose, context, evidence, and limitation.

Warm-up: Preview the source study, “Explaining Economic Collapse.” Predict two creator purposes and identify evidence needed to test the prediction.

Lesson: Unemployment reached extraordinary levels, families lost homes and farms, and local charities were overwhelmed. Black, Mexican American, Asian American, and immigrant workers often faced 'last hired, first fired' discrimination. Drought and farming practices contributed to the Dust Bowl and migration.

Guided practice: Analyze bank data, unemployment testimony, photography, and competing policy explanations.

Independent practice: Complete Assignment 1 using OPVL or HIPP analysis, then corroborate the source with at least one independent item.

Exit check: State one claim the source can support and one claim it cannot establish alone.

Wednesday · Map, Measure, and Model

Objective: Interpret geographic and quantitative evidence while distinguishing pattern, correlation, and causation.

Warm-up: Study the week’s map or data without reading its title. List two visible patterns, one anomaly, and one question.

Lesson: Students compare unemployment data, a bank depositor’s account, photographs, a business leader’s statement, and testimony from unemployed people. They examine how images can humanize hardship yet still reflect a photographer’s selection and agency.

Guided practice: Map bank failures, unemployment, Dust Bowl counties, migration routes, and Florida economic effects.

Independent practice: Construct a causal loop linking bank failures, credit contraction, spending, prices, production, and employment.

Exit check: Describe one pattern, one plausible explanation, and one important limitation of the evidence.

Thursday · Deliberate and Write

Objective: Develop an evidence-based historical argument that addresses a competing interpretation.

Warm-up: Choose an initial position on: Was the Great Depression mainly a failure of markets, policy, institutions, or international coordination?

Lesson: The Depression changed expectations about federal responsibility for economic stability and social welfare. It also demonstrates why economic analysis requires systems thinking rather than assigning a single cause.

Guided practice: Use a claim-evidence-reasoning organizer requiring evidence from the weekly reading, source study, and map/data investigation.

Independent practice: Complete Assignment 3 as a seminar contribution, policy-style brief, or formal historical argument with a counterclaim.

Exit check: Revise the claim after hearing a counterargument and identify what evidence caused or resisted revision.

Friday · Synthesize, Assess, and Correct

Objective: Synthesize content and disciplinary skills, complete the weekly assessment, and plan a targeted correction.

Warm-up: Retrieve six key terms and three major understandings without notes; then correct in another color.

Lesson: Review The Great Depression and the Crisis of Confidence through chronology, causation, continuity and change, comparison, geography, institutions, economy, culture, and evidence.

Guided practice: Complete a four-part synthesis: one continuity, one change, one cause, and one consequence.

Independent practice: Complete the weekly quiz and constructed response. Use the correction form to explain each missed item and cite the correct evidence.

Exit check: Answer the essential question in two precise sentences: Why did an economic downturn become a prolonged social and political crisis?
