Weekly Reading

Setting the Stage

After World War II, rising productivity, high employment, union contracts, veteran benefits, and consumer demand supported broad economic growth. College attendance and homeownership expanded, while television and automobile-centered development reshaped daily life.

People, Power, and Systems

Opportunity was not equally accessible. Local administration, restrictive covenants, lending discrimination, and employment practices limited many Black veterans and families. Highway construction often displaced urban communities, while Native, Latino, and migrant workers experienced distinct forms of exclusion.

Reading the Evidence

Students compare GI Bill provisions, a housing map, a suburban advertisement, oral histories, and income and homeownership data. They separate a policy’s formal text from its local implementation.

Why It Matters

Postwar prosperity built institutions and landscapes that still shape wealth, schools, transportation, and metropolitan inequality. Florida grew rapidly through military investment, tourism, air conditioning, migration, highways, and space-related development.

Source Study: Building the Postwar Dream

Analyze a GI Bill excerpt, mortgage policy, redlining evidence, suburban advertising, and veteran testimony.

Vocabulary and Key Understandings

GI BillFederal benefits supporting veterans’ education, home loans, and transition to civilian life.
suburbanizationPopulation and development growth outside central cities.
baby boomThe major increase in births after World War II.
redliningDiscriminatory lending and investment practices associated with neighborhood racial classifications.
interstate highway systemA federally supported national network of limited-access highways.
Sun BeltThe growing southern and western region of the United States.
  • Federal spending, consumer demand, unions, technology, and global dominance supported postwar growth.
  • Housing, education, and employment programs expanded opportunity but were administered unequally.
  • Suburbanization and highway construction changed cities, land use, segregation, and regional growth.
