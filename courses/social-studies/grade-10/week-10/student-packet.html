Weekly Reading

Setting the Stage

After the Civil War, railroads, land grants, mining, ranching, commercial farming, and migration accelerated western transformation. Federal policy encouraged settlers while confining Native peoples to reservations and attempting cultural assimilation through allotment and boarding schools.

People, Power, and Systems

Native nations followed different strategies depending on geography and political circumstances. Some negotiated, some fought, some pursued legal cases, and many preserved language, religion, governance, and kinship despite dispossession. Black migrants, Mexican American communities, Chinese laborers, women homesteaders, and European immigrants also shaped the West.

Reading the Evidence

Students compare the Dawes Act, a Native leader’s account, railroad advertising, a Black migrant letter, and census maps. They evaluate the language of opportunity against evidence of coercion and exclusion.

Why It Matters

In the South, textile mills, railroads, phosphate mining, citrus, and tourism expanded, including in Florida. Yet sharecropping, convict leasing, segregation, and disenfranchisement limited freedom. National growth therefore depended on policies that distributed opportunity and burden unequally.

Source Study: Land Policy and Lived Experience

Compare the Homestead Act, Dawes Act, a Native testimony, an Exoduster letter, and railroad promotional material.

Vocabulary and Key Understandings

reservationLand designated by the federal government for Native nations, often through coercive policy.
allotmentDivision of communally held tribal land into individual parcels under federal policy.
Homestead ActA federal law offering western land to eligible settlers who met conditions.
ExodusterAn African American migrant who moved from the South to the West after Reconstruction.
New SouthA postwar vision of a diversified, industrializing southern economy.
convict leasingA system in which governments leased incarcerated people to private employers.
  • Western settlement was supported by federal land policy, military force, railroads, and corporate investment.
  • Native nations used diplomacy, armed resistance, legal action, cultural persistence, and adaptation to defend sovereignty.
  • The post-Reconstruction South combined industrial development with racial exclusion and coercive labor.
