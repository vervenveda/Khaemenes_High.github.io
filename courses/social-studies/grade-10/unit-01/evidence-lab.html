How to read historical evidence

  1. Observe: What does the source actually say or depict?
  2. Source: Who created it, when, for whom, and for what purpose?
  3. Contextualize: What was happening around its creation?
  4. Corroborate: Which independent sources agree, differ, or are missing?
  5. Argue: Make a limited claim and cite two precise details, explaining uncertainty.

Evidence rule: An archive's modern interpretive introduction is a secondary source; an original document is a primary source for the questions its date and provenance can answer. Neither is automatically complete or unbiased.

Week 1: History, memory, and the American story

Evidence: Smithsonian National Museum of the American Indian · Native Knowledge 360°

Source type: Interpretive educational framework, incorporating Native perspectives.

The framework emphasizes that Native peoples have diverse cultures and histories and remain present-day nations and communities.

Historical inquiry

  1. How might a national narrative change when it begins with Indigenous communities rather than European arrival?
  2. What evidence would be needed to support a claim about a particular Indigenous nation?
  3. Why must this modern interpretive framework not be mislabeled a primary source from 1491?

Week 2: Indigenous nations and Atlantic encounters

Evidence: Smithsonian · Native Knowledge 360° lessons and resources

Source type: Curated teaching resources and Indigenous historical perspectives.

European imperial charters and maps are records of European claims, not evidence that land was empty or that Indigenous sovereignty disappeared.

Historical inquiry

  1. Distinguish an empire's territorial claim from a Native nation's sovereignty.
  2. Name two types of Indigenous-generated or community-informed evidence that would help test a colonization narrative.
  3. How did exchange, resistance, and disease interact without producing identical outcomes everywhere?

Week 3: Independence and competing liberties

Evidence: U.S. National Archives · Declaration of Independence

Source type: Primary-source transcript, 1776.

The Declaration states principles and grievances used to justify separation from Britain. Its political ideals must be compared with contemporaneous law and experiences, including people excluded from equal rights.

Historical inquiry

  1. Identify one political principle and one grievance articulated in the Declaration.
  2. Whose experiences or voices are not fully represented by its signatories?
  3. What additional source would help evaluate whether its ideals were realized?

Online source optional; the context and questions above remain accessible when offline. If internet is available, inspect the original source before making a direct quotation.

Week 4: Constitution, federalism, and the early republic

Evidence: U.S. National Archives · Constitution and Bill of Rights

Source type: Primary-source texts, constitutional documents.

Compare the Constitution's allocation of national powers and the amendments protecting specified liberties. Remember that early constitutional rights and political participation were unequal in practice.

Historical inquiry

  1. Find an example of delegated federal authority and explain the branch responsible.
  2. Compare one protection in the Bill of Rights with a possible conflict over government power.
  3. What evidence besides constitutional language is needed to evaluate whose rights were protected in practice?
