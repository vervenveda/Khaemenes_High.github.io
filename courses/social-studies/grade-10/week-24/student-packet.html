Weekly Reading

Setting the Stage

Students launched sit-ins, formed SNCC, rode interstate buses, registered voters, and organized local campaigns. National organizations and local leaders collaborated and sometimes disagreed about pace, leadership, and tactics.

People, Power, and Systems

Campaigns in Birmingham, Mississippi, Selma, and elsewhere exposed violent resistance. The Civil Rights Act and Voting Rights Act expanded federal enforcement. Yet housing discrimination, unequal schools, employment barriers, police violence, and poverty remained.

Reading the Evidence

Students compare a SNCC statement, Martin Luther King Jr.’s argument, Fannie Lou Hamer’s testimony, Malcolm X, legislation, and movement photographs. They avoid reducing the movement to a few famous leaders.

Why It Matters

The movement transformed law and political participation, but it also broadened toward economic justice, northern inequality, and Black Power. Its legacy demonstrates both the power and limits of federal legislation.

Source Study: Strategies for Freedom

Compare SNCC, King, Hamer, Malcolm X, federal legislation, and local campaign evidence.

Vocabulary and Key Understandings

sit-inA protest in which participants occupy a space and refuse to leave.
Freedom RidesInterracial bus journeys challenging segregation in interstate travel.
SNCCThe Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee.
Civil Rights Act of 1964A federal law prohibiting discrimination in major areas of public life and employment.
Voting Rights Act of 1965A federal law targeting racial discrimination in voting.
Black PowerA broad movement emphasizing self-determination, political power, cultural pride, and varied strategies.
  • Student activism and local campaigns widened the movement beyond courtroom strategy.
  • Televised violence affected public opinion, but organizing, negotiation, and political pressure produced legislation.
  • Movement participants debated nonviolence, self-defense, integration, economic justice, and community control.
