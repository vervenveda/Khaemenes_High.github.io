Week 9 · Reconstruction and the Meaning of Freedom

Archival evidence: National Archives · Reconstruction Amendments

Classification: Primary-source constitutional texts

The Thirteenth Amendment abolished slavery except as criminal punishment, the Fourteenth defined citizenship and equal protection, and the Fifteenth restricted racial discrimination in voting rights. Constitutional guarantees and lived enforcement must be studied separately.

Source investigation

  1. Identify one specific protection created by each amendment. How do their purposes differ?
  2. Which forms of evidence could show a gap between the rights stated in constitutional law and their enforcement during Reconstruction?
  3. Explain how a legal change might be historically transformative even if implementation remains contested.

Week 10 · Westward Expansion, Native Sovereignty, and the New South

Archival evidence: National Archives · Dawes Act (1887)

Classification: Primary-source federal legislation

The Dawes Act authorized the allotment of reservation lands into individual holdings. Examine the law as evidence of federal policy—not as proof of Indigenous consent—and distinguish stated intentions from land loss and community consequences.

Source investigation

  1. Whose decisions does the statute authorize, and which communities' authority is constrained?
  2. How does dividing collectively held land into individual allotments affect sovereignty and land relationships?
  3. Identify two additional perspectives or records needed to evaluate the law's outcomes.

Week 11 · Industrial Capitalism, Labor, and Immigration

Archival evidence: National Archives · Interstate Commerce Act (1887)

Classification: Primary-source federal legislation

Railroad expansion connected regions and markets but also prompted disputes over pricing, corporate power, and regulation. The 1887 Interstate Commerce Act established federal oversight of interstate railroad practices.

Source investigation

  1. Why did interstate railroad commerce present challenges that individual states might not resolve alone?
  2. Compare an imagined farmer's complaint about freight rates with a railroad company's explanation of operating costs. What records would test either argument?
  3. What evidence would demonstrate whether federal regulation changed economic outcomes?

Week 12 · Gilded Age Politics, Jim Crow, and Populism

Archival evidence: National Archives · Plessy v. Ferguson (1896)

Classification: Primary-source judicial decision and historical context

The Supreme Court upheld a state segregation law in Plessy v. Ferguson. Examine the reasoning as an example of how constitutional language, judicial interpretation, and discriminatory practices interacted; do not equate legal approval with equality.

Source investigation

  1. What legal issue and interpretation did the Supreme Court address in this case?
  2. What evidence of actual conditions would help assess claims that separated public facilities were equal?
  3. Connect judicial interpretation to broader struggles over citizenship and political participation.
