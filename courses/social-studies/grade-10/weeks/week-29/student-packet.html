Weekly Reading

Setting the Stage

By the 1980s and 1990s, the United States was becoming more deeply integrated into global systems of production, finance, trade, migration, and communication. Manufacturing employment declined sharply in many older industrial communities, but no single cause explains the change. Automation increased output with fewer workers; firms reorganized production; transportation and container shipping lowered costs; trade agreements changed incentives; and corporations built supply chains that crossed national borders. At the same time, service, finance, healthcare, logistics, and technology employment expanded. National economic growth could therefore coexist with severe local disruption. To evaluate globalization historically, students must separate total gains from their distribution and ask who gained, who absorbed risk, and how public policy responded.

People, Power, and Systems

Immigration after the 1965 Immigration and Nationality Act transformed the United States just as these economic changes accelerated. New arrivals from Latin America, Asia, the Caribbean, Africa, and the Middle East joined long-established communities and internal migrants. Immigrants built businesses, supplied labor, created cultural institutions, sent remittances abroad, and changed electoral coalitions and metropolitan life. Yet immigration debates often collapsed very different experiences into one category. A strong historical explanation distinguishes legal status, skill level, region, occupation, generation, and the receiving community. It also recognizes that migration is shaped by both U.S. policy and conditions abroad, including family networks, labor demand, conflict, and uneven development.

Reading the Evidence

This week’s evidence laboratory asks students to compare trade-policy arguments, displaced-worker testimony, immigrant entrepreneurship, corporate claims, labor-market statistics, and evidence about the emerging digital economy. Each source measures a different level of experience. National productivity data may show aggregate gains while a factory closure reveals concentrated local loss. A business advertisement may show optimism about technology but not prove broad access. A personal testimony can reveal lived experience without establishing national frequency. Students therefore practice a distributional test: identify the unit of analysis, the population represented, the time period, and the outcome being measured before deciding whether two sources actually conflict.

Why It Matters

The early Internet, personal computing, and expanding telecommunications altered how Americans worked, learned, bought goods, organized politically, and encountered information. These technologies reduced some barriers to communication but also produced a digital divide shaped by income, geography, infrastructure, education, and skills. Florida illustrates the convergence of these forces: migration, tourism, ports, aerospace, finance, services, and ties to Latin America and the Caribbean made the state a major gateway in the new economy. The central historical problem is not whether globalization was simply good or bad. It is how interconnected systems redistributed opportunity, bargaining power, vulnerability, and political conflict across workers, consumers, firms, regions, and communities.

Vocabulary and Key Understandings

globalizationThe intensification of cross-border economic, cultural, political, and technological connections.
deindustrializationThe decline or relocation of manufacturing employment and capacity in a region.
information economyEconomic activity centered on data, communication, services, and knowledge.
free-trade agreementA treaty reducing barriers to trade among participating countries.
digital divideUnequal access to computers, connectivity, and digital skills.
remittanceMoney sent by migrants to family or communities elsewhere.
  • Globalization redistributed gains and losses through several mechanisms at once: trade, automation, finance, logistics, corporate strategy, and public policy.
  • Immigration after 1965 reshaped labor markets, entrepreneurship, culture, politics, and community life while connecting U.S. communities more tightly to global networks.
  • Digital technology lowered some barriers to information and communication while creating new inequalities in access, skills, market power, and geographic opportunity.

Daily Lessons

Monday · How Did Globalization Reshape the American Economy?

Objective: Use trade, technology, and production evidence to distinguish globalization from the broader processes of automation and corporate restructuring.

Warm-up: List three possible causes of manufacturing employment change. Rank them only after identifying what evidence would be needed to test each explanation.

Lesson: Globalization was not one policy or one event. It was the growing integration of production, finance, trade, migration, and communication across borders. Students will trace several mechanisms separately—automation, supply chains, transportation costs, trade rules, finance, and corporate strategy—before asking how they interacted. This prevents a common causal error: attributing every manufacturing job loss to foreign trade or every productivity gain to technology. The historical task is to identify mechanisms, timing, and geography, then explain why national growth and local economic pain could occur at the same time.

Guided practice: Build a mechanism chart separating automation, trade, logistics, finance, and corporate restructuring. Add one piece of evidence that could support or weaken each mechanism.

Independent practice: Write a 250–350 word causal explanation of why national growth and local job loss can occur simultaneously. Use at least three mechanisms and one geographic distinction.

Exit check: Explain how chronology or geography prevents one misleading interpretation of this week’s topic.

Tuesday · Immigration, Opportunity, and Community Change

Objective: Evaluate immigration evidence by distinguishing national trends from local experience and by identifying how policy, labor demand, family networks, and conditions abroad shaped migration.

Warm-up: Compare two short migration stories. Identify what each reveals and what neither can establish about national immigration trends.

Lesson: Post-1965 immigration changed the demographic and economic geography of the United States. Students will examine immigrant entrepreneurship, labor participation, family reunification, remittances, and cultural institution-building while avoiding the assumption that immigrants formed one uniform group. Source analysis will focus on who is represented, which legal or occupational category is being discussed, and whether the evidence describes a national pattern or a specific community. Corroboration requires comparing personal testimony with demographic and labor-market evidence rather than treating either form as sufficient alone.

Guided practice: Use an OPVL/HIPP matrix on immigrant testimony and one demographic or labor source. Mark where personal experience and aggregate evidence corroborate or complicate each other.

Independent practice: Complete Assignment 1 by evaluating the source set and writing a qualified claim about how immigration reshaped one U.S. community or economic sector.

Exit check: State one claim the source can support and one claim it cannot establish alone.

Wednesday · Who Gained, Who Lost, and at What Scale?

Objective: Interpret economic and geographic evidence by separating aggregate gains from distributional effects and by testing alternative explanations for regional change.

Warm-up: Study two charts at different geographic scales. Identify one pattern that appears nationally but changes when viewed regionally.

Lesson: A national average can hide major differences among regions and households. Students will compare productivity, employment, wages, prices, migration, and digital access across multiple scales. They will identify whether an observed relationship is temporal, geographic, or causal and will test competing explanations for deindustrialization. The goal is to build a distributional analysis: total output may rise while some workers lose bargaining power, some consumers gain from lower prices, some cities attract new investment, and others experience population loss and fiscal strain.

Guided practice: Create a four-column table: indicator, observed change, plausible mechanism, limitation. Use productivity, manufacturing jobs, income, migration, and internet access.

Independent practice: Complete Assignment 2 by producing a map/data explanation that distinguishes aggregate gains from distributional effects and explicitly avoids treating correlation as proof of cause.

Exit check: Describe one pattern, one plausible explanation, and one important limitation of the evidence.

Thursday · Growth, Disruption, and Public Responsibility

Objective: Construct an evidence-based policy argument about how governments should respond when broad economic gains are accompanied by concentrated losses.

Warm-up: Define one measurable meaning of 'economic success' and one meaning of 'community disruption.' Explain why the measures could point in different directions.

Lesson: Historical policy debates turn on both evidence and values. Students will distinguish empirical questions—who lost employment, how long adjustment took, which programs worked—from normative questions about responsibility and fairness. They will compare approaches such as worker retraining, wage insurance, regional investment, education, infrastructure, and nonintervention. A strong argument must identify the intended beneficiary, mechanism, cost, possible unintended consequence, and evidence that would show whether the policy succeeded.

Guided practice: Compare two policy responses to concentrated economic disruption. For each, identify beneficiary, mechanism, cost, evidence of effectiveness, and possible unintended consequence.

Independent practice: Complete Assignment 3 as an evidence-based policy brief or seminar argument answering the week’s revised prompt and addressing a substantial counterargument.

Exit check: Revise the claim after hearing a counterargument and identify what evidence caused or resisted revision.

Friday · A Connected Nation: Synthesis and Mastery

Objective: Synthesize globalization, immigration, technology, and regional change into a multi-causal explanation and complete the weekly mastery assessment.

Warm-up: From memory, draw a causal chain connecting at least six of these: trade, automation, migration, productivity, prices, wages, digital access, regional change, political response.

Lesson: Students will build a final causal model linking global production, technology, migration, labor markets, consumer effects, regional change, and political response. The model must show that different mechanisms can reinforce or offset one another. Students then answer the essential question using evidence at more than one scale and complete the weekly assessment. Corrections require explaining why the original choice or claim was weaker, not merely recording the correct answer.

Guided practice: Test the chain for missing links and label which connections are strongly supported, plausible but uncertain, or contradicted by the week’s evidence.

Independent practice: Complete the 10-question mastery quiz and constructed response. For every missed item, explain the misconception and cite the evidence that corrects it.

Exit check: Answer the essential question in two precise sentences: How did globalization and digital technology redistribute opportunity, risk, and political power?
