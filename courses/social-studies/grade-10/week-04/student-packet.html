Weekly Reading

Setting the Stage

The Articles of Confederation created a weak central government that struggled with finance, interstate disputes, and enforcement. The Constitutional Convention designed a stronger federal system through representation compromises, separated powers, and enumerated authority. The Constitution also protected slavery through political compromises without using the word itself.

People, Power, and Systems

Ratification debates revealed competing fears. Federalists argued that an extended republic and divided powers could control faction; Anti-Federalists feared distant authority and demanded explicit protections for individual rights. The Bill of Rights became a condition of political trust, though its protections were initially directed mainly at the federal government.

Reading the Evidence

Students compare Federalist No. 10, an Anti-Federalist essay, constitutional clauses, and an early Supreme Court opinion. They identify claim, evidence, institutional design, and assumptions about human behavior.

Why It Matters

Constitutional interpretation remains central because the document establishes structures while leaving many applications contested. Understanding amendment, precedent, federalism, and civic participation allows students to evaluate modern disputes without reducing them to slogans.

Source Study: Designing a Republic

Analyze selected clauses, Federalist No. 10, an Anti-Federalist objection, and Marbury v. Madison. Trace how each addresses the problem of concentrated power.

Vocabulary and Key Understandings

federalismA system dividing authority between national and state governments.
separation of powersDivision of governmental powers among branches.
checks and balancesConstitutional mechanisms allowing branches to restrain one another.
ratificationFormal approval of a constitution, treaty, or amendment.
judicial reviewThe power of courts to assess whether government action is constitutional.
factionA group united by interests or beliefs that may compete for political power.
  • The Constitution was a negotiated framework rather than a complete settlement of political conflict.
  • Federalists and Anti-Federalists disagreed about the risks of national power and majoritarian rule.
  • Early precedents shaped federal authority, political parties, foreign policy, and judicial review.
