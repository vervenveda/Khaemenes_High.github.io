Housing: Renting, Mortgages, and Total Occupancy Cost

Essential question: How should a household compare renting and buying without assuming either choice is always superior?

Key understandings

  • Housing cost includes utilities, transportation, insurance, fees, maintenance, taxes, deposits, and transaction costs.
  • Buying can build equity but creates concentration, maintenance, price, interest-rate, and mobility risk.
  • Lease and mortgage decisions require contract review, inspection, realistic cash flow, and contingency planning.

Vocabulary

leaseA contract granting temporary use of property under stated terms.
security depositMoney held to cover specified damage or obligations, subject to law.
mortgageA loan secured by real property.
escrowFunds held by a third party for taxes, insurance, or transaction conditions.
equityProperty value minus debt secured by the property.
closing costFees and charges required to complete a real-estate purchase and financing.

Weekly Reading

Renting

Renting can provide flexibility and lower responsibility for major maintenance, but rent may rise and no ownership equity is created. Deposits, application fees, utilities, renters insurance, parking, pets, and commuting affect total cost.

Buying

A mortgage payment includes principal and interest; property taxes, insurance, association fees, repairs, and closing costs add substantial expense. A low down payment can improve access while raising payment, insurance, and negative-equity risk.

Time Horizon

Transaction costs make buying less attractive for short stays. Expected appreciation is uncertain and local. Renting and investing the difference can outperform ownership in some scenarios; ownership can outperform in others.

Due Diligence

Consumers should review inspection, title, flood and hazard risk, insurance availability, taxes, association documents, loan estimate, closing disclosure, and emergency reserves before committing.
