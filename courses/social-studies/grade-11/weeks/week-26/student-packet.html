Externalities, Public Goods, and Collective Action

Essential question: When do private choices create public costs or benefits that justify collective action?

Key understandings

  • Market prices may omit harms or benefits affecting people outside a transaction.
  • Public goods and common resources create different collective-action problems.
  • Policy tools include taxes, subsidies, standards, property rules, information, public provision, and tradable permits.

Vocabulary

externalityA cost or benefit imposed on a third party and not fully reflected in a transaction price.
public goodA good that is difficult to exclude people from and can be used without substantially reducing availability to others.
common resourceA resource that is difficult to exclude users from but can be depleted by use.
free riderA person who benefits from a good or action without contributing to its cost.
Pigouvian taxA tax intended to align private incentives with an external social cost.
cap-and-tradeA system limiting total emissions and allowing permits to be traded.

Weekly Reading

Private and Social Costs

A factory and consumer may agree on a price while nearby residents bear pollution. Education or vaccination can create benefits beyond the buyer. Externalities cause private decisions to differ from socially preferred quantities.

Public Goods and Common Resources

National defense and certain information are difficult to exclude and nonrival, encouraging free riding. Fisheries and groundwater are difficult to exclude but rival, creating overuse. Classification depends on technology and institutions, not labels alone.

Policy Instruments

A tax can price harm, a subsidy can encourage benefit, a standard can prohibit dangerous conduct, and tradable permits can limit total quantity. Public provision, liability, bargaining, property rights, and disclosure may work in other settings.

Government Failure

Intervention can be poorly targeted, captured, costly, or based on weak information. The relevant comparison is not perfect government versus imperfect markets, but realistic alternatives with evidence, administration, and accountability.
