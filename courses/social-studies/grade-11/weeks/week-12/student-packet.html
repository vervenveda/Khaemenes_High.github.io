Due Process, Privacy, and Criminal Procedure

Essential question: How should legal procedure protect liberty and fairness while enabling government to investigate, prosecute, and prevent harm?

Key understandings

  • Criminal procedure distributes power among police, prosecutors, defense counsel, judges, juries, and appellate courts.
  • Rights depend on definitions such as search, seizure, custody, interrogation, probable cause, and reasonable expectation of privacy.
  • Digital technology creates new questions about location data, devices, biometrics, surveillance, and informational privacy.

Vocabulary

due processConstitutional requirements of fair law, notice, procedure, and impartial decision-making before government deprives life, liberty, or property.
probable causeA reasonable basis, grounded in facts, to believe a crime occurred or evidence will be found.
search warrantA judicial authorization to search a specified place or person for described evidence.
exclusionary ruleA remedy that may bar evidence obtained through certain constitutional violations.
self-incriminationCompelled testimonial evidence exposing a person to criminal liability.
right to counselThe constitutional protection of legal assistance in specified criminal proceedings.

Weekly Reading

Procedure as a Constitutional Safeguard

The Fourth, Fifth, Sixth, Eighth, and Fourteenth Amendments establish limits and procedures for investigation, prosecution, trial, and punishment. These protections do not decide whether a person is innocent; they regulate how government may establish and punish guilt.

Search and Seizure

A warrant normally requires probable cause, particularity, and a neutral magistrate, but doctrine contains exceptions. A constitutional analysis asks whether government conduct was a search or seizure, whether a person had a protected interest, and whether the action was reasonable.

Fair Process

The right to counsel, confrontation, jury trial, notice, and protection against compelled self-incrimination support adversarial testing. Due process also operates outside criminal law when government affects benefits, licenses, education, employment, or other protected interests.

Technology and Privacy

Phones, cloud accounts, facial recognition, DNA databases, cameras, and location records blur the boundary between public observation and intimate information. Courts often adapt older constitutional principles to new capabilities, making factual precision essential.
