Inflation, Unemployment, and the Business Cycle

Essential question: How can policymakers respond to inflation and unemployment when causes, timing, and tradeoffs differ across economic episodes?

Key understandings

  • Inflation is a rate of general price change, not simply a high price or one product becoming more expensive.
  • The unemployment rate excludes people not in the labor force and should be interpreted with participation, hours, wages, and underemployment.
  • Demand shocks, supply shocks, financial disruptions, policy, expectations, and structural change can produce different downturns.

Vocabulary

inflationA sustained rise in the general price level, reducing money’s purchasing power.
consumer price indexA measure of average price change for a defined basket of consumer goods and services.
unemployment rateThe unemployed actively seeking work as a percentage of the labor force.
labor force participation rateThe labor force as a percentage of the civilian noninstitutional working-age population.
recessionA significant, broad decline in economic activity lasting more than a brief fluctuation.
business cycleRecurring expansions and contractions in aggregate economic activity.

Weekly Reading

Measuring Price Change

Price indexes compare the cost of a representative basket over time. Substitution, quality change, new products, housing treatment, and household differences complicate measurement. Inflation can vary widely by category and family spending pattern.

Measuring Labor Markets

A person without a job is counted as unemployed only when available for work and actively seeking it under survey definitions. Participation, employment-population ratio, job openings, hours, and wage growth add context.

Cycles and Shocks

Recessions may follow financial crises, collapsing demand, policy tightening, supply disruption, or external shock. Recovery can be uneven by region, industry, race, age, education, and wealth.

Policy Tradeoffs

Fiscal and monetary policy can support demand and employment but may operate with lags or intensify inflation. Supply policies address capacity and productivity but often take time. The correct response depends on diagnosis, expectations, distribution, and institutional credibility.
