International Trade, Exchange Rates, and Global Interdependence

Essential question: How should nations balance gains from global exchange with security, labor, environmental, and adjustment concerns?

Key understandings

  • Comparative advantage explains potential gains from trade, while distribution and adjustment determine who benefits or loses.
  • Tariffs protect some domestic producers but raise costs for consumers and downstream firms and may trigger retaliation.
  • Trade balances interact with saving, investment, exchange rates, and capital flows rather than functioning as a simple national profit statement.

Vocabulary

tariffA tax imposed on imported goods or services.
quotaA quantitative limit on imports or exports.
exchange rateThe price of one currency in terms of another.
appreciationAn increase in a currency’s value relative to another currency.
trade balanceThe value of exports minus imports of goods and services.
capital flowCross-border investment, lending, borrowing, or asset purchase.

Weekly Reading

Why Trade Occurs

Differences in opportunity cost, resources, skills, scale, technology, climate, and consumer preference create gains from exchange. Trade can lower prices, expand variety, spread technology, and support specialization.

Barriers and Strategic Concerns

Tariffs, quotas, standards, procurement rules, subsidies, and sanctions alter trade. Governments may cite infant industries, national security, unfair practices, labor, environment, or bargaining leverage. Each justification requires evidence and a realistic comparison with alternatives.

Exchange Rates

Currency appreciation makes imports cheaper and exports more expensive to foreign buyers, all else equal. Rates respond to interest, inflation, growth expectations, capital flows, risk, and policy. Effects differ for consumers, exporters, travelers, debtors, and investors.

Balances and Capital

A current-account deficit is matched by financial flows in national accounting. It can accompany productive investment or unsustainable borrowing. Treating exports as wins and imports as losses ignores consumption benefits, inputs, ownership, and financing.
