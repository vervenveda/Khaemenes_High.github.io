Federalism in Practice

Essential question: When should a public problem be addressed nationally, by states, locally, or through shared authority?

Key understandings

  • Federalism can permit local adaptation, policy experimentation, and multiple access points for participation.
  • National authority can address interstate effects, protect rights, and create uniform standards.
  • Many real policies are administered through overlapping funding, regulation, and implementation responsibilities.

Vocabulary

federalismA constitutional system dividing authority between national and state governments.
reserved powersPowers retained by states or the people under the Tenth Amendment.
concurrent powersPowers exercised by both national and state governments.
preemptionDisplacement of state or local law by valid federal law.
intergovernmental relationsCooperation, bargaining, conflict, and fiscal relationships among levels of government.
home ruleAuthority granted to local governments to manage local affairs.

Weekly Reading

A Dynamic Relationship

Federalism is not a fixed list applied mechanically. Constitutional text, court decisions, grants, mandates, emergencies, and political bargaining continually shape the relationship between national and state authority.

Why Divide Power?

States can tailor policy to local conditions and serve as laboratories. National action may be necessary when harms cross borders, rights require protection, or fragmented rules impose large costs. Local governments often deliver services closest to residents but depend on state-created authority.

Conflict and Cooperation

Education, transportation, healthcare, elections, environmental protection, disaster response, and criminal justice commonly involve several levels. Funding conditions can influence policy without direct national administration, while preemption can override conflicting rules.

Florida and the Union

Florida interacts with counties, municipalities, school districts, special districts, other states, tribal governments, and the federal government. Students should identify who has legal authority, who pays, who administers, and who can be held accountable.
