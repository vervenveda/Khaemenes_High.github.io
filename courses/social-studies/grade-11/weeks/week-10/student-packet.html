Courts, Judicial Review, and Legal Reasoning

Essential question: What gives courts authority to interpret law, and how should judges justify decisions that affect public power and individual rights?

Key understandings

  • Federal and state courts have different jurisdictions, structures, appointment systems, and relationships.
  • A case brief separates facts, issue, rule, reasoning, holding, and significance.
  • Constitutional interpretation uses text, structure, history, precedent, purpose, and consequences in contested combinations.

Vocabulary

jurisdictionA court’s legal authority to hear and decide a category of case.
judicial reviewThe power to determine whether government action is consistent with the Constitution.
precedentA prior judicial decision used as authority in a later case.
stare decisisThe principle of generally following established precedent while allowing justified departure.
holdingThe court’s authoritative resolution of the legal issue necessary to decide the case.
dissentA judicial opinion disagreeing with the majority’s result or reasoning.

Weekly Reading

A Dual Court System

The United States contains federal and state court systems. Federal district courts usually conduct trials, courts of appeals review legal error, and the Supreme Court exercises limited original and broad discretionary appellate jurisdiction. State courts handle most criminal, family, property, contract, and state-law disputes.

Judicial Review

Marbury v. Madison articulated the judiciary’s duty to apply the Constitution when a statute conflicts with it. Judicial review does not permit courts to decide any political question; courts require jurisdiction, a live dispute, proper parties, and a legal basis for relief.

Reading Opinions

The syllabus or opening identifies the judgment. The majority’s holding controls; concurrences may agree for different reasons; dissents preserve competing arguments but do not govern the case. Facts and procedural posture matter because holdings are narrower than slogans.

Legitimacy and Constraint

Courts lack direct control of budgets and enforcement. Their authority depends on legal reasoning, institutional acceptance, appointment, precedent, compliance, and public confidence. Judicial independence protects law from immediate pressure, while transparency and reasoned opinions support accountability.
