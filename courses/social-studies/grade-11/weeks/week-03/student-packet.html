Declaration, Revolution, and the Articles

Essential question: How did the principles used to justify independence create both a standard for government and a problem for the new nation?

Key understandings

  • The Declaration combines universal principles, a theory of legitimate government, and a list of specific grievances.
  • The Articles protected state authority but gave the Confederation limited capacity to tax, regulate trade, or enforce decisions.
  • The revolution’s ideals were broader than the rights actually enjoyed by many people in the new nation.

Vocabulary

grievanceA stated complaint alleging an abuse, injury, or violation of a principle.
unalienable rightsRights that cannot legitimately be surrendered or removed by government.
consent of the governedThe principle that legitimate political authority depends on the people’s authorization.
confederationA union in which member states retain substantial sovereignty and the central authority is limited.
requisitionA request from the Confederation Congress to states for money or resources.
sovereigntySupreme political authority within a territory or political community.

Weekly Reading

From Protest to Independence

Colonial resistance moved from claims about inherited English rights to an argument that political authority itself had become illegitimate. The Declaration explains why governments exist, where their just powers originate, and when a people may alter or abolish a government.

A Confederation of States

The Articles of Confederation created a national congress but no separate executive or national judiciary. Congress could conduct diplomacy, declare war, and manage western lands, yet depended heavily on states for revenue and compliance.

Capacity and Liberty

The Articles reflected fear of centralized power. Their weaknesses became visible in interstate trade disputes, debt problems, foreign-policy vulnerability, and difficulty responding to unrest. The challenge was to increase national capacity without recreating arbitrary government.

Unfinished Equality

Independence did not erase slavery, dispossession, restrictions on women, property qualifications, or unequal citizenship. Later movements repeatedly used the Declaration’s language to measure the nation against its stated principles.
