Taxes, Public Spending, and Government Budgets

Essential question: How should government raise and spend money when every tax and budget choice distributes burdens, benefits, risk, and opportunity?

Key understandings

  • Tax analysis should distinguish statutory payer, economic incidence, marginal rate, average rate, base, exemptions, and credits.
  • Budgets express public priorities but are constrained by law, revenue, debt, capacity, and political bargaining.
  • A sound public-spending analysis considers goals, evidence, alternatives, distribution, implementation, and opportunity cost.

Vocabulary

progressive taxA tax whose effective rate rises as the tax base or ability to pay rises.
proportional taxA tax applying the same rate across the tax base.
regressive taxA tax whose burden consumes a larger share of lower income than higher income.
transfer paymentA government payment that is not direct compensation for current production.
budget deficitThe amount by which government spending exceeds revenue during a period.
appropriationLegal authority to spend public money for a designated purpose.

Weekly Reading

How Governments Raise Revenue

Federal, state, and local governments use income, payroll, sales, property, excise, corporate, fee, and other revenues. A tax can be described by its rate, base, filing unit, exemptions, credits, timing, and administration.

Who Bears a Tax

The person who sends payment to government may not bear the full economic burden. Prices, wages, profits, rents, and behavior can change. Equity analysis considers ability to pay, benefits received, horizontal treatment, and distribution.

Public Budgets

Budgets finance defense, courts, infrastructure, education, health, safety, administration, transfers, debt service, and many other functions. Mandatory and discretionary categories, trust funds, state balanced-budget rules, and local revenue limits shape choices.

Evaluating Programs

A program should be judged against a clear objective and counterfactual. Inputs, outputs, outcomes, unintended effects, administrative cost, access, fraud controls, and long-term obligations all matter. Cutting a cost is not necessarily eliminating a need; increasing spending is not necessarily improving an outcome.
