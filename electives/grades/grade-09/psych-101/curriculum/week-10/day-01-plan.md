# Week 10 · Day 01
## Social Influence, Norms & Conformity

**Week Unit:** Social & Cultural Psychology

**Essential Question:** Why do people sometimes change behavior when other people are present?

## Learning Objectives
Students can:
- define social influence and social norms;
- distinguish informational from normative influence;
- explain conformity without treating it as weakness;
- identify conditions that can strengthen or reduce conformity;
- distinguish group influence from proof that a belief is true.

## Vocabulary in Context
**social influence · social norm · conformity · informational influence · normative influence · unanimity · group size · public response · private belief**

## Core Reading
People often use other people as information. In an uncertain situation, observing what others do may help identify what is safe, normal, or effective. This is one reason **informational influence** can be useful.

People also care about belonging, approval, reputation, and avoiding social cost. **Normative influence** occurs when behavior shifts because acceptance or rejection matters.

Conformity therefore should not be reduced to a character flaw. The scientific question is not “Who is weak?” but “What information, incentives, uncertainty, group structure, and response conditions changed behavior?”

A person can conform publicly without privately agreeing. A person can also privately accept group information. These are psychologically different outcomes.

## Worked Example
A fictional student changes an answer after seeing every other group member choose a different option.

Possible informational explanation: the student thinks the group may know something they missed.

Possible normative explanation: the student wants to avoid standing out.

The observed answer change alone does not reveal which process occurred.

## Psychological Scientist's Desk
### Claim
“People conform because they are weak.”

Judgment: **Unsupported moral interpretation.**

Students replace the moral label with measurable variables: uncertainty, unanimity, group size, expertise, public/private response, and social cost.

## Data Lens
Fictional conformity rates:

| Condition | Conforming responses |
|---|---:|
| Alone | 8% |
| Group, one dissenter | 19% |
| Unanimous group | 37% |
| Unanimous group + public answer | 45% |

Students identify:
- which condition has the highest conformity;
- whether the data prove normative influence;
- what additional evidence would distinguish normative from informational influence;
- why percentages describe groups rather than determine individuals.

## Claims Laboratory
**Claim:** “If many people believe something, it is probably true.”

Judgment: **Cannot be inferred from popularity alone.**

Students distinguish social consensus from independent evidence.

## Guided Investigation
### Why Did the Person Conform?
Students analyze six fictional scenarios and classify the strongest current explanation as informational, normative, mixed, or cannot tell. Each answer requires evidence.

## Independent Assignment
### Conformity Conditions Audit
For two fictional cases, students identify:
1. the social norm;
2. uncertainty level;
3. whether response is public or private;
4. possible social cost;
5. one alternative explanation;
6. one experimental manipulation that could test the explanation.

## Research Notebook Checkpoint
If social influence may affect the semester question, define the social variable precisely. Do not write only “peer pressure.” Specify what peers do, what information is visible, and what response is measured.
