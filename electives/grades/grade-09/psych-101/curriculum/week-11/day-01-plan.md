# Week 11 · Day 1 — Stress as a Psychophysiological Response

**Essential Question:** What happens when perceived demands challenge available resources?

## Learning Objectives
Students will be able to:
- define stress and stressor without equating stress with disorder;
- distinguish environmental demand from appraisal and response;
- explain acute stress as a coordinated biological, cognitive, emotional, and behavioral process;
- identify sympathetic/autonomic and endocrine contributions at an introductory level;
- distinguish measured physiology from inferred emotional meaning;
- analyze a simple stress-response dataset.

## Psychology Word of the Day
**stress** — a psychophysiological response to perceived demands or challenges.

## Vocabulary in Context
stressor · appraisal · acute stress · sympathetic activation · parasympathetic recovery · endocrine response · cortisol · physiological measure · baseline · recovery

## Core Reading
Stress is not merely a feeling and is not automatically harmful. In psychological science, stress involves coordinated responses to demands. A stressor is the event or condition; appraisal concerns how demands and resources are interpreted; the response may include changes in attention, emotion, heart rate, muscle tension, hormones, and behavior.

Students examine why the same event can produce different responses across people and across occasions. The lesson introduces autonomic activation and endocrine signaling without teaching a one-hormone/one-emotion model.

## Worked Example
A student gives a public presentation. Heart rate increases from baseline and returns toward baseline afterward.

Appropriate conclusion: the body showed physiological activation during the task.

Too-strong conclusion: the student was definitely terrified.

The measure does not by itself reveal the exact subjective experience.

## Psychological Scientist's Desk
**Claim:** “High heart rate proves someone is anxious.”

Audit:
- What was measured?
- What construct is being inferred?
- What alternative causes of elevated heart rate exist?
- What additional evidence would strengthen interpretation?

## Data Lens
Students graph fictional heart-rate data across baseline, challenge, and recovery for three participants. They compare peak response and recovery rather than ranking who is “most stressed.”

## Claims Laboratory
Evaluate:
- “Stress is always bad.”
- “People under the same stressor should respond the same way.”
- “A physiological response reveals exactly what someone is feeling.”

## Guided Investigation
### Demand → Appraisal → Response Map
Using fictional scenarios, students separate:
1. observable stressor;
2. possible appraisal;
3. physiological response;
4. cognitive response;
5. behavioral response;
6. evidence actually available.

## Independent Assignment
Write a 350–500 word scientific explanation of one fictional stress response. Include at least two plausible interpretations and one measurement limitation.

## Research Notebook Checkpoint
If stress is relevant to the semester question, define:
- the stressor;
- the proposed appraisal;
- the response measure;
- one alternative explanation.

If stress is not relevant, justify exclusion.

## Academic Journal Reflection
What is the difference between observing a stress response and explaining why it occurred? Use a fictional example rather than private disclosure.

## Daily Review — 5 Questions
1. Distinguish stress from stressor.
2. What is appraisal?
3. Why is heart rate not a direct measure of fear?
4. What is the purpose of a baseline?
5. Name one reason two people may respond differently to the same demand.
