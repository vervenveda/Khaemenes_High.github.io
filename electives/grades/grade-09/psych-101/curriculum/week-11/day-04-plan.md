# Week 11 · Day 4 — Health Behavior, Habits & Environmental Opportunity

**Essential Question:** Why is knowing what is healthy not always enough to change behavior?

## Learning Objectives
Students will be able to:
- define health behavior without moralizing it;
- connect learning, motivation, habit, self-efficacy, and social context to health-related choices;
- distinguish intention from opportunity and actual behavior;
- identify environmental barriers and supports;
- design a hypothetical behavior-change study without experimenting on classmates;
- interpret pre/post behavior data cautiously.

## Psychology Word of the Day
**health behavior** — behavior that can influence health, risk, prevention, functioning, or recovery.

## Vocabulary in Context
habit · cue · reinforcement · intention · self-efficacy · adherence · barrier · opportunity · implementation intention · behavior change

## Core Reading
Health behavior sits at the intersection of many earlier units. Learning histories shape habits; motivation influences effort; self-efficacy influences persistence; social norms affect choices; memory affects adherence; and environments determine what options are actually available.

Students examine why knowledge alone often fails to predict behavior. A person can understand a recommendation while facing cost, time, transportation, schedule, family, safety, fatigue, cultural, disability, or access barriers. Psychological explanations should therefore avoid blame and simplistic willpower models.

## Worked Example
Two fictional students intend to be physically active after school. One has a safe nearby space and flexible transportation; the other has caregiving responsibilities and no safe route.

Different behavior does not establish different motivation.

## Psychological Scientist's Desk
**Claim:** “If people cared about their health, they would follow health advice.”

Students identify hidden assumptions and propose measurements that distinguish knowledge, intention, opportunity, and behavior.

## Data Lens
Students examine fictional pre/post data from an intervention that changes the environment rather than only giving information. They calculate absolute change and compare groups while identifying regression-to-the-mean and self-selection as possible limitations.

## Claims Laboratory
Evaluate:
- “Knowledge automatically changes behavior.”
- “Willpower explains whether people follow health recommendations.”
- “A habit is just a bad choice repeated.”
- “If an intervention works on average, everyone benefits.”

## Guided Investigation
### Behavior × Opportunity Map
For four supplied cases, students chart:
**Goal → Cue → Behavior → Consequence → Barrier → Support → Measure**

They then propose one environmental change and one individual strategy without claiming either will work universally.

## Independent Assignment
Design a hypothetical low-risk study evaluating one neutral health-support behavior such as taking scheduled screen breaks or keeping a regular study-stop time. Use fictional participants/data only. Include IV, DV, comparison, ethics, and one environmental barrier.

## Research Notebook Checkpoint
Audit whether the semester question confuses:
- intention with behavior;
- behavior with motivation;
- access with preference;
- correlation with causal effect.

## Academic Journal Reflection
Why can two people with the same goal show different behavior without one being “more disciplined”?

## Daily Review — 5 Questions
1. Define health behavior.
2. Why is intention different from behavior?
3. Give one environmental barrier that can affect behavior.
4. How can self-efficacy influence persistence?
5. Why should intervention averages not be treated as individual guarantees?
