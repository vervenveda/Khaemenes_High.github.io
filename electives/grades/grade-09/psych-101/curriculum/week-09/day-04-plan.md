# Week 09 · Day 04
## Measuring Personality: Reliability, Validity & Self-Report Limits

**Week Unit:** Personality & Individual Differences

**Essential Question:** How can psychologists measure personality without confusing a questionnaire score with the whole person?

## Learning Objectives
Students can:
- compare self-report, informant report, behavioral observation, and repeated measures;
- apply reliability and validity to personality assessment;
- identify social desirability, wording, memory, and reference-group effects as possible sources of bias;
- explain why agreement across methods can strengthen confidence;
- distinguish test usefulness from overclaiming.

## Vocabulary in Context
**self-report · informant report · behavioral observation · repeated measure · reliability · validity · social desirability · response bias · reference-group effect · converging evidence**

## Core Reading
Personality cannot be observed directly in the way height can. Researchers infer personality patterns from measures. A self-report asks people to describe themselves. An informant report asks someone who knows the person to describe recurring patterns. Behavioral observations record what a person actually does under specified conditions. Repeated measures examine behavior or reports across time.

Each method has strengths and limitations. Self-report provides access to private beliefs and experiences but can be influenced by memory, self-presentation, interpretation of wording, or comparison with one's usual social group. Informants may observe patterns the participant misses but have their own limited perspective. Behavioral measures can be concrete but may capture only one narrow situation.

A strong measurement argument therefore asks whether the instrument is **reliable**, whether evidence supports the intended **validity**, and whether multiple methods converge.

## Worked Example
A new “leadership personality test” gives nearly identical scores when repeated two weeks later but has no relationship to observed leadership behavior or relevant outcomes.

Conclusion: the test may show reliability, but that does not establish validity for the claimed use.

## Psychological Scientist's Desk
### Claim
“If a personality test is consistent, it must be accurate.”

Students use the Week 6 distinction:

**Reliability ≠ validity.**

Consistency is necessary for many uses but does not by itself prove that the intended construct is being measured.

## Data Lens
Fictional method comparison:

| Participant | Self-report trait score | Informant score | Observed-behavior index |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| A | 78 | 74 | 72 |
| B | 61 | 58 | 63 |
| C | 84 | 62 | 59 |
| D | 49 | 53 | 51 |

Students identify:
- where methods converge;
- which participant shows the largest discrepancy;
- several possible explanations for disagreement;
- why disagreement does not automatically tell us which method is “wrong.”

## Claims Laboratory
**Claim:** “Self-report tells us exactly what a person is like.”

Judgment: **Too strong.**

Students identify wording effects, social desirability, memory limitations, self-awareness, cultural reference points, and situational specificity.

## Guided Investigation
### Measurement Audit
Students compare four fictional personality measures. They rate each for:
- operational clarity;
- reliability evidence;
- validity evidence;
- susceptibility to response bias;
- appropriateness for the stated population;
- ethical risk if used for high-stakes decisions.

## Independent Assignment
### Build a Better Measure
Students design a short, fictional research plan for measuring one non-clinical trait dimension using at least two methods. They must explain:
1. what the construct means;
2. how each method operationalizes it;
3. one reliability question;
4. one validity question;
5. one likely bias;
6. why converging evidence would help.

## Ethical Boundary
Students may not use the assignment to rank or label classmates, family members, or themselves. All assessed measurement work uses fictional participants or provided datasets.

Choose the best measurement strategy for any proposed individual-difference variable in the semester question. Record one response-bias risk and one validity question that would need evidence.
