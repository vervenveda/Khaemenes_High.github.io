# Week 09 · Day 03
## Person × Situation: Why Behavior Changes Across Contexts

**Week Unit:** Personality & Individual Differences

**Essential Question:** How can a person show stable tendencies while behaving differently in different situations?

## Learning Objectives
Students can:
- explain person–situation interaction;
- distinguish trait consistency from identical behavior;
- identify situational affordances, norms, roles, and incentives that can shape behavior;
- interpret repeated-measure evidence across contexts;
- avoid treating situational variability as proof of dishonesty or inconsistency.

## Vocabulary in Context
**person–situation interaction · context · role · norm · affordance · situational strength · within-person variability · between-person difference · consistency**

## Core Reading
Personality research does not require people to behave the same way everywhere. A person may be talkative with close friends, quiet in a formal interview, highly assertive during a debate, and reserved in a new group. The behavior changes because the situation changes, while some underlying tendencies may still be visible across many observations.

The person–situation approach asks how individual tendencies and environmental conditions combine. Some situations are highly structured: rules and expectations narrow the range of acceptable behavior. Other situations allow more freedom for individual differences to appear.

This produces an important scientific distinction:

> **Stable tendency does not mean fixed response.**

## Worked Example
A fictional participant scores relatively high on extraversion but speaks less during a solemn ceremony than at a party.

Weak interpretation: “The test was wrong.”

Stronger interpretation: the trait describes a broad tendency while situational norms strongly constrain behavior in one setting.

## Psychological Scientist's Desk
### Claim
“If someone acts differently in two situations, one version must be fake.”

Students identify alternative explanations involving role expectations, perceived safety, goals, audience, incentives, cultural norms, or familiarity.

## Data Lens
Fictional speaking-frequency data:

| Participant | Close Friends | Class Discussion | Formal Interview |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| A | 18 | 11 | 5 |
| B | 12 | 8 | 4 |
| C | 7 | 5 | 2 |
| D | 15 | 10 | 6 |

Students examine:
- whether relative ordering is partly stable;
- how every participant changes across context;
- within-person versus between-person variation;
- why one setting should not define the whole person.

## Claims Laboratory
**Claim:** “A trait score predicts exactly how someone will behave.”

Judgment: **Too strong.**

A trait score may improve probabilistic prediction across repeated observations, but behavior also depends on context, goals, learning, opportunity, and measurement conditions.

## Guided Investigation
### Same Person, Different Setting
Students analyze five fictional cases. For each they identify:
1. possible trait tendency;
2. situational demand;
3. observed behavior;
4. one alternative explanation;
5. what repeated evidence would be needed before making a stable-trait claim.

## Independent Assignment
### Person × Situation Matrix
Students choose one fictional profile and map expected behavioral possibilities across four settings. They must write probabilities or cautious predictions rather than certainties and explain which situational variables matter.
