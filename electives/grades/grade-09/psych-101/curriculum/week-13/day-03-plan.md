# Week 13 · Day 3 — Human Factors, Technology, Persuasion & Ethical Design

**Essential Question:** How can psychology improve design without becoming manipulation?

## Learning Objectives
Students will be able to:
- define human factors and explain how perception, attention, memory, and workload affect design;
- identify cognitive load, error likelihood, defaults, salience, and feedback in interfaces;
- distinguish persuasion from coercion and transparent influence from deceptive design;
- identify privacy and autonomy concerns in behavior-shaping technologies;
- evaluate design claims without assuming user behavior equals free preference.

## Psychology Word of the Day
**human factors** — the application of psychological and behavioral science to design systems, tools, and environments that fit human abilities and limitations.

## Vocabulary in Context
human factors · cognitive load · affordance · feedback · default · salience · persuasive design · dark pattern · autonomy · privacy · accessibility · error prevention

## Core Reading
Human factors begins with a simple idea: systems should be designed around real human attention, memory, perception, movement, and error patterns rather than assuming perfect users. Good design can reduce mistakes, clarify choices, and improve accessibility.

The same psychological knowledge can also be used to influence behavior. Students examine defaults, notifications, friction, social proof, and salience. Influence is not automatically unethical, but ethical design asks who benefits, whether the user understands the choice, whether privacy is protected, whether opting out is realistic, and whether the system exploits known vulnerabilities.

## Worked Example
A website makes “Accept” large and bright while hiding “Decline” behind several screens.

Students analyze:
- salience;
- friction;
- default pressure;
- user autonomy;
- whether consent is meaningfully informed.

## Psychological Scientist's Desk
**Claim:** “If users click the button, they chose freely.”

Students identify how design context can shape probability without eliminating agency.

## Data Lens
Students compare task-completion time and error rate across two fictional interface designs. They distinguish speed from accuracy and discuss accessibility as a separate outcome rather than assuming one best metric.

## Claims Laboratory
Evaluate:
- “Good design makes people do the right thing.”
- “A default is neutral because users can change it.”
- “More notifications increase engagement, so they improve the product.”
- “If behavior changes, manipulation occurred.”
- “Privacy concerns disappear if data are useful.”

## Guided Investigation
### Ethical Interface Audit
Students inspect a fictional app screen and identify:
1. intended behavior;
2. psychological principle used;
3. user benefit;
4. designer/platform benefit;
5. transparency level;
6. privacy cost;
7. accessibility issue;
8. alternative design preserving more autonomy.

## Independent Assignment
Redesign one fictional school or study interface using human-factors principles. Explain how the redesign reduces cognitive load or error while preserving autonomy and accessibility.

## Research Notebook Checkpoint
If the semester application involves technology or persuasion, add:
- target behavior;
- mechanism of influence;
- transparency requirement;
- privacy risk;
- accessibility concern;
- one design alternative with less coercive pressure.
