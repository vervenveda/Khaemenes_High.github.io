4 · Psychology Word of the Day

neurotransmitter

Formal definition

A chemical messenger released by neurons that crosses a synapse and influences receptor-bearing cells.

Student-friendly definition

One kind of chemical signal neurons use to communicate across the tiny gap between cells.

Related: synapse · receptor · neuron · signaling · reuptake

5 · Vocabulary in Context

Parts of neural communication

Dendrite / cell body

Dendrites receive many signals; the cell body contains the nucleus and integrates cellular activity.

Axon / myelin

The axon carries electrical signaling away from the cell body; myelin can support faster and more efficient conduction.

Action potential

A rapid electrical event that travels along an axon once threshold conditions are met.

Synapse

The junction where one neuron communicates with another cell.

Receptor

A protein structure that responds to particular chemical signals.

Neural communication

An electrochemical process involving electrical signaling within neurons and chemical signaling between many neurons.

6 · Core Reading

Neural communication is a system, not a chemical slogan

The nervous system depends on enormous networks of specialized cells. Neurons are one major cell type involved in receiving, integrating, and transmitting information. Their shapes vary, but a simplified neuron often includes dendrites, a cell body, an axon, and terminal regions that communicate with other cells.

Within the neuron, changes in electrical charge can produce an action potential. Action potentials are not miniature thoughts. They are electrical signaling events. Their meaning depends on which cells are active, how often they fire, which networks they belong to, what inputs they receive, and what other processes are occurring at the same time.

At many synapses, an arriving electrical signal triggers release of neurotransmitters. These molecules cross a microscopic gap and bind to receptors. The effect can increase or decrease the likelihood that another cell will respond, depending on the transmitter, receptor type, cell, and circuit. Neurotransmitters can then be broken down, diffuse away, or be taken back up.

This complexity matters because popular explanations often turn neurotransmitters into personality labels: “dopamine is the happiness chemical,” “serotonin is confidence,” or “adrenaline is fear.” Such slogans are scientifically weak. The same transmitter can act in multiple pathways and participate in many functions. Psychological outcomes emerge from patterns across systems, not from a single chemical floating alone.

Neural systems also interact with learning, memory, perception, hormones, social context, and behavior. A biological explanation is therefore not automatically a complete explanation. It becomes useful when it specifies a mechanism that can be measured and linked to evidence.

Interpretation boundary: Students should never infer an invisible neurotransmitter state from someone's mood, behavior, facial expression, or social media post.
7 · Worked Example

From signal to synapse

  1. Inputs influence a neuron.
  2. If threshold conditions are reached, an action potential travels along the axon.
  3. At the terminal, chemical messengers may be released.
  4. Neurotransmitters cross the synaptic gap.
  5. They bind to receptors on another cell.
  6. The receiving cell's probability of responding changes.
The sequence describes communication. It does not tell us the psychological meaning of the signal without knowing the larger network and task.
8 · Psychological Scientist's Desk

Claim audit

Claim: “Dopamine is the happiness chemical.”
  1. Claim: Is “happiness” operationally defined?
  2. Measure: What dopamine-related process is actually measured?
  3. Evidence: Which pathways, tasks, and outcomes are involved?
  4. Alternative: Reward learning, motivation, movement, expectation, and context may be relevant.
  5. Limitation: One transmitter participates in many systems.
  6. Conclusion: Avoid one-chemical-one-emotion language.

10 · Claims Laboratory

“More neurotransmitter is always better.”

Verdict

Unsupported. Effects depend on transmitter, receptor, location, timing, dose, network state, and many interacting processes.

Better statement

Neurochemical signaling is regulated, context-dependent, and part of larger systems.
