4 · Psychology Word of the Day

neuroplasticity

Formal definition

The nervous system's capacity to change its structure, function, or connectivity in response to development, experience, learning, injury, and other conditions.

Student-friendly definition

The brain and nervous system can change with experience—but those changes are constrained by biology, development, timing, and context.

Related: learning · adaptation · recovery · connectivity · experience-dependent change

5 · Vocabulary in Context

Ways to study brain and behavior

EEG

Records electrical activity from the scalp with high temporal resolution but limited precision about exact deep sources.

fMRI

Measures blood-oxygen-related changes associated with neural activity, offering useful spatial information but not direct recordings of thoughts.

Lesion evidence

Examines how damage to brain tissue relates to changes in function; valuable for causal reasoning but complicated by injury variability and reorganization.

Behavioral measure

Performance such as accuracy, reaction time, choices, recall, or observed action.

Comparison condition

A condition used to determine what changes relative to a meaningful baseline or alternative.

Converging evidence

Confidence strengthened by compatible results across methods, measures, samples, and studies.

6 · Core Reading

Every brain method answers some questions better than others

Scientists cannot understand the brain by relying on a single measurement. Each method provides a different window. EEG records rapid changes in electrical activity from electrodes on the scalp, making it useful for questions about timing. However, locating the exact neural sources of the signal can be difficult.

fMRI detects changes in blood oxygenation related to neural activity. It provides much finer spatial information than scalp EEG, but the signal unfolds more slowly than the electrical events that ultimately generate it. A colorful fMRI image is therefore not a photograph of a thought. It is a statistical representation of measured signal differences across conditions.

Lesion and injury evidence can reveal what happens when parts of a system are damaged. If a specific ability is reliably impaired following damage to a region or network, that evidence may suggest the system is necessary for normal performance. Yet injuries are rarely perfectly isolated, and the brain may reorganize over time.

Behavioral measures remain essential. A brain difference matters psychologically only when researchers connect it to clearly defined behavior, cognition, or experience. Reaction time, recall, accuracy, choices, and self-report can help test what neural differences mean.

Neuroplasticity describes the capacity of neural systems to change. Learning can alter synaptic strength and network organization. Development changes neural systems dramatically. Recovery after injury may involve reorganization. But plasticity is not unlimited, instant, or guaranteed. “The brain can change” does not mean any person can become anything under any condition.

Strong neuroscience therefore uses converging evidence. If behavioral performance, EEG timing, fMRI location, lesion evidence, and replicated experiments point toward a compatible explanation, confidence grows. If methods disagree, the disagreement becomes a research question.

Interpretation boundary: neuroplasticity should never be used to blame people for not “rewiring” themselves, nor should brain scans be treated as diagnostic mind readers in this course.

8 · Psychological Scientist's Desk

Scan claim audit

Claim: “Brain scans can read people's thoughts.”
  1. What signal does the method actually measure?
  2. Was the task constrained and known in advance?
  3. Are researchers classifying patterns across groups or decoding arbitrary thoughts?
  4. How accurate is the model outside the original sample?
  5. What privacy and consent concerns arise?

10 · Claims Laboratory

“Neuroplasticity means the brain can change without limit.”

Verdict

Unsupported. Plasticity is real, but constrained by biological systems, development, experience, injury, timing, and task demands.

Better statement

Neural systems can change in response to experience and conditions, with limits that vary by system and context.
