Coping, Control & Social Support

What makes a coping strategy useful in one context but less useful in another?

2 · Essential Question

Essential Question

What makes a coping strategy useful in one context but less useful in another?

3 · Learning Objectives

Learning Objectives

Students will be able to:

  • define coping as efforts to manage demands rather than eliminate all distress;
  • compare problem-focused and emotion-focused coping without ranking one as universally superior;
  • explain why perceived control and actual control should be distinguished;
  • describe emotional, informational, and practical social support;
  • evaluate coping claims using fit, outcome measures, context, and evidence.

Use it in context: Explain how problem-focused coping connects to today’s evidence, measure, or explanation. Do not treat knowing the word as the same thing as understanding the construct.
5 · Vocabulary in Context

Vocabulary in Context

6 · Core Reading

Substantial Core Reading

Coping is not a contest in toughness. A strategy may work well when a stressor can be changed and poorly when the relevant conditions cannot be changed. Problem-focused strategies target the demand or its consequences; emotion-focused strategies target emotional response or regulation. Many real situations involve both.

Perceived control can influence appraisal, but psychology must also respect real constraints. Telling someone to “take control” is not evidence-based reasoning when money, safety, policy, transportation, disability, family obligations, or other conditions limit options.

7 · Worked Example

Worked Example / Synthesis

A fictional student has two upcoming deadlines and one unavoidable medical appointment.

For the deadlines, planning and task breakdown may target controllable demands. For the appointment, preparation, information, breathing, or social support may target the experience rather than remove the event.

The lesson emphasizes strategy–situation fit.

8 · Scientist's Desk

Psychological Scientist's Desk

Claim: “Problem-focused coping is healthier than emotion-focused coping.”

Students identify why this is too broad and specify what evidence would be required to compare strategies fairly.

9 · Data Lens

Data Lens

A fictional study compares coping strategies across controllable and uncontrollable scenarios. Students interpret an interaction pattern: the same strategy does not produce identical outcomes across contexts.

10 · Claims Laboratory

Claims Laboratory

Evaluate:

  • “Good copers do not feel stressed.”
  • “Asking for support means someone cannot cope independently.”
  • “More perceived control is always better.”
  • “One coping strategy should work for everyone.”

11A · Extended Inquiry

Extended Inquiry from the Locked Plan

Psychology Word of the Day

coping — cognitive and behavioral efforts used to manage demands and their effects.

Guided Investigation

Apply Coping, Control & Social Support to a fictional or de-identified case. Separate observation from interpretation, identify the psychological concept that best explains the pattern, compare at least two possible explanations, and state what additional evidence would be needed before favoring one explanation. Record one ethical boundary that limits what can responsibly be inferred.

Strategy–Situation Match

Students receive six fictional scenarios and choose among several possible coping strategies. For each choice they must explain:

  1. what is controllable;
  2. what is not controllable;
  3. what outcome is being targeted;
  4. what evidence would show the strategy helped;
  5. one possible unintended consequence.

Independent Assignment
