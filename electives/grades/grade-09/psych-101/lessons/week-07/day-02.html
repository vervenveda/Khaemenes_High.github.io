Week 07 · Emotion & Motivation

Appraisal, Expression & Emotion Regulation

Why can the same event produce different emotional responses in different people or at different times?

Retrieval Warm-Up

2 · Essential Question

Essential Question

Why can the same event produce different emotional responses in different people or at different times?

3 · Learning Objectives

Learning Objectives

Students will be able to:

  • define appraisal as interpretation of an event in relation to goals, expectations, and context;
  • explain why similar physiological arousal can be interpreted differently;
  • distinguish emotional expression from the entire emotional experience;
  • describe several emotion-regulation strategies without labeling one strategy as universally best;
  • analyze how culture and social context can influence emotional expression and interpretation.

Vocabulary in Context

6 · Core Reading

Substantial Core Reading

People do not respond only to events themselves; they also respond to what those events mean to them. Appraisal can involve questions such as:

  • Is this relevant to my goals?
  • Is it threatening, helpful, fair, controllable, or unexpected?
  • What do I think will happen next?

Two people can encounter the same event and form different appraisals. The difference does not prove that one is irrational or that one emotional response is false.

Emotion regulation refers to processes that influence which emotions occur, when they occur, and how they are experienced or expressed. Strategies can include changing a situation, shifting attention, reappraising meaning, problem solving, seeking support, or modifying expression. Their usefulness depends on context, timing, goals, and consequences.

7 · Worked Example

Worked Example / Synthesis

Two students receive the same difficult assignment.

Student A appraises it as: “This is a challenge I can learn from.” Student B appraises it as: “This proves I am going to fail.”

The assignment is the same, but the interpretation differs. That appraisal may influence emotion, motivation, and later choices.

8 · Scientist's Desk

Psychological Scientist's Desk

Claim: “If two people feel differently about the same event, one of them must be wrong.”

Students identify:

  • the observable event;
  • possible appraisals;
  • evidence needed about emotional experience;
  • why differing appraisals can coexist.
9 · Data Lens

Data Lens

Fictional experiment:

| Instruction before difficult task | Mean reported tension (1–10) | Persistence (minutes) |
|---|---:|---:|
| “Treat this as a threat to your score” | 7.8 | 6.4 |
| “Treat this as a practice challenge” | 5.2 | 9.1 |

Students identify the manipulated variable, two dependent variables, and a cautious conclusion. They must also name at least one limitation.

10 · Claims Laboratory

Claims Laboratory

Claim: “You can tell exactly what someone feels from their face.”

Target judgment: Too strong. Facial behavior can provide useful information, but expression varies across individuals and contexts, can be deliberately altered, and should not be treated as mind-reading.

11 · Guided Investigation

Guided Investigation

Students analyze four fictional events using: Event → Possible Appraisal A → Emotion A → Likely Choice A and Event → Possible Appraisal B → Emotion B → Likely Choice B.

They then identify what evidence would distinguish the two interpretations.

12 · Independent Assignment

Independent Assignment

Same Event, Different Meaning. Write a 350–500 word analysis showing how context, expectation, appraisal, and regulation could produce different responses to the same fictional event.

13 · Research Notebook

Research Notebook Checkpoint

If emotion or motivation is relevant to the semester question, ask:

  • Could appraisal mediate the relationship between the situation and behavior?
  • How could appraisal be measured?
  • Could wording of the measure create its own framing effect?

Academic Journal Reflection

Privacy boundary: The journal is academic, not therapeutic. Fictional, public, non-sensitive, or course-supplied examples are always acceptable.
