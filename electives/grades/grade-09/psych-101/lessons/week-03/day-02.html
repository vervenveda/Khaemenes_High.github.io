Vision, Hearing & the Other Senses

How do different sensory systems extract different kinds of information from the same world?

2 · Essential Question

How do sensory systems specialize?

Different sensory systems transduce different forms of physical information, and perception often combines them.

4 · Psychology Word of the Day

proprioception

Formal definition

The sense of the position and movement of body parts, based on information from muscles, tendons, joints, and related systems.

Student-friendly definition

Your body's “where are my limbs?” sense—even when you are not looking at them.

Related: vestibular sense · body position · movement · balance · touch

5 · Vocabulary in Context

Sensory systems

Rod / cone

Retinal photoreceptors. Rods are especially sensitive in dim light; cones support color vision and high-acuity vision in brighter conditions.

Frequency / pitch

Sound-wave frequency is related to perceived pitch.

Amplitude / loudness

Sound-wave amplitude contributes to perceived loudness, though perception is not a simple one-to-one copy.

Touch

Multiple receptor systems contribute to pressure, vibration, temperature, pain, and body-surface information.

Taste / smell

Chemical senses that interact strongly in flavor perception.

Vestibular sense

Provides information about head movement, acceleration, and balance, strongly involving the inner ear.

6 · Core Reading

Each sensory system answers a different physical question

Vision begins when light enters the eye and reaches photoreceptors in the retina. Rods are highly sensitive under dim conditions and support low-light vision. Cones support fine detail and color vision, especially in brighter light. Color perception depends on patterns across cone types and later neural processing; it is not simply “red cells, green cells, and blue cells” operating independently.

Hearing begins when sound waves change pressure in the air. The outer and middle ear transmit that energy to the cochlea, where mechanical movement is transduced into neural signals. Frequency is strongly related to perceived pitch, while amplitude contributes to loudness. Auditory perception also depends on timing, context, and neural processing.

Touch is not a single receptor. Different receptors contribute information about pressure, vibration, stretch, temperature, and tissue damage. Pain is especially complex because sensory signals interact with attention, expectation, context, learning, and descending neural processes. This does not mean pain is “imaginary”; it means perception is a biological interpretation of signals.

Taste and smell are chemical senses. Taste receptors contribute information about basic taste qualities, while smell contributes enormously to the experience of flavor. Texture, temperature, vision, expectation, and prior learning also shape flavor. That is why the statement “taste happens entirely on the tongue” is incomplete.

The vestibular system provides information about head movement and balance. Proprioception provides information about the position and movement of limbs and joints. Together with vision and touch, these systems help the brain estimate where the body is and how it is moving.

Perception often combines information across senses. Seeing a speaker's mouth can influence what syllable a listener thinks they heard. Smell changes flavor. Vision can affect balance. These are examples of cross-modal perception—the senses interact rather than functioning as sealed channels.

7 · Worked Example

Flavor is multisensory

A person identifies a fruit-flavored candy correctly 90% of the time with smell available but only 55% when airflow through the nose is blocked.

Supported: smell contributed to identification under these conditions.

Not supported: the tongue contributes nothing to flavor.

8 · Psychological Scientist's Desk

Claim audit

Claim: “Taste happens entirely on the tongue.”
  1. Define taste versus flavor.
  2. Measure identification with and without smell cues.
  3. Consider texture, temperature, and visual information.
  4. Write a conclusion that distinguishes receptor input from the full perceptual experience.

10 · Claims Laboratory

“Humans have exactly five senses.”

Verdict

Unsupported as a complete scientific description. Vision, hearing, taste, smell, and touch are useful familiar categories, but vestibular, proprioceptive, and internal sensory systems also provide information.

Better statement

Humans have multiple sensory systems specialized for different forms of information.
