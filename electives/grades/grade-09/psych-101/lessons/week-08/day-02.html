Week 08 · Human Development

Early Development: Biology, Relationships & Experience

How do biology and early experience interact during development?

2 · Essential Question

Essential Question

How do biology and early experience interact during development?

3 · Learning Objectives

Learning Objectives

Students will be able to:

  • describe development as an interaction among biology, experience, relationships, and environment;
  • explain why prenatal influences and early environments matter without treating them as destiny;
  • distinguish temperament from personality diagnosis;
  • describe attachment as a relationship construct studied through patterns of interaction rather than a label for a whole person;
  • explain why developmental milestones are best understood as ranges with meaningful individual variation.

6 · Core Reading

Substantial Core Reading

Early development reflects ongoing interaction among biological maturation and environmental experience. Prenatal development can be influenced by genetic, nutritional, medical, and environmental factors, but developmental outcomes are rarely reducible to one factor.

After birth, infants and young children develop through changing sensory, motor, cognitive, emotional, and social systems. Temperament refers to early-appearing patterns of reactivity and self-regulation. Temperament is not a diagnosis and does not fix later personality.

Attachment research examines patterns in close caregiver-child relationships. The scientific lesson is not that one brief behavior permanently classifies a child. Relationship history, context, culture, caregiving conditions, and measurement all matter.

7 · Worked Example

Worked Example / Synthesis

Two toddlers begin walking at somewhat different ages.

A scientifically responsible interpretation asks:

  • Are both within a broad developmental range?
  • What opportunities for movement do they have?
  • Are there meaningful medical or environmental factors?

It does not conclude that the earlier walker is more intelligent or will be more successful later.

8 · Scientist's Desk

Psychological Scientist's Desk

Claim: “The first few years permanently determine who a person becomes.”

Students identify why the claim is too strong. Early experience can matter, but later relationships, learning, schooling, culture, health, opportunity, and continuing development also contribute.

9 · Data Lens

Data Lens

Fictional age-of-milestone data:

| Child | Age at first independent steps |
|---|---:|
| A | 10.5 months |
| B | 11.8 months |
| C | 13.2 months |
| D | 14.0 months |
| E | 15.1 months |

Students calculate a simple mean, identify the range, and explain why the average is not a deadline.

10 · Claims Laboratory

Claims Laboratory

Claim: “A child who reaches a milestone later than average is necessarily behind in every area.”

Target judgment: Unsupported. Development is multidimensional, individual timing varies, and interpretation requires appropriate developmental and clinical context.

11 · Guided Investigation

Guided Investigation

Students analyze fictional examples involving:

  • motor development;
  • early vocabulary;
  • emotional regulation;
  • social interaction.

For each they identify biological contributors, experience, relationships, environmental opportunity, and what evidence would be needed before making a causal claim.

12 · Independent Assignment

Independent Assignment

Development Is Interaction. Create a 400–550 word analysis of one fictional early-development case. Include biology, experience, relationships, individual variation, and one limitation of the available evidence.

13 · Research Notebook

Research Notebook Checkpoint

If early experience or developmental history is relevant to the semester question:

  • specify what developmental variable would be measured;
  • avoid vague statements such as “childhood caused it”;
  • identify at least one later-life factor that could also matter.
