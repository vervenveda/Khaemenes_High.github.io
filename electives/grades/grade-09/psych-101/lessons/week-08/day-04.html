Week 08 · Human Development

Adolescence, Identity & Social Development

How do biological change, relationships, culture, and growing autonomy interact during adolescence?

80% Daily ReviewLocal-firstAcademic, not therapeutic

Retrieval Warm-Up

Without notes, write one idea you already know that might connect to Adolescence, Identity & Social Development. Then write one question the lesson should help you answer.

2 · Essential Question

Essential Question

How do biological change, relationships, culture, and growing autonomy interact during adolescence?

3 · Learning Objectives

Learning Objectives

Students will be able to:

  • describe adolescence as a developmental period involving biological, cognitive, social, and cultural change;
  • explain puberty without reducing adolescent behavior to hormones;
  • describe identity exploration, autonomy, peer influence, and family relationships as developmental processes;
  • explain why adolescent risk-taking is context-dependent rather than universal;
  • reject simplistic claims that an “unfinished brain” makes adolescents incapable of reason.

5 · Vocabulary in Context

Vocabulary in Context

adolescence · puberty · identity · autonomy · peer influence · social context · risk perception · reward sensitivity · self-regulation · developmental variability

6 · Core Reading

Substantial Core Reading

Adolescence includes major physical change, expanding social worlds, increasing independence, and continued growth in cognitive and self-regulatory systems. Biological maturation matters, but adolescent behavior cannot be explained by hormones or brain development alone.

Identity development can involve exploration of interests, values, goals, roles, relationships, and future possibilities. This is not one test or one fixed stage completed at a single age.

Peer influence can increase or decrease risk depending on the peer group, situation, values, and behavior being modeled. Families, schools, communities, culture, opportunity, and prior learning remain important.

7 · Worked Example

Worked Example / Synthesis

A teenager makes a cautious decision in class but a riskier decision when surrounded by excited friends.

Possible contributors:

  • social reward;
  • peer norms;
  • arousal;
  • immediate incentives;
  • confidence;
  • task context.

The observation does not support the conclusion that “teenagers cannot reason.”

8 · Scientist's Desk

Psychological Scientist's Desk

Claim: “Teenagers make bad decisions because the frontal lobe is not finished developing.”

Students identify problems:

  • single-region explanation;
  • deterministic wording;
  • failure to define “bad decision”;
  • context and individual differences;
  • confusion between developmental average and individual capacity.
9 · Data Lens

Data Lens

Fictional experimental data:

| Condition | Risky option selected |
|---|---:|
| Alone | 29% |
| With neutral peers | 34% |
| With peers encouraging risk | 58% |
| With peers encouraging caution | 21% |

Students describe the effect of social context and explain why the data do not prove that all adolescents are highly peer-dependent.

10 · Claims Laboratory

Claims Laboratory

Claim: “Adolescents cannot make good decisions because their brains are unfinished.”

Target judgment: Unsupported as a universal conclusion. Brain systems continue developing, but adolescent decision-making varies by person, task, emotional state, knowledge, incentives, and social context.

11 · Guided Investigation

Guided Investigation

Students analyze fictional adolescent decisions involving:

  • school;
  • driving;
  • sports;
  • friendship;
  • online behavior.

For each they identify: Situation → Immediate Incentive → Social Context → Appraisal → Possible Choice → Evidence Needed.

12 · Independent Assignment

Independent Assignment

Adolescence Without Stereotypes. Write a 450–650 word analysis correcting three common claims about adolescence using developmental, social, and research-method reasoning.

13 · Research Notebook

Research Notebook Checkpoint

If the semester question involves adolescents:

  • identify which developmental process is actually relevant;
  • distinguish age from social context;
  • identify whether peer, family, school, or cultural variables should be measured;
  • state one claim that would be too deterministic.
14 · Academic Journal

Academic Journal Reflection

Why is “typical for an age group” different from “true of every person in that age group”?

No private identity or family disclosure is required.

Privacy boundary: The journal is academic, not therapeutic. Fictional, public, non-sensitive, or course-supplied examples are always acceptable.
