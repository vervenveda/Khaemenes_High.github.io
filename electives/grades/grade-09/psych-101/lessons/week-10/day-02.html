Essential Question

3 · Learning Objectives

Learning Objectives

Students can:

  • distinguish dispositional and situational explanations;
  • explain attribution as an inference rather than direct observation;
  • recognize the tendency to overemphasize personality when situational information is limited;
  • compare actor and observer information;
  • use evidence to resist premature character judgments.

6 · Core Reading

Substantial Core Reading

When people observe behavior, they often ask why it happened. An attribution is an explanation assigned to behavior. Some attributions focus on the person: traits, intentions, abilities, or motives. Others focus on the situation: rules, incentives, time pressure, social expectations, opportunity, or constraints.

Because observers often see behavior more clearly than they see the hidden situation, they can overestimate personal causes. Introductory psychology often calls this tendency the fundamental attribution error, but students should understand it as a probabilistic pattern rather than a law that affects everyone equally in every context.

Attribution is another place where Week 1's rule matters:

Observation is not explanation.
7 · Worked Example

Worked Example / Synthesis

Observation: a driver cuts quickly into another lane.

Dispositional inference: “That driver is reckless.”

Situational possibilities: avoiding debris, responding to another vehicle, unfamiliar road layout, or a genuine emergency.

The point is not that the situational explanation must be correct. The point is that one observed action does not establish character.

9 · Data Lens

Data Lens

Fictional study:

| Information given to observer | Mean personality blame rating |
|---|---:|
| Behavior only | 7.8 |
| Behavior + time-pressure context | 5.2 |
| Behavior + strong external constraint | 3.6 |

Students determine:

  • what changed across conditions;
  • whether context affected judgments;
  • what causal wording is justified if assignment to conditions was random;
  • why the result does not prove every observer ignores situations.

11A · Extended Inquiry

Extended Inquiry from the Locked Plan

Psychological Scientist's Desk

Build a testable claim connected to Attribution: Person, Situation & the Stories We Tell. Define one observable variable or behavior, state how it could be measured without diagnosing a real person, identify one plausible alternative explanation, and name the kind of evidence that would strengthen or weaken the claim. Finish with a calibrated conclusion that does not exceed the evidence.

Claims Laboratory

Claim: “If someone made a bad choice, personality caused it.”

Judgment: Cannot tell from the choice alone.

Students generate person, situation, learning, motivation, and information-based alternatives.

Guided Investigation

Apply Attribution: Person, Situation & the Stories We Tell to a fictional or de-identified case. Separate observation from interpretation, identify the psychological concept that best explains the pattern, compare at least two possible explanations, and state what additional evidence would be needed before favoring one explanation. Record one ethical boundary that limits what can responsibly be inferred.

Attribution Court

Students receive eight fictional behaviors with incomplete information. They must write:

  1. one dispositional explanation;
  2. one situational explanation;
  3. what evidence would distinguish them;
  4. which conclusion is currently justified.

Independent Assignment

Create an evidence brief on Attribution: Person, Situation & the Stories We Tell. Use one course concept, one concrete fictional example or dataset, and one alternative explanation. Explain how the evidence supports the concept, identify a limitation, and end with a conclusion written in probability-aware language rather than a label or diagnosis.

Observation → Inference → Evidence

For three scenarios, students build a three-column audit:

  • observable behavior;
  • inferred explanation;
  • evidence needed.

They then rewrite any moralizing or diagnostic language into testable psychological language.

Completion Rule

Completion requires core reading, Attribution Court, independent audit, notebook checkpoint, and review.
