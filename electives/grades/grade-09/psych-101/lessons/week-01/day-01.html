What Is Psychology?

What makes psychology a science rather than simply the study of interesting human behavior?

4 · Psychology Word of the Day

psychology

Formal definition

The scientific study of behavior and mental processes.

Student-friendly definition

A science that asks how people and other organisms behave, think, feel, learn, remember, perceive, develop, and interact—and tests those questions with evidence.

Related concepts: behavior · mental process · evidence · research method · measurement

5 · Vocabulary in Context

Language for today's science

behavior

An observable action or response that can be recorded or measured.

mental process

An internal psychological process such as thinking, remembering, perceiving, deciding, or feeling that must often be inferred from measures.

observation

A description of what was directly recorded, measured, heard, counted, or otherwise documented.

inference

An interpretation or explanation that goes beyond what was directly observed.

evidence

Information used to evaluate a claim. Evidence can differ greatly in quality.

basic research / applied research

Basic research builds understanding; applied research uses psychological science to address defined real-world problems. The two often inform one another.

6 · Core Reading

Psychology begins with questions—and disciplines the answers

Human behavior is fascinating, which is one reason psychology is easy to misunderstand. Everyone has experience with memory, emotion, learning, relationships, stress, motivation, and decision-making. That experience can produce useful questions, but personal experience alone cannot tell us which explanation is correct. Psychology becomes scientific when questions are connected to measurements, systematic evidence, alternative explanations, and conclusions that can be checked.

Psychology is the scientific study of behavior and mental processes. Behavior includes actions that can be observed or recorded: choosing an option, pressing a key, speaking, studying, helping, avoiding, sleeping, or solving a problem. Mental processes include activities such as perception, memory, attention, emotion, reasoning, and expectation. Because many mental processes cannot be observed directly, psychologists use measures that provide evidence about them. A reaction-time task may provide evidence about attention; a recall task may provide evidence about memory; a survey may provide evidence about a person's reported experience. The measure is useful, but it is not identical to the psychological construct itself.

This distinction is already important. Suppose a student answers only two questions during a class discussion. That is an observation if the questions were counted. Saying the student is “unmotivated” is an inference. Low participation could reflect motivation, but it could also reflect uncertainty, language demands, anxiety, classroom norms, prior knowledge, fatigue, hearing difficulty, a preference for writing, or many other factors. Good psychological science does not ban inference. It makes inference accountable to evidence.

Psychologists also distinguish basic research from applied research. Basic research may ask how attention works, how memory changes over time, or how people form impressions. Applied research may use those findings to improve classroom instructions, safety warnings, software interfaces, health communication, workplace systems, or public programs. Applied questions still require scientific discipline. A program that sounds helpful is not automatically effective; its outcomes must be measured.

Another major feature of psychology is that the same behavior can often have several plausible explanations. This is not a weakness. It is a reason to design stronger research. If two explanations predict different patterns of evidence, a study can help distinguish them. A strong psychological thinker therefore learns to ask not only, “What might explain this?” but also, “What would I expect to observe if my explanation were correct? What else could produce the same pattern?”

Psychology also operates at multiple levels. Biological systems can matter. Learning history can matter. Memory and expectations can matter. Development can matter. Personality differences can matter. Social relationships, culture, institutions, and environments can matter. A sophisticated explanation does not automatically include every level. Instead, it includes the levels justified by the question and evidence.

Throughout this course, conclusions will be written in proportion to evidence. If a study observes that two variables move together, the appropriate language may be “associated with,” not “caused by.” If an experiment changes one variable while controlling alternatives and finds a difference, stronger causal language may be justified under those conditions. If the sample is narrow, the conclusion should be narrow. If evidence conflicts, the conclusion should show uncertainty.

Course boundary: learning psychology does not qualify a student to diagnose classmates, relatives, public figures, or themselves from casual observations. Scientific curiosity must protect dignity and privacy.
7 · Worked Example

Observation is not explanation

Observation: During a 20-minute independent work period, Jordan looked away from the worksheet 14 times.

Possible inference A: Jordan was distracted.

Possible inference B: Jordan was searching the room for information or materials.

Possible inference C: Jordan was using brief visual breaks while thinking.

All three are possible. None is established by the observation alone.

Better research question: Under what task conditions does off-page looking increase, and how is it related to accuracy or response time?
8 · Psychological Scientist's Desk

Claim audit

Permanent routine: Claim → Measure → Evidence → Alternative Explanation → Limitation → Conclusion

Claim: “Students who doodle during a lesson are not paying attention.”

  1. Claim: What exactly does “not paying attention” mean?
  2. Measure: How would attention be measured—recall, eye gaze, reaction time, notes, performance, self-report?
  3. Evidence: What data would support the claim?
  4. Alternative explanation: Could doodling coexist with listening or help some students regulate attention?
  5. Limitation: Could the task, individual, classroom, or measurement method alter the result?
  6. Conclusion: What wording would match the evidence instead of the stereotype?
9 · Data Lens

What does the table actually show?

A researcher records how often four participants check a posted instruction sheet during a puzzle task.

ParticipantInstruction checksPuzzle accuracy
A170%
B490%
C785%
D275%

Supported observation: Participant C checked the instruction sheet most often.

Not supported from this table alone: C was the most anxious, B had the best memory, or checking instructions caused higher accuracy.

Data question: What additional design or evidence would be needed to test whether instruction checking causes a change in accuracy?
10 · Claims Laboratory

Classify the claim

Claim: “People who make eye contact are more confident.”

Possible verdict

Cannot Tell from the claim alone. Eye contact may vary with confidence, culture, social norms, neurodiversity, task, relationship, visual comfort, or many other factors.

Better question

How is eye-contact behavior related to a defined confidence measure in a specific setting and population?
