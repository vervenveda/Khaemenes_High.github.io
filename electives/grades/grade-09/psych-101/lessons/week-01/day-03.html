4 · Psychology Word of the Day

operational definition

Formal definition

A precise statement describing how a variable or construct will be measured or manipulated in a particular study.

Student-friendly definition

The exact rule that turns an idea like “memory,” “sleep,” or “cooperation” into something researchers can record.

Related: variable · measurement · reliability · validity · construct

5 · Vocabulary in Context

Research language

Hypothesis

A specific, testable prediction about variables or outcomes.

Theory

An organized explanatory framework that generates testable predictions; not a casual guess.

Variable

A characteristic or condition that can vary across people, situations, or time.

Population / sample

The population is the broader group of interest; the sample is the subset actually studied.

Reliability

Consistency or stability of a measurement under appropriate conditions.

Validity

The degree to which evidence supports the interpretation being made from a measure or study.

6 · Core Reading

Measurement is where psychological ideas meet evidence

Psychology studies concepts such as attention, memory, stress, motivation, intelligence, prejudice, and well-being. These concepts are useful, but they are not directly visible in the same way as height or temperature. Researchers therefore define procedures that provide evidence about them.

An operational definition specifies what will count as the variable in a particular study. “Memory” might be operationalized as the number of words recalled after ten minutes. “Attention” might be response accuracy in a visual-search task. “Sleep duration” might be hours estimated from a diary, measured by a wearable device, or recorded in a laboratory. Different operational definitions can produce different evidence because they capture different aspects of a construct.

Research methods answer different questions. Naturalistic observation describes behavior in settings with limited researcher intervention. Case studies provide rich information about a person, group, or event but usually do not establish broad generalizations. Surveys and interviews can efficiently collect reports but depend on wording, memory, willingness, and sampling. Correlational studies examine whether variables are associated. Experiments manipulate an independent variable, measure a dependent variable, and use controls—often including random assignment—to strengthen causal inference.

Sampling matters because a perfectly measured sample can still misrepresent the population. A survey of volunteers from one club should not automatically be generalized to all ninth graders. Representativeness depends on who had a chance to enter the sample, who participated, and whether relevant groups are missing.

Measurement quality also requires separating reliability and validity. A bathroom scale that is always five kilograms too high may be consistent but inaccurate. In psychology, a test can produce stable scores while still failing to support the interpretation researchers want to make. Reliability is important, but it does not guarantee validity.

Finally, method choice is not a hierarchy in which experiments are always “best.” A method is strong when it fits the question, ethics, and practical constraints. Some questions cannot be ethically manipulated. Some rare experiences require case studies. Some population questions require surveys. Strong psychology matches the method to the claim.

8 · Psychological Scientist's Desk

Claim audit

Claim: “Students who study with music learn less.”
  1. Define “music,” “study,” and “learn less.”
  2. Choose a method: observation, correlation, or experiment.
  3. Specify the sample and comparison condition.
  4. Identify possible confounds: task type, volume, preference, prior knowledge.
  5. Decide what result would support, weaken, or fail to answer the claim.

10 · Claims Laboratory

“A larger sample makes any study valid.”

Verdict

Unsupported. Larger samples can improve precision, but poor measurement, biased sampling, confounds, or misleading interpretation can remain.

Better statement

Sample size is one feature of study quality. Representativeness, measurement, design, ethics, and analysis also matter.
