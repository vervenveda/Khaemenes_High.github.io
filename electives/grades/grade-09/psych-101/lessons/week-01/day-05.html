4 · Psychology Word of the Day

replication

Formal definition

The process of repeating a study or closely related test to determine whether a finding can be observed again under specified conditions.

Student-friendly definition

Checking whether a result appears again instead of treating one study as the final word.

Related: reproducibility · converging evidence · peer scrutiny · uncertainty · revision

5 · Vocabulary in Context

Ethics and scientific checking

Informed consent

Participants receive relevant information needed to make a voluntary decision about participation.

Privacy / confidentiality

Privacy concerns access to personal information or spaces; confidentiality concerns protecting information that has been shared or collected.

Minimizing harm

Researchers reduce unnecessary physical, psychological, social, legal, or privacy risks.

Deception / debriefing

Deception may be used only under justified and approved conditions; debriefing explains withheld information and the study's purpose afterward.

Ethical review

Independent review evaluates whether procedures protect participants and whether risks are justified.

Converging evidence

Confidence grows when different studies, samples, measures, and methods point toward a compatible conclusion.

6 · Core Reading

Psychological science studies people, so ethics is part of the method

Psychology cannot separate scientific quality from ethical responsibility. A technically clever study can still be unacceptable if it violates consent, exposes private information, creates unnecessary risk, or uses people merely as means to an end. Ethical safeguards are therefore not decorations added after a study is designed; they shape what questions can be asked and how they can be investigated.

Informed consent means that people receive enough relevant information to decide voluntarily whether to participate. Consent is not meaningful if participation is coerced or if refusing carries an unfair penalty. Some research designs may use limited deception when revealing the full purpose beforehand would invalidate the study, but deception requires strong justification, ethical review, risk minimization, and appropriate debriefing.

Privacy and confidentiality are related but different. A researcher might violate privacy by collecting information from a setting where people reasonably expect not to be observed. A researcher might violate confidentiality by collecting information appropriately but later revealing a participant's identity or sensitive responses. Responsible data practices limit what is collected, who can access it, and how long it is retained.

Ethics also influences what students in this course may do. You will analyze fictional, public, academic, and provided datasets. You are not asked to secretly test classmates, manipulate family members, provoke distress, collect medical information, or create amateur diagnostic profiles. Learning research design does not require exposing real people to avoidable risk.

Scientific responsibility continues after data collection. A surprising result should not become a universal claim. Replication tests whether a finding can be observed again. Replications can be close repeats or conceptual tests using different procedures. A finding that appears across multiple independent studies, samples, and methods provides stronger evidence than a single isolated result.

Science also includes revision. A failed replication does not automatically prove that the original researchers were dishonest, and a successful replication does not prove that a theory is eternally true. Differences in samples, procedures, measurement quality, statistical power, or context may matter. The task is to investigate why evidence converges or diverges.

This is why the course uses an Evidence Ladder. Observation supports description. Association supports relational claims. Controlled experiments can strengthen causal claims under defined conditions. Converging evidence across methods supports broader confidence. Strong science is not certainty at all costs; it is disciplined updating.

Permanent course boundary: Psychology 101 teaches how evidence is generated and evaluated. It does not authorize students to diagnose, treat, manipulate, or secretly study other people.
7 · Worked Example

Useful question, unsafe design

Question: Does social exclusion change willingness to cooperate?

Unsafe classroom idea: Secretly arrange for real classmates to exclude one student, then observe cooperation.

Why unacceptable here: It intentionally manipulates a real student's social treatment, could create distress or reputational harm, and lacks meaningful consent.

Safer alternative: Analyze a fictional dataset, use published research examples, or present hypothetical scenarios and ask students to identify design features rather than manipulating peers.

8 · Psychological Scientist's Desk

Replication audit

Claim: “One famous experiment proved that people always obey authority.”
  1. Claim: “always obey” is universal and therefore extremely strong.
  2. Measure: What counted as obedience in the specific procedure?
  3. Evidence: What happened in the original sample and in later replications or variations?
  4. Alternative: Context, interpretation, experimenter behavior, culture, and procedure may influence outcomes.
  5. Limitation: One experimental setting cannot establish a universal human rule.
  6. Conclusion: Authority can influence behavior under some conditions; the magnitude and form depend on context.

10 · Claims Laboratory

“If a study was peer reviewed, its conclusion is proven.”

Verdict

Unsupported. Peer review is a quality-control process, not a guarantee of truth.

Better statement

Peer review can identify weaknesses before publication, while replication, critique, additional data, and converging evidence continue the scientific checking process.
